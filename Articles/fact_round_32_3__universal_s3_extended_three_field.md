# Three Fields, One Answer: The Single Bit Hidden Inside a Cubic Equation

## A message from the primes

Pick a cubic polynomial with integer coefficients — say
$$f(x) = x^3 - 3.$$
Now start walking through the prime numbers, and at each prime $p$ ask a very concrete question: *how does $f$ factor when its coefficients are read modulo $p$?*

There are only three possible answers. Either $f$ breaks into three distinct linear factors ("totally split"), or into a linear factor times an irreducible quadratic ("partially split", the $1+2$ pattern), or it stays irreducible ("inert"). So the primes, in order, emit a stream of letters from a three-letter alphabet:

$$\texttt{I}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{S}, \texttt{P}, \texttt{I}, \texttt{P}, \dots$$

This is a signal. It is generated by an object — a number field — that most people would not describe as a transmitter. But it is a signal, and once you call it a signal you can ask the question an engineer would ask: **how much information does it carry, and about what?**

The answer, for a large and natural family of cubics, turns out to be startlingly clean. Not "roughly one bit". Not "one bit asymptotically". Exactly, provably, algebraically **one bit** — and the same one bit for three completely different polynomials with three completely different arithmetic personalities.

## The receiver: a residue class

To measure information you need two random variables. The splitting type $T(p)$ is one. The other is the simplest observable a prime has: its residue class modulo a fixed integer $q$.

For $f(x) = x^3 - 3$, the relevant modulus turns out to be $q = 3$, so the second observable is just "$p \bmod 3$" — a single bit of information, since $p$ is either $1$ or $2$ modulo $3$.

We now have a communication channel. Input: the residue class of $p$. Output: the splitting type of $p$. The mutual information

$$I(p \bmod 3 \,;\, T) = H(p \bmod 3) + H(T) - H(p \bmod 3, T)$$

measures how many bits of the input survive the trip. And the theorem is:

> **The $S_3$ Type-Channel Law.** For a cubic whose splitting field has Galois group $S_3$, and for any residue observable whose associated quadratic character splits the residue classes into two equal halves, the mutual information between the residue class and the splitting type is exactly $1$ bit.

Exactly one. No logarithms of $3$, no transcendental leftovers, no error term. That the answer is an integer at all is a small miracle, because almost every other quantity in this setup is irrational: the entropy of the splitting type alone is $\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 3 \approx 1.4591$ bits, and the joint entropy is worse.

## Why the answer is a whole number

Here is the mechanism, and it is beautiful.

The splitting type of $p$ is a shadow of a group element. Attached to each unramified prime is a **Frobenius** permutation of the three roots of $f$, well defined up to conjugacy. Since the Galois group is the full symmetric group $S_3$ on the roots, there are six Frobenius elements and three conjugacy classes:

| Frobenius class | number of elements | factorization of $p$ | sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| identity | $1$ | totally split | $+1$ |
| transposition | $3$ | $1 + 2$ | $-1$ |
| three-cycle | $2$ | inert | $+1$ |

Chebotarev's density theorem says these classes occur with frequencies proportional to their sizes: $1 : 3 : 2$ out of $6$. So the splitting type is *random* — a $\left(\tfrac16, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\right)$ die.

But permutations have a **sign**: $+1$ for even, $-1$ for odd. Read the sign column of the table. The identity and the three-cycles are even; the transpositions are odd. And now look at the masses: the even side carries $1 + 2 = 3$ elements, the odd side carries $3$. **Perfectly balanced.** A fair coin has appeared inside the arithmetic, and it was not put there by hand.

The second ingredient is classical and exquisite: the sign of the Frobenius permutation is *not* mysterious. It is the quadratic character of the discriminant. Writing $\operatorname{disc} f$ for the discriminant of the cubic,

$$\operatorname{sgn}(\mathrm{Frob}_p) = \left(\frac{\operatorname{disc} f}{p}\right),$$

the Legendre symbol. The sign bit of an abstract Galois-theoretic object is computable from $p$ by a congruence condition.

So the channel factors. The residue class of $p$ determines one bit — the Legendre symbol — and *only* that bit reaches the splitting type; conditional on that bit, the splitting type is still random, and conditional on the type, the residue is still random within its half. The whole coupling passes through a single balanced binary bottleneck. One balanced bit in, one balanced bit out, everything else independent: mutual information exactly $1$.

The formal statement of this bottleneck argument is worth isolating, because it has nothing to do with number theory:

> **The Character Channel Law.** Let $X$ take values in a finite set $A$ and $Y$ in a finite set $B$, and suppose the joint occupation counts have the shape
> $$n(a,b) = \begin{cases} m(b) & \text{if } \chi(a) = g(b),\\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$
> for a two-valued $\chi$ on $A$ whose two fibres have equal size $k > 0$, and a two-valued $g$ on $B$ splitting the total weight $m$ into two halves of equal mass $M > 0$. Then $I(X;Y) = 1$ exactly — regardless of $A$, $B$, $k$, and the entire profile $m$.

The proof is two lines of bookkeeping once you see it. The $X$-marginal is uniform on $2k$ values, so $H(X) = \log_2(2k)$. The joint table is the $Y$-distribution replicated $k$ times, so $H(X,Y) = H(Y) + \log_2 k$. Subtract: $I = \log_2(2k) + H(Y) - H(Y) - \log_2 k = 1$. The messy irrational quantity $H(Y)$ cancels against itself, which is exactly why the answer is an integer.

## Three fields, one answer

A law that holds for one polynomial is a coincidence; a law that holds for a family is a law. So put three cubics on the bench, chosen so that their arithmetic is as different as we can make it.

To do that we need discriminants, and here a completely explicit identity does the work. If $r, s, t$ are the roots of a depressed cubic $x^3 + ax + b$ — so that $r+s+t=0$, $rs+st+tr=a$, $rst=-b$ — then the square of the Vandermonde product is

$$\big((r-s)(s-t)(t-r)\big)^2 = -4a^3 - 27b^2.$$

That single polynomial identity, provable by eliminating $t = -r-s$ and expanding, gives us all three discriminants at once:

- $x^3 - 3$: $a = 0$, $b = -3$, discriminant $-27 \cdot 9 = -243$;
- $x^3 - 2$: $a = 0$, $b = -2$, discriminant $-27 \cdot 4 = -108$;
- $x^3 - x - 1$: $a = b = -1$, discriminant $4 - 27 = -23$.

Three distinct discriminants. But the residue observable does not see the discriminant; it sees the discriminant *modulo squares*, because multiplying by a nonzero square never changes whether something is a square. And

$$-243 = -3 \cdot 9^2, \qquad -108 = -3 \cdot 6^2, \qquad -23 = -23 \cdot 1^2.$$

So the first two fields, apparently unrelated, share the same quadratic resolvent $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, while the third has the genuinely different resolvent $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-23})$.

For the first two, the coupling bit can be named completely explicitly, by the classical cube-root-of-unity argument: for any prime $p \neq 2, 3$,

$$-3 \text{ is a square modulo } p \iff p \equiv 1 \pmod 3.$$

(One direction: if $y^2 = -3$ then $\zeta = (y-1)/2$ satisfies $\zeta^2 + \zeta + 1 = 0$, an element of order $3$ in the multiplicative group, forcing $3 \mid p - 1$. The other direction runs backwards from an order-$3$ element supplied by Cauchy's theorem.) So for $x^3-3$ and $x^3-2$ the Frobenius sign bit is *literally* $p \bmod 3$ — the residue observable and the coupling bit are the same object.

For $x^3 - x - 1$ nothing of the sort happens: $-23$ is squarefree, the character has conductor $23$, and the residue observable lives in $(\mathbb{Z}/23)^\times$ with eleven quadratic residues and eleven non-residues. A different modulus, a different group, twenty-two input letters instead of two, a completely different arithmetic of primes.

And yet: the character is balanced, so the Character Channel Law fires, and the answer is again exactly $1$.

**Three independent $S_3$ fields, three distinct discriminants, two distinct residue groups, one universal answer.** The channel value depends on the Galois group and on nothing else — not on the polynomial, not on the conductor, not on the size of the alphabet.

## The same bit survives multiplication

Number theorists rarely look at one prime at a time. Consider a *semiprime* $n = pq$, and suppose all you can observe is the unordered pair of splitting types of its two prime factors — six possibilities, $\{S,S\}, \{S,P\}, \{S,I\}, \{P,P\}, \{P,I\}, \{I,I\}$, occurring among the $36$ Frobenius pairs with multiplicities

$$1 : 6 : 4 : 9 : 12 : 4.$$

A ragged, asymmetric profile with no visible structure. But the sign character is multiplicative: the sign of the pair is the product of the signs, hence a function of the *unordered* pair. Its two fibres carry masses $1 + 4 + 9 + 4 = 18$ and $6 + 12 = 18$ — balanced again, and again by accident of nothing.

So the channel from "$n$ modulo the conductor" to "the unordered type pair of $n$" is also exactly one bit. Six letters, a lopsided profile, and the same integer answer.

## Why exactly one bit is a fingerprint

An exact value is only interesting if nearby values are different. Three tests show that $1$ is genuinely detecting the group $S_3$ and the sign readout, and is not an artefact of small alphabets.

**Test 1: the type carries more than one bit.** The splitting-type distribution $\left(\tfrac16, \tfrac12, \tfrac13\right)$ has entropy
$$H(T) = \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 3 \approx 1.4591 \text{ bits} > 1.$$
So the channel is strictly lossy: the residue class knows the sign of the Frobenius, but not whether $p$ splits completely or stays inert. The residual $H(T) - I = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 3 - \tfrac13 \approx 0.4591$ bits is exactly the part of the splitting type that is invisible modulo the conductor.

**Test 2: change the readout, lose the integer.** Take the same field and the same primes, but ask a different yes/no question: *does $f$ have a root modulo $p$?* Yes for split and $1+2$, no for inert. This is a binary readout of the same three-letter alphabet, so if the one-bit law were an artefact of counting letters it would give $1$ again. It does not. The joint counts are $1, 2, 3, 0$ out of $6$, and the exact answer is
$$I(\text{residue}\,;\,\text{has a root}) = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 3 - \tfrac{1}{3} \approx 0.4591 \text{ bits}.$$
Strictly between $0$ and $1$, and not a rational number. The integer belongs to the sign character specifically.

**Test 3: change the group, lose the integer.** Take a *cyclic* cubic instead — one with square discriminant, Galois group $C_3$, such as $x^3 - 3x - 1$ with discriminant $81$. Now the Frobenius is itself abelian, hence completely determined by the residue class, and the channel from residue to Frobenius carries the whole group:
$$I = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits}.$$
Coarsen to the splitting type ("split or not"), and it drops to
$$I = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918 \text{ bits}.$$
One is above $1$, the other below, and neither is $1$. The value $1.0000$ is a spectral line of $S_3$.

## The general law behind all of it

Once the mechanism is visible, the binary hypothesis is obviously inessential. Replace the two-valued character by an invariant $\delta$ taking values in any finite set $D$, and assume only that its fibres are equally populated on the input side and carry equal weight on the output side. Then the same cancellation gives

> **The Coupling-Quotient Law.** If the sole statistical coupling between two finite observables is a $D$-valued invariant, balanced on both sides, then $I = \log_2 |D|$ exactly.

This is the master statement. The $S_3$ story is $|D| = 2$: the coupling invariant is the sign character, i.e. the quotient of $S_3$ by its commutator subgroup $A_3$. The cyclic cubic is $|D| = 3$: the group is abelian, its own abelianization, and the whole Frobenius passes through. The pattern suggests a general principle — that for a Galois group $G$ the residue-to-Frobenius channel should carry exactly $\log_2 [G : G']$ bits, where $G'$ is the commutator subgroup. Class field theory makes the abelianized Frobenius a function of the residue class; the non-abelian part is statistically invisible to congruences. For $S_3$ that index is $2$ and the answer is $1$; for $C_3$ it is $3$; for $A_4$, $S_4$ and $A_5$ it would predict $\log_2 3$, $1$, and $0$ — the last saying that for a simple group, congruence conditions tell you *nothing at all* about how primes split.

## Where the sign comes from

One loose thread deserves tying, because it explains why a *sign* is the natural invariant in the first place. Given any list of quantities $v_1, \dots, v_n$, form the Vandermonde product
$$\delta(v) = \prod_{i < j} (v_j - v_i).$$
Permuting the entries by $\sigma$ multiplies this product by the sign of $\sigma$:
$$\delta(v \circ \sigma) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, \delta(v).$$
This follows from the determinant formula for the Vandermonde matrix and the fact that permuting rows of a matrix multiplies its determinant by the sign. Consequently $\delta^2$ — the discriminant — is completely symmetric and therefore a rational number, while $\delta$ itself is a square root of it that the Galois group moves *only by a sign*. That square root generates the quadratic resolvent field, and the action of Galois on it is precisely the sign character. Everything else in this story is downstream of that one equivariance.

## The moral

There is a habit of thinking of information theory as a tool for noisy wires and compressed images, and of Galois theory as a tool for equations. The channel law says they are describing the same object here. The Chebotarev distribution is a source; the residue class is a receiver; and the capacity of the link between them is not an empirical number to be estimated but a group-theoretic invariant to be computed — the logarithm of the size of an abelianized quotient.

What makes the result satisfying is its *rigidity*. Change the polynomial from $x^3 - 3$ to $x^3 - 2$ and every individual number changes: the discriminant, the roots, the list of split primes. Change it again to $x^3 - x - 1$ and even the modulus changes, from $3$ to $23$, and the input alphabet grows from two letters to twenty-two. Nothing survives — except the answer.

One bit. Three fields. One answer.

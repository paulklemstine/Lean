# Six Is Two Times Three: How the Primes Split in a Sextic World

*Watching a prime break apart in a number field can look like a coin toss. At degree six it turns out to be two coin tosses running side by side, one fair and one weighted, that never talk to each other.*

---

## A prime, a clock, and a question

Pick a prime, say $p = 29$. Ask what it leaves when divided by $13$: $29 = 2\cdot 13 + 3$, so the remainder is $3$. That looks like a small, forgettable fact. But in number theory the remainder $p \bmod 13$ is one of the richest pieces of information a prime has. It decides how $p$ *breaks apart* in a whole family of number systems built from the thirteenth roots of unity.

Here is the background. The ordinary integers can be enlarged into bigger systems of "algebraic integers", for example by adjoining $\sqrt{13}$, or a complex number $\zeta_{13}$ with $\zeta_{13}^{13} = 1$. A prime that cannot be factored among ordinary integers may factor in the larger system. How it factors is called its **splitting type**. In the nicest case a prime of a degree-$n$ field breaks into pieces that all have the same **residue degree** $f$, a divisor of $n$:

- $f = 1$: the prime **splits completely** into $n$ pieces;
- $f = n$: the prime stays **inert**, one piece that refuses to break;
- anything in between: partial splitting into $n/f$ pieces.

For fields built from roots of unity (the *abelian* fields), there is a classical rule: the splitting type of $p$ depends **only on $p$ modulo a fixed number**, the conductor. Dirichlet's theorem then says each allowed remainder occurs equally often among the primes. So the splitting type behaves like a random variable with an exactly computable law, and one can ask how much information it carries.

This article is about one rung of a ladder of such questions: the field of degree six called $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})^+$.

## The ladder

Picture a ladder whose rungs are abelian fields of increasing degree: a quadratic field (degree 2), a cyclic cubic (3), a quartic (4), a quintic (5), and now a sextic (6). On each rung the same three questions come up:

1. **What are the splitting types and how often does each occur?** This gives an entropy $H(T)$, the number of bits of genuine uncertainty in the type $T$ of a random prime.
2. **Is the type pinned by the remainder?** Does knowing $p \bmod f$ remove all of that uncertainty? In information terms: is $I(p \bmod f;\,T) = H(T)$?
3. **What happens for a product of two primes?** If $N = pq$ is a semiprime and you only see $N \bmod f$, how many bits do you learn about the *pair* of splitting types of $p$ and $q$?

The third question is where the story touches the old problem of factoring. $N \bmod 13$ is a very cheap piece of data. How much of the hidden structure of $p$ and $q$ leaks through it?

Degree six is special because $6$ is the first rung that is **neither a prime nor a prime power**. Degrees $2, 3, 5$ are prime and $4 = 2^2$ is a prime power. Six is where two different primes, $2$ and $3$, meet for the first time.

## The sextic field and its splitting law

The field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})^+$ is the "real part" of the thirteenth cyclotomic field. It is generated by $\zeta_{13} + \zeta_{13}^{-1} = 2\cos(2\pi/13)$. Its symmetries form the group $(\mathbb{Z}/13)^\times/\{\pm 1\}$, the twelve nonzero remainders mod $13$ with each remainder identified with its negative. That group is **cyclic of order six**, $C_6$.

The residue degree of a prime $p \neq 13$ is the **order of $p$ in this group**: the smallest $k \ge 1$ with $p^k \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{13}$. Working through the twelve remainders gives the complete splitting law:

| $p \bmod 13$ | residue degree | behaviour | share of primes |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\pm 1$ | $1$ | splits completely into 6 | $1/6$ |
| $\pm 5$ | $2$ | splits into 3 pieces | $1/6$ |
| $\pm 3,\ \pm 4$ | $3$ | splits into 2 pieces | $1/3$ |
| $\pm 2,\ \pm 6$ | $6$ | inert | $1/3$ |

For example, $5^2 = 25 \equiv -1$, so $5$ has order $2$. And $3^3 = 27 \equiv 1$, so $3$ has order $3$. The remainder $2$ is a primitive root mod $13$, and its order in the quotient group is the full $6$.

The counts $2, 2, 4, 4$ follow a neat rule. In a cyclic group of order $6$, the number of elements of order $d$ is Euler's $\varphi(d)$: that is $1, 1, 2, 2$ for $d = 1, 2, 3, 6$. Each element of the quotient group comes from two remainders ($u$ and $-u$), so the counts double.

Our prime $29 \equiv 3$ therefore has residue degree $3$: in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})^+$ it breaks into exactly two pieces. Among real primes below two million, the four types occur at rates $0.1665,\ 0.1666,\ 0.3331,\ 0.3338$, in close agreement with $1/6, 1/6, 1/3, 1/3$.

## How much uncertainty is in the type?

The entropy of the type is

$$H(T) = 2\cdot\tfrac16\log_2 6 + 2\cdot\tfrac13\log_2 3 = \tfrac13 + \log_2 3 \approx 1.91830 \text{ bits}.$$

This value is exact. The experiment that motivated this work reported $H(T) = 1.9192$ and called it "exact". That was a small slip: the true value is below $1.91831$, so the reported figure overshoots by more than $0.0008$ bits. The difference is tiny, but it is real, and it matters when you are checking whether an identity holds exactly.

## Full pinning: the remainder knows everything

The second question has a clean answer. Since the residue degree is a function of the class of $p$ in $C_6$, and that class is a function of $p \bmod 13$, **knowing $p \bmod 13$ removes all uncertainty about the type**:

$$H(T \mid p \bmod 13) = 0, \qquad I(p \bmod 13;\,T) = H(T) = \tfrac13 + \log_2 3.$$

This is **full pinning**. It holds on every rung of the abelian ladder for a structural reason: the type is always read off from the Frobenius class, and the Frobenius class is always a function of the remainder. What degree six adds is a first look inside the pinning.

## The heart of the matter: six is two times three

The group $C_6$ is secretly a product. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, $\mathbb{Z}/6 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3$: knowing a number mod $6$ is the same as knowing it mod $2$ and mod $3$. The field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})^+$ mirrors this. It contains two smaller fields:

- the **quadratic field** $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{13})$, where $p$ splits exactly when $p$ is a *square* mod $13$ (equivalently $p^6 \equiv 1$);
- a **cyclic cubic field**, where $p$ splits exactly when $p$ is a *cube* mod $13$ (equivalently $p^4 \equiv 1$).

Write $T_2$ for the residue degree of $p$ in the quadratic field (so $T_2 \in \{1, 2\}$), $T_3$ for its residue degree in the cubic field ($T_3 \in \{1, 3\}$), and $T_6$ for its residue degree in the sextic field. The first main theorem is a factorisation.

> **The CRT Factorisation Theorem.** For every prime $p \ne 13$,
> $$T_6 = T_2 \cdot T_3,$$
> and conversely $T_2 = \gcd(T_6, 2)$ and $T_3 = \gcd(T_6, 3)$.

So the sextic behaviour of a prime is exactly the product of its quadratic behaviour and its cubic behaviour, with nothing left over. Take $p \equiv 5$: $5$ is not a square mod 13 but it is a cube ($5 \equiv 8^3$), so $T_2 = 2$, $T_3 = 1$, and $T_6 = 2$. Take $p \equiv 2$: neither a square nor a cube, so $T_6 = 2\cdot 3 = 6$ and $p$ is inert.

## Two independent coin tosses

The factorisation has an information-theoretic twin. The quadratic type is a **fair coin**: half the remainders are squares, so $H(T_2) = 1$ bit exactly. The cubic type is a **weighted coin**: one third of the remainders are cubes, so

$$H(T_3) = h_2(1/3) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac23 \approx 0.91830 \text{ bits},$$

where $h_2(x) = -x\log_2 x - (1-x)\log_2(1-x)$ is the binary entropy. The second main theorem says the two coins are independent and together make up the whole sextic uncertainty.

> **The Orthogonal Split Theorem.** For $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})^+$:
> - the sextic type carries exactly one bit about the quadratic field: $I(T_6;\,T_2) = H(T_2) = 1$;
> - it carries exactly $\log_2 3 - \tfrac23$ bits about the cubic field: $I(T_6;\,T_3) = H(T_3)$;
> - these add up to everything: $H(T_6) = I(T_6;T_2) + I(T_6;T_3)$;
> - the quadratic and cubic types are **independent**: $I(T_2;\,T_3) = 0$.

A consequence is that **neither subfield pins the sextic type on its own**. If you know the quadratic behaviour of $p$, you are still missing $\log_2 3 - \tfrac23 \approx 0.918$ bits. If you know the cubic behaviour, you are still missing exactly one bit. You need both, and both is exactly enough.

The arithmetic checks out:

$$\underbrace{1}_{\text{quadratic}} + \underbrace{\left(\log_2 3 - \tfrac23\right)}_{\text{cubic}} = \tfrac13 + \log_2 3 = H(T_6).$$

## The general law: composite rungs add nothing new

Degree six was the test case, but the same thing happens on every composite rung. In the abstract model, a random prime's Frobenius is a uniformly random element $a$ of the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n$, and its type is the order $n/\gcd(a, n)$. Let $H(T_n)$ be the entropy of that order.

> **CRT Additivity Theorem.** If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then
> $$H(T_{mn}) = H(T_m) + H(T_n).$$
> Consequently, for any $n = p_1^{e_1}\cdots p_r^{e_r}$,
> $$H(T_n) = H(T_{p_1^{e_1}}) + \cdots + H(T_{p_r^{e_r}}).$$

The general version of the orthogonal split also holds: for coprime $m, n$ the composite type determines each component through $T_m = \gcd(T_{mn}, m)$, carries exactly $H(T_m)$ and $H(T_n)$ bits about them, and the components share no information.

The proof uses one tool that deserves mention, the **chain rule** $H(X \mid Y) = H(X, Y) - H(Y)$. From it the mutual information takes its symmetric form $I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$, so it is symmetric. It also gives a principle that runs through all of the above: *if $Y$ is a function of $X$, then $X$ tells you everything about $Y$*, $I(X;Y) = H(Y)$.

This is the precise sense of the slogan **"the ladder is complete."** It does not mean nothing remains to be discovered. It means that past the prime-power rungs $2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, \ldots$, **a composite abelian rung carries no information of its own**. Its entropy is the sum of the entropies of its primary pieces. Degree $6$ is $2$ plus $3$, degree $12$ is $4$ plus $3$, degree $30$ is $2$ plus $3$ plus $5$. The new mathematics lives only at prime powers.

## Semiprimes: what does $N \bmod 13$ reveal?

Now the factoring question. Let $N = pq$ be a product of two primes. The Frobenius of $N$ is the *product* of the Frobenius classes of $p$ and $q$; written additively in $\mathbb{Z}/6$, it is $a + b$. Seeing $N \bmod 13$ tells you $a + b$ but not $a$ and $b$ separately. How much does it reveal about the pair of types $(T(p), T(q))$?

Direct enumeration of the $36$ equally likely pairs $(a, b)$ gives

$$I\big(N \bmod 13;\ (T(p),T(q))\big) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac19 \approx 1.47385 \text{ bits}.$$

The experiment reported $1.4704$, which is too low by more than $0.003$ bits. The exact value also splits: $1$ bit from the quadratic channel plus $\log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9} \approx 0.47385$ bits from the cubic channel.

A cruder read-out is the **split count**: how many of $p, q$ split completely. It knows only whether $a = 0$ and whether $b = 0$. Its channel has the closed form

$$I\big(N \bmod 13;\ \text{split count}\big) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{55}{36}\log_2 5 + \tfrac{19}{9} \approx 0.14868 \text{ bits}.$$

That is **less than 11%** of the pair information, so about ninety percent of what $N \bmod 13$ says about the factor types is lost. Degree $6$ is the second composite degree, after $4$, where this strict loss has been established.

There is also a curiosity. For prime degrees $q$ there is a known closed formula for the split-count channel. At $q = 6$, where $6$ is not prime, the formula still gives the right answer. The split count is **blind to compositeness**: it only ever asks "is $a$ zero?", and that question is the same whether or not $6$ factors. The full type pair does see the $2 \times 3$ structure, and that is why it retains so much more.

## Why it matters

Why study how much a remainder "knows"? Partly because the answer is clean. Splitting of primes is among the oldest subjects in algebraic number theory, and the information-theoretic view rephrases classical facts (Dirichlet density, the Chinese Remainder Theorem, Frobenius elements) as exact identities between entropies. It also calibrates experiments. When a numerical experiment reports $1.9192$ and the truth is $\tfrac13 + \log_2 3 = 1.91830\ldots$, only a proof can tell rounding noise from an actual effect. Finally, it clarifies what cheap modular data can and cannot reveal about the factors of a semiprime. The answer here is quite a lot about their types, and nothing at all about their identities.

## What comes next

The completed ladder points toward the prime powers. Conjecturally, the entropies $H(T_{p^k})$ increase in $k$ and level off at $\frac{p}{p-1}\,h_2(1/p)$, the entropy of a geometric law. For $p = 2$ the values are $1, \tfrac32, \tfrac74, \tfrac{15}{8}, \ldots \to 2$. For $p = 3$ they are $0.918, 1.224, 1.326, \ldots \to 1.377$. If that holds, every rung of the abelian ladder, however composite, would carry fewer than $\sum_{p\mid n}\frac{p}{p-1}h_2(1/p)$ bits.

So degree six turns out to be two coin tosses, a fair one for $\sqrt{13}$ and a weighted one for the cubic field, and the whole sextic splitting law is what you get by reading them together.

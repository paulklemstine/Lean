# Two Trees That Refused to Be the Same

## A tale of Pythagoras, Markoff, and the exact place where a beautiful analogy breaks

There is a particular kind of pleasure in noticing that two things which grew up in different neighbourhoods of mathematics look suspiciously alike. It is the pleasure of the pun, and like a pun it can be shallow or profound. Occasionally the resemblance is a genuine identity in disguise, and pushing on it collapses two subjects into one. More often the resemblance is real but partial, and the interesting mathematics is not the analogy itself but the precise location where it fails.

This is the story of one such pun, pursued until it broke — and of the unexpected fragments that survived the break.

---

## Two trees

The first tree is old. Take a right triangle with whole-number sides: $3, 4, 5$. Now note that there is an operation — three of them, in fact — that turns any such triangle into a new one, and that starting from $(3,4,5)$ and applying these operations in every possible order produces **every** primitive Pythagorean triple exactly once. ("Primitive" means the three sides share no common factor; $(6,8,10)$ is just $(3,4,5)$ wearing a costume.)

The cleanest way to see the operations is through the classical parametrisation: every primitive triple is
$$a = m^2 - n^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = m^2 + n^2$$
for a coprime pair $m > n > 0$ of opposite parity. The triple $(3,4,5)$ is the pair $(2,1)$. The three moves are then startlingly simple:
$$(m,n) \;\longmapsto\; (2m-n,\; m), \qquad (m,n) \;\longmapsto\; (2m+n,\; m), \qquad (m,n) \;\longmapsto\; (m+2n,\; n).$$
From $(2,1)$ these produce $(3,2), (5,2), (4,1)$ — that is, the triangles $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$. Each of those has three children of its own, and so on forever. Every node has exactly three children and — the crucial point — exactly one parent. The result is an infinite **ternary** tree containing every primitive right triangle in the universe, each one exactly once. Level $n$ of the tree holds precisely
$$3^n \text{ nodes}.$$

The second tree is younger, from 1879, and lives on a different equation. Call a triple of positive integers $(x,y,z)$ a **Markoff triple** if
$$x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 3xyz.$$
The smallest is $(1,1,1)$. Then $(1,1,2)$, $(1,2,5)$, $(1,5,13)$, $(2,5,29)$, $(1,13,34)$, $(5,29,433)$, and onwards. These innocuous-looking numbers are secretly everywhere: they control how badly an irrational number can be approximated by fractions (the *Markoff spectrum*), they index a distinguished family of closed geodesics on the modular surface, and they parametrise the simple closed curves on a once-punctured torus.

The Markoff tree is built by a trick going back to Vieta. Fix $x$ and $y$; the equation is a *quadratic* in $z$,
$$z^2 - (3xy)\,z + (x^2+y^2) = 0,$$
so if $z$ is one root, the other root is $3xy - z$ — and it is automatically an integer. This **Vieta involution** takes Markoff triples to Markoff triples. Applying it to each of the three coordinates in turn and always growing rather than shrinking, one finds that every Markoff triple flows out of $(1,1,1)$, and that above the small exceptional triples the tree is **binary**: level $n$ holds precisely
$$2^n \text{ nodes}.$$

Both trees are canonical. Both are generated by involutions preserving a quadratic-flavoured equation. Both carry hyperbolic geometry. Both have a distinguished "spine" of fastest growth. It is almost irresistible to conjecture that they are, at bottom, the same tree.

## The conjecture, and the number three

The conjecture was worth making because the Pythagorean side is *solved* and the Markoff side is *not*. The outstanding open problem here is the **Markoff uniqueness conjecture**, stated by Frobenius in 1913: is a Markoff triple determined by its largest entry? Nobody knows. If the two trees were isomorphic in a structure-preserving way, the mature machinery on the Pythagorean side — the Lorentz-group action, the hyperbolic embedding, the exact growth theory — could be shipped wholesale to the Markoff side, and one would have a genuinely new line of attack on a hundred-year-old problem.

So: are the two trees isomorphic?

They are not, and the reason is embarrassingly elementary once you see it. **The Berggren tree is ternary and the Markoff tree is binary.** Three does not equal two. Level $n$ of one has $3^n$ nodes and level $n$ of the other has $2^n$, so from level $1$ onwards there is not even an *injection* of the first into the second.

One might hope this is an artefact of matching levels globally, and that a cleverer local correspondence could work. It cannot, and the obstruction is completely local:

> **The Branching Obstruction.** There is no injective map $f$ from Pythagorean triples to Markoff triples with the property that each of the three children of the root is sent to a child of $f(\text{root})$. Indeed, the root of the Pythagorean tree has three genuinely distinct children, while any node of the Markoff tree offers only two child slots; by the pigeonhole principle two of the three must be sent to the same place, contradicting injectivity.

That is the whole proof. It requires no compatibility with roots, no global hypotheses, nothing but counting to three. The conjecture dies at the very first branch point.

## Golden and silver

Killing a conjecture with the pigeonhole principle is unsatisfying, so it is worth asking whether the failure is *only* combinatorial. Perhaps if one forgot the branching and looked only at the metric — the rate at which numbers grow along the tree — the two structures would match.

They do not, and here the obstruction has a completely different flavour: it is arithmetic.

Each tree has a **spine**, the path of fastest growth obtained by repeating one move forever. On the Pythagorean side, iterating $(m,n) \mapsto (2m+n, m)$ from $(2,1)$ produces hypotenuses
$$5,\; 29,\; 169,\; 985,\; 5741,\; \dots$$
and these satisfy the linear recursion
$$u_{n+2} = 6\,u_{n+1} - u_n,$$
whose characteristic polynomial is $X^2 - 6X + 1$ with roots $3 \pm 2\sqrt{2} = (1 \pm \sqrt{2})^2$. The growth rate is the square of the **silver ratio** $1+\sqrt 2$. This is the Pell world; $\sqrt 2$ is in charge.

On the Markoff side, the analogous spine is the branch of triples whose smallest entry is $1$:
$$(1,1,2),\ (1,2,5),\ (1,5,13),\ (1,13,34),\ (1,34,89),\ \dots$$
whose entries $1,1,2,5,13,34,89,\dots$ satisfy
$$s_{n+2} = 3\,s_{n+1} - s_n,$$
with characteristic polynomial $X^2 - 3X + 1$, roots $(3\pm\sqrt 5)/2 = \varphi^{\pm 2}$. These are the odd-index Fibonacci numbers, and the growth rate is the square of the **golden ratio**. Here $\sqrt 5$ is in charge.

Could a linear change of coordinates carry one dynamic to the other? No: conjugate matrices have equal traces, and the two transfer matrices have traces $6$ and $3$. But there is a sharper statement, and it is the one that really explains the gap:

> **The Metric Obstruction.** $\sqrt 5$ does not lie in the field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt 2)$. The golden growth rate is not merely different from the silver one; it is not even *expressible* in the quadratic field where the silver growth lives.

The two trees measure length in incompatible number systems — discriminant $5$ versus discriminant $8$. No amount of ingenuity in choosing a correspondence can reconcile them, because the reconciliation would have to happen inside a field that does not contain the required square root.

## The third wall: linearity

There is one more difference, and it is the deepest of the three. The Pythagorean moves are **matrices**. Written on triples $(a,b,c)$, each Berggren move is a $3\times 3$ integer matrix preserving the Lorentz form $a^2+b^2-c^2$; the whole tree is an orbit of a discrete subgroup of the Lorentz group $O(2,1)$, which is exactly why hyperbolic geometry applies to it so cleanly.

The Vieta move is not a matrix. It cannot be:

> **The Linearity Obstruction.** No $3\times3$ matrix with rational entries acts on the Markoff surface as the Vieta move $(x,y,z) \mapsto (x, y, 3xy - z)$. Four explicit Markoff triples — $(1,1,1)$, $(1,2,5)$, $(1,5,13)$, $(2,5,29)$ — already impose four mutually inconsistent linear conditions on the last row of such a matrix.

The Markoff dynamics is irreducibly quadratic; the term $3xy$ is a product of two coordinates and no linear map can manufacture it. So the Lorentz-geometric half of the Pythagorean machinery has nothing to attach itself to.

Three walls, three different reasons: **combinatorial** (three versus two), **arithmetic** ($\sqrt5$ versus $\sqrt2$), **algebraic** (quadratic versus linear). The conjectured transfer is not merely false; it is false in three independent ways.

## What survived

And yet the story does not end in demolition, because the wreckage contains something nobody was looking for.

**First, freeness transfers.** The purely combinatorial heart of the Pythagorean structure is that distinct words in the three generators give distinct triples — the tree is *free* of rank $3$. The exact analogue holds on the Markoff side with rank $2$: distinct binary words give distinct Markoff triples. Better, the Markoff tree embeds, depth-preservingly and injectively, into the Pythagorean tree as the sub-tree spanned by two of the three generators. The free-monoid half of the machinery transfers perfectly; it just lands inside a rank-$2$ sub-structure and can never be an isomorphism.

**Second — and this is the surprise — the Vieta dynamics *is* linear, one fibre at a time.** Sort each Markoff triple so its smallest entry comes first and group the triples by that smallest entry $x$. Within such a group, the moves that keep $x$ fixed act on the remaining pair by
$$(y, z) \;\longmapsto\; (z,\; 3xz - y),$$
which is the linear map
$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 3x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det = 1, \quad \operatorname{tr} = 3x.$$
Determinant $1$: it lies in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$. Trace $3x > 2$ for every $x \ge 1$: it is hyperbolic, so every branch grows exponentially. The global quadratic nonlinearity dissolves into a family of linear systems, one per fibre.

**Third, the two smallest fibres are exactly the two classical spines, and one of them is literally the Pythagorean spine.** Set $x=1$: the trace is $3$, the polynomial is $X^2-3X+1$, and the fibre is precisely the consecutive odd-index Fibonacci pairs — the golden branch, and *nothing else* lies over $x=1$. Set $x=2$: the trace is $6$, the polynomial is $X^2 - 6X + 1$ — the silver polynomial — and the fibre is
$$(2,5,29),\ (2,29,169),\ (2,169,985),\ (2,985,5741),\ \dots$$
Those numbers $5, 29, 169, 985, 5741$ are exactly the hypotenuses along the Pythagorean spine. The identification is exact in both directions: consecutive spine hypotenuses always form a Markoff triple with smallest entry $2$, and *every* Markoff triple with smallest entry $2$ arises this way.

So the transfer that was conjectured globally does exist — but *fibrewise*. The Pythagorean silver spine is not an analogue of a Markoff branch. It **is** a Markoff branch, the one sitting over $x=2$.

## What the tree method still buys

The descent argument that builds the tree is a workhorse, and it yields concrete arithmetic about the Markoff surface. Every positive integer solution of $x^2+y^2+z^2=3xyz$ has **pairwise coprime** coordinates; **no coordinate is divisible by $3$**; and **at most one coordinate is even**. Each is proved by checking the property at the root $(1,1,1)$ and showing the Vieta move preserves it, then invoking the fact that every solution descends to the root.

Descent also sharpens the open problem. Here is a clean rigidity statement:

> **Middle-Entry Rigidity.** If $(x,y,z)$ and $(x,y',z)$ are ordered positive Markoff triples with the same smallest and largest entries, then $y = y'$.

The proof is two lines. Both $y$ and $y'$ are roots of $t^2 - 3xz\,t + (x^2+z^2)$. If they differed they would be *the* two roots, so their product would be $x^2 + z^2 > z^2$ — impossible, since both are at most $z$.

The consequence is a genuine reduction of Frobenius's conjecture. Uniqueness asks that the largest entry determine the whole triple. Rigidity says the middle entry comes for free once the smallest and largest are known. Therefore:

> **Reduction.** The Markoff uniqueness conjecture is *equivalent* to the statement that the largest entry determines the **smallest** entry.

Combine that with the fibrewise picture. Every Markoff triple lives in the fibre over its smallest entry; within a fibre the dynamics is a hyperbolic element of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ of trace $3x$; and within a fibre the largest entry determines the triple outright. So uniqueness is exactly the assertion that **the fibres do not collide**: that two different values of $x$ never produce the same maximum. The conjecture has been repackaged from a statement about a two-dimensional tree into a statement about the interleaving of a one-parameter family of exponentially growing sequences.

## The moral

The hypothesis was that a solved structure and an open one were secretly the same. They are not, and the three obstructions — pigeonhole, $\sqrt5 \notin \mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$, and the impossibility of a matrix producing $3xy$ — are now theorems rather than suspicions. That is worth something on its own: a precise map of where a natural analogy stops is more useful than a vague sense that it might work.

But the better outcome is the one that was not planned. Chasing the failed global transfer forced a look at the Markoff surface fibre by fibre, and fibrewise the transfer is *real*: the dynamics is linear, the transfer matrix is a hyperbolic element of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ of trace $3x$, the fibre over $1$ is Fibonacci, and the fibre over $2$ is, on the nose, the ancient spine of Pythagorean triangles. Two trees that refused to be the same turned out to share a branch after all.

The natural next question writes itself. If $x=1$ gives Fibonacci and $x=2$ gives Pell — both classical, both exactly identified — what does the fibre over a general $x$ know? Each is governed by a trace-$3x$ element of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$, and the uniqueness conjecture is the demand that this infinite family of hyperbolic sequences never accidentally agree. It is not a proof. But it is a considerably more concrete thing to fail to prove.

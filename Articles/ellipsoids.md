# The Shadow Arithmetic of Ellipsoids

## How one determinant and one vector length decide the size of every slice

Cut an orange in half. The face you expose is a disc, and if you cut through the centre it is the biggest disc you can get. Now imagine the orange squashed — a rugby ball, a lens, a cigar. Cut *that* through its centre, at any angle you like, and the exposed face is an ellipse whose area depends, sometimes dramatically, on the angle. Tilt the knife a little and the ellipse swells; tilt it the other way and it shrinks.

That simple picture hides a question that has occupied geometers for a century. If I hand you a convex body in high dimensions and let you look only at its central cross-sections — its silhouettes, its slices — how much do you learn about the body itself? Can two different bodies have the same slice profile? Is there always a slice that is unusually large, or unusually small?

For general convex bodies these are hard and in some cases still-open questions. For **ellipsoids** — the squashed, stretched, rotated images of a round ball — everything can be answered exactly, in closed form, with a formula so clean it fits on one line. This article is about that formula, and about how far it reaches.

---

## What an ellipsoid really is

An ellipse in the plane is usually introduced by its equation, $x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 = 1$. That is a fine description, but it is the wrong one for what follows. The better description is *dynamic*: an ellipsoid is what you get when you take a perfectly round ball and push it through a linear map.

Write $B^n$ for the closed unit ball in $n$-dimensional space, the set of all points at distance at most $1$ from the origin. Let $A$ be an $n \times n$ matrix. Define

$$E(A) = A\,B^n = \{\, Ax \;:\; \|x\| \le 1 \,\}.$$

If $A$ is the identity, $E(A)$ is the ball. If $A$ is the diagonal matrix with entries $a$ and $b$, $E(A)$ is the familiar ellipse with semiaxes $a$ and $b$. If $A$ is a rotation, $E(A)$ is the ball again — rotating a ball changes nothing. And if $A$ is a general invertible matrix, $E(A)$ is a tilted, stretched ellipsoid in some skew position.

This "image of the ball" definition is the one that makes everything computable, because linear algebra does the geometry for us. The very first consequence is the classical volume rule. A linear map multiplies every volume by the absolute value of its determinant, so

$$\operatorname{vol}\bigl(E(A)\bigr) = |\det A| \cdot \operatorname{vol}(B^n).$$

One number, $|\det A|$, is the entire volume story. In particular, an ellipsoid generated by a matrix of determinant $1$ has exactly the volume of the unit ball, however extravagantly elongated it may be. Squeezing in one direction and stretching in another can leave you thin as a wafer and long as a needle — and still exactly ball-sized.

There is a static description too, dual to the dynamic one. For invertible $A$,

$$E(A) = \bigl\{\, x \;:\; \langle (AA^{\mathsf T})^{-1} x,\; x\rangle \le 1 \,\bigr\},$$

which is the "quadratic form" picture: an ellipsoid is a sublevel set of a positive definite quadratic form. The matrix $A$ that generates the ellipsoid is not unique — only the symmetric matrix $AA^{\mathsf T}$ is. Indeed if $U$ is orthogonal (a rotation or reflection), then $A U$ and $A$ generate *the same* ellipsoid, because $U$ merely reshuffles the ball before $A$ acts on it. So without loss we may take the generator to be positive definite and symmetric, and then the **spectral theorem** gives the canonical form: every ellipsoid is a rotated copy of a diagonal one,

$$E(A) = U \cdot E\bigl(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)\bigr),$$

where the $\lambda_i > 0$ are the eigenvalues of $A$ — the **semiaxes** — and the columns of the orthogonal matrix $U$ are the axis directions. The picture in your head (a rugby ball tilted in space) is now a theorem.

Two immediate corollaries. First, the ellipsoid is trapped between two round balls:

$$B(0, \lambda_{\min}) \subseteq E(A) \subseteq B(0, \lambda_{\max}).$$

The smallest semiaxis is the inradius, the largest is the circumradius. Second, since the determinant of a positive definite matrix is the product of its eigenvalues, $\operatorname{vol}(E(A)) = (\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n)\operatorname{vol}(B^n)$: volume is the product of the semiaxes, as everyone expects.

## The slicing formula

Now the knife. Fix a unit vector $u$ and cut along the hyperplane $u^\perp$ — all points perpendicular to $u$, through the origin. The slice $E(A) \cap u^\perp$ is itself an ellipsoid, one dimension down. What is its $(n-1)$-dimensional volume?

The answer is startlingly compact:

$$\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}\bigl(E(A) \cap u^\perp\bigr) \;=\; \frac{|\det A|}{\|A^{\mathsf T} u\|} \cdot \operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(B^{n-1}).$$

Everything about the direction of the cut is compressed into a single number, the length of the vector $A^{\mathsf T}u$. Everything about the body is compressed into $|\det A|$. Nothing else matters.

Why should such a thing be true? The proof is a chain of three steps, each pleasant in itself.

**Step one: parametrise the slice.** A hyperplane through the origin is an $(n-1)$-dimensional space, so choose an orthonormal frame for it: an $n\times(n-1)$ matrix $\iota$ whose columns are an orthonormal basis of $u^\perp$. The slice, read in those coordinates, is the set of $y \in \mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ with $\iota y \in E(A)$, and by the "image of the ball" definition that says exactly $\|A^{-1}\iota\, y\| \le 1$. The slice has become a preimage of the unit ball under a *rectangular* linear map $T = A^{-1}\iota$.

**Step two: the Gram determinant.** A rectangular map has no determinant, but it has a Gram matrix $T^{\mathsf T}T$, and that is square and positive definite when $T$ is injective. The correct generalisation of the volume rule is

$$\operatorname{vol}_m\{\, y : \|Ty\| \le 1 \,\} = \frac{\operatorname{vol}(B^m)}{\sqrt{\det (T^{\mathsf T}T)}}.$$

This is the "$\sqrt{\det T^{\mathsf T}T}$" that appears in every change-of-variables formula for surfaces; here it comes out of a genuine geometric computation rather than being postulated.

**Step three: compute the Gram determinant.** This is the only technical moment, and it is a determinant identity in disguise. One shows

$$\det\bigl((A^{-1}\iota)^{\mathsf T}(A^{-1}\iota)\bigr) = \frac{\|A^{\mathsf T}u\|^2}{(\det A)^2}.$$

The trick is to *complete the frame*: append the extra column $A^{\mathsf T}u/\|A^{\mathsf T}u\|$ (suitably normalised) to $A^{-1}\iota$ so that the rectangular matrix becomes square, note that the appended column is orthogonal to the others, and read off the determinant of the enlarged square matrix in two ways. Squares of determinants of square matrices are Gram determinants; the identity falls out.

Combine the three steps and the slicing formula appears. Notice a hidden but important point: the formula does not depend on which orthonormal frame $\iota$ of the hyperplane you chose. It could not, of course — the slice is a fixed set and its volume is a fixed number — but the *proof* passes through a frame, so frame-independence is something to be checked, and it is. Moreover such a frame always exists: extend $u$ to an orthonormal basis and take the other $n-1$ vectors. That last remark sounds like a triviality, but it is what upgrades a conditional statement ("given a frame, the slice has volume ...") into an unconditional one ("the slice has volume ...").

## Reading the formula

Once you have $\operatorname{vol}_{n-1}(E(A)\cap u^\perp) = (|\det A| / \|A^{\mathsf T}u\|)\operatorname{vol}(B^{n-1})$, a whole landscape opens up.

**Extremes are eigendirections.** For a positive definite generator with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$, and $u$ a unit vector, the quantity $\|A^{\mathsf T}u\| = \|Au\|$ is a weighted average of the eigenvalues: writing $u$ in the eigenbasis with coordinates $c_i$, one has $\|Au\|^2 = \sum_i \lambda_i^2 c_i^2$ with $\sum_i c_i^2 = 1$. Hence $\lambda_{\min} \le \|Au\| \le \lambda_{\max}$, and therefore

$$\frac{\det A}{\lambda_{\max}}\operatorname{vol}(B^{n-1}) \;\le\; \operatorname{vol}_{n-1}\bigl(E(A)\cap u^\perp\bigr) \;\le\; \frac{\det A}{\lambda_{\min}}\operatorname{vol}(B^{n-1}).$$

Slicing perpendicular to the *shortest* axis gives the *biggest* slice; slicing perpendicular to the longest axis gives the smallest. That matches intuition — cutting a cigar across the middle at right angles to the long axis gives a small disc; cutting it lengthwise gives a long thin ellipse of far greater area.

And the converse holds, which is the sharper statement: the bounds are attained *only* in eigendirections. If the slice orthogonal to $u$ has the maximal volume $\det A/\lambda_{\min}$ times the ball, then $u$ is an eigenvector for $\lambda_{\min}$; likewise at the other end. The reason is the equality case of the weighted average: $\sum \lambda_i^2 c_i^2 = \lambda_{\max}^2$ with $\sum c_i^2 = 1$ forces $c_i = 0$ for every $i$ with $\lambda_i < \lambda_{\max}$, so $u$ lives in the top eigenspace. Extremal slices, in other words, *see* the spectrum: the geometry of the cross-sections recovers the eigenvalues and eigenspaces of the generator exactly.

**A determinant conservation law.** Take the $n$ principal sections, one orthogonal to each axis. Their normalised volumes are $\det A/\lambda_1, \dots, \det A/\lambda_n$. Multiply them:

$$\prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\det A}{\lambda_i} = \frac{(\det A)^n}{\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_n} = (\det A)^{n-1}.$$

The individual principal slices can be anything at all — huge, minuscule — but their product is pinned to a power of the volume. Distortion is conserved: you cannot make every slice small.

**Unimodular ellipsoids straddle the ball.** Specialise to $\det A = 1$, so that $E(A)$ has exactly the ball's volume. Then some eigenvalue is $\le 1$ and some is $\ge 1$ (their product is $1$), so there is always a direction whose central slice is *at least* as big as the unit ball's central slice, and another whose central slice is *at most* that big. A volume-normalised ellipsoid can never be uniformly thinner, nor uniformly fatter, than a ball. Only the ball itself is exactly ball-like in every direction — and the reason will surface again in a moment.

## Duality: the ellipsoid you cannot see

Every convex body $K$ containing the origin has a **polar** body,

$$K^\circ = \{\, y : \langle y,x\rangle \le 1 \text{ for all } x \in K \,\},$$

which encodes $K$ by its supporting hyperplanes rather than by its points. Polarity is a beautiful involution: it reverses inclusions, turns unions into intersections, and fixes the unit ball ($B^\circ = B$: the ball is self-dual).

For ellipsoids it is completely explicit:

$$E(A)^\circ = E\bigl((A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\bigr).$$

Squashing a ball in one direction is dual to stretching it in that direction — exactly as one would hope. More generally, polarity is *covariant* under any invertible linear map: $(A \cdot S)^\circ = (A^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\cdot S^\circ$ for any set $S$ at all. And that covariance has an immediate and remarkable consequence for the **volume product** $\operatorname{vol}(K)\operatorname{vol}(K^\circ)$: the determinant factor $|\det A|$ appearing in the first factor is cancelled exactly by the $|\det A|^{-1}$ in the second. The volume product is a *linear invariant*.

For ellipsoids we can therefore evaluate it once and for all:

$$\operatorname{vol}\bigl(E(A)\bigr)\cdot \operatorname{vol}\bigl(E(A)^\circ\bigr) = \operatorname{vol}(B^n)^2.$$

This is the **Blaschke–Santaló equality**: every ellipsoid — every one, of every shape and eccentricity — has exactly the same volume product as the ball. The famous Blaschke–Santaló *inequality* says that for symmetric convex bodies this product is at most $\operatorname{vol}(B^n)^2$, with equality precisely for ellipsoids. The equality half is what we have just proved, in three lines, from covariance.

Duality also yields a soft but genuinely useful estimate: if a body $S$ is squeezed between two ellipsoids, $E(A) \subseteq S \subseteq E(B)$, then

$$\operatorname{vol}(S)\operatorname{vol}(S^\circ) \le \frac{|\det B|}{|\det A|}\operatorname{vol}(B^n)^2.$$

Approximating a body by ellipsoids from inside and out immediately bounds its volume product; the quality of the bound is precisely the ratio of the two determinants.

## The intersection body, and why the ball is alone

Here is the most striking result of the whole circle of ideas.

Given a body $K$, define a new body $IK$ — its **intersection body** — by declaring the distance from the origin to the boundary of $IK$ in the direction $u$ to be the $(n-1)$-volume of the slice $K \cap u^\perp$ (normalised by the ball's slice). In other words: *draw the slice profile as a shape*. Intersection bodies are the central object of the dual Brunn–Minkowski theory, and they are the engine behind the celebrated Busemann–Petty problem, where the question "do smaller slices imply smaller volume?" turns out to have answer *yes* in dimensions up to $4$ and *no* from dimension $5$ onwards.

For an ellipsoid, the slicing formula says the radial function in the direction $u$ is $|\det A| / \|A^{\mathsf T}u\|$. Is that the radial function of some ellipsoid? It is. Writing $S = \sqrt{AA^{\mathsf T}}$ for the positive definite square root of the Gram matrix, one finds

$$I\bigl(E(A)\bigr) = E\bigl(|\det A|\, S^{-1}\bigr),$$

so **the intersection body of an ellipsoid is an ellipsoid**. Its generator has determinant $|\det A|^{n-1}$, so its volume is $|\det A|^{n-1}\operatorname{vol}(B^n)$ — again all the geometry compressed into the determinant.

When the generator is already positive definite, $S = A$ and the recipe simplifies beautifully: the intersection operator is
$$A \longmapsto (\det A)\,A^{-1}.$$
Restrict to unimodular generators, $\det A = 1$. Then the operator is just $A \mapsto A^{-1}$ — an involution, applying it twice returns the original ellipsoid. And its only fixed point is $A = I$, because $A^{-1} = A$ for a positive definite matrix means $A^2 = I$ means $A = I$.

Translated into geometry: **among all ellipsoids of the same volume as the unit ball, the ball is the unique one that is its own intersection body.** The ball is the only shape whose slice profile reproduces itself.

## The largest ellipsoid inside a body

One last thread, of a different flavour. Ellipsoids are the standard tool for *approximating* other convex bodies, and the standard approximation is John's ellipsoid: the ellipsoid of maximal volume contained in a body $K$. Its existence underlies a large part of modern convex geometry and functional analysis, because it lets one replace an arbitrary body by an ellipsoid at a controlled cost.

Existence is a compactness argument, and the "image of a ball" definition makes it transparent. Consider the set of **inscribed generators**

$$\mathcal{G}(K) = \{\, A : A \text{ positive semidefinite}, \ E(A)\subseteq K \,\}.$$

Because $\operatorname{vol}(E(A)) = |\det A|\operatorname{vol}(B^n)$, maximising volume is the same as maximising $\det A$ over $\mathcal{G}(K)$. And $\mathcal{G}(K)$ is closed (this is where allowing *degenerate*, singular, positive semidefinite generators pays off — flat "ellipsoids" are exactly the limits that keep the set closed) and bounded whenever $K$ is bounded, hence compact. The determinant is continuous. A continuous function on a compact set attains its maximum. Provided $K$ contains *some* nondegenerate ellipsoid to start from, the maximiser has positive determinant, hence is positive definite, hence is a genuine ellipsoid: **every closed bounded body with nonempty interior contains a maximal-volume ellipsoid.**

---

## What it all adds up to

The recurring theme is a kind of *arithmetic of shadows*. A single scalar, $|\det A|$, controls volume. A single scalar per direction, $\|A^{\mathsf T}u\|$, controls the slice in that direction. The extremes of the second quantity are the eigenvalues of the first quantity's matrix, so slicing data and spectral data are the same data seen from two sides. Products of slices are constrained by determinants; volume products are determinant-invariant; the slice profile itself is again an ellipsoid, and the operator that produces it is an involution whose only fixed point is the ball.

Ellipsoids are the one family of convex bodies where all of this can be written down exactly, and that is why they are the yardstick against which the rest of convex geometry is measured. When a general theorem about slices is conjectured, one tests it on ellipsoids first; when a general body must be tamed, one puts an ellipsoid inside it. The formulas above are the reason the yardstick is trustworthy: they are not estimates or asymptotics but identities, valid in every dimension, for every ellipsoid, with equality cases characterised exactly.

Cut the orange wherever you like. The size of the face you expose is $|\det A|$ divided by $\|A^{\mathsf T}u\|$ — and now you know why.

# The Future Has Many Pasts: Cryptography and the Logistic Map

*By Aristotle — July 18, 2026*

A secure lock should be hard to reverse. That intuition is so familiar that it is easy to miss a second question: if we run a system backward, do we recover one past, or many?

The logistic map gives a particularly vivid answer. Begin with a real number $x$ between zero and one and repeatedly apply

$$
f(x)=4x(1-x).
$$

This tiny quadratic formula is one of the classic emblems of chaos. Nearby starting points can separate rapidly, producing trajectories that look irregular despite being generated by a deterministic rule. Such sensitivity has inspired proposals to use chaotic dynamics in encryption and pseudorandom generation. Yet sensitivity alone is not security. The backward structure matters too—and at the parameter $4$, that structure is an exact binary tree.

The central result is simple to state. Choose any target $y$ strictly between $0$ and $1$, and choose any nonnegative integer $n$. There are at least $2^n$ distinct starting values in $(0,1)$ that arrive at $y$ after exactly $n$ applications of the logistic map. Better still, those starting values can be constructed explicitly, one for each binary word of length $n$.

That is not merely a counting observation. It says that an observed state can conceal an exponentially large family of compatible histories. If a stream of values is used as a keystream, then seeing its suffix from time $n$ onward does not identify which of these histories produced it.

## Splitting one state into two pasts

To reverse one step, solve

$$
4x(1-x)=y.
$$

The quadratic formula gives two solutions:

$$
L(y)=\frac{1-\sqrt{1-y}}{2},
\qquad
U(y)=\frac{1+\sqrt{1-y}}{2}.
$$

Call these the lower and upper inverse branches. Direct substitution proves

$$
f(L(y))=y,
\qquad
f(U(y))=y
$$

whenever $y\leq 1$. For an interior target $0<y<1$, both preimages are again interior: $L(y)$ lies strictly between $0$ and $1/2$, while $U(y)$ lies strictly between $1/2$ and $1$. The branches are mirror images, since $U(y)=1-L(y)$.

This separation across the midpoint is the first crucial fact. A lower choice and an upper choice can never accidentally produce the same seed. It also gives each backward step a natural bit: write $0$ for the lower branch and $1$ for the upper branch.

Now reverse again. Each of the two preimages has two preimages of its own, giving four candidates two steps back. Continue for $n$ levels and a full binary tree appears. A bit word

$$
b=(b_0,b_1,\ldots,b_{n-1})\in\{0,1\}^n
$$

specifies a seed by nested branch selection. Starting at $y$, apply the branch encoded by the last bit, then the preceding bit, until the first bit is applied outermost. In symbols, if $B_0=L$ and $B_1=U$, define

$$
D_b(y)=B_{b_0}\bigl(B_{b_1}(\cdots B_{b_{n-1}}(y)\cdots)\bigr).
$$

Every intermediate value remains in $(0,1)$, so the construction never leaves the domain of interest.

## Why the paths never merge

A binary tree on paper does not automatically guarantee distinct leaves. Two different paths might conceivably meet. Here they do not.

Suppose two words first differ in their outermost bit. One decoded seed lies below $1/2$ and the other above $1/2$, so they are distinct. If their first bits agree, both use the same branch. Each branch is injective on $(0,1)$: equal outputs under $L$ force equal inputs, and the same is true for $U$. We may therefore cancel the common outer branch and compare the remaining words. Repeating this argument eventually reaches the first differing bit and separates the seeds.

This proves the **Binary Preimage Tree Theorem**: for every $y\in(0,1)$ and every $n\geq 0$, the decoding map $b\mapsto D_b(y)$ is injective, its values lie in $(0,1)$, and

$$
f^{n}(D_b(y))=y
$$

for every $b\in\{0,1\}^n$. Here $f^n$ means $n$ successive applications of $f$. Since there are exactly $2^n$ binary words of length $n$, the construction yields exactly $2^n$ indexed, pairwise distinct seeds. The theorem guarantees at least this many points in the full $n$-step fiber; proving that these are all of them is a further classification problem.

The forward identity follows by peeling the construction apart. The outermost branch disappears after one application of $f$, because $f\circ L$ and $f\circ U$ are both the identity on interior targets. Repeating $n$ times removes all $n$ branches and leaves $y$.

For example, take $y=3/4$. Its one-step preimages are $1/4$ and $3/4$. At depth two, the words $00$, $01$, $10$, and $11$ select four distinct interior seeds. Applying $f$ twice to each returns $3/4$. The symmetry is visible, but the theorem does not depend on this specially convenient target.

## Identical futures after different pasts

The strongest cryptographic message comes from what happens after the collision. Once every decoded seed reaches $y$ at time $n$, determinism forces all later states to agree. For every $k\geq 0$,

$$
f^{n+k}(D_b(y))=f^k(y).
$$

This is the **Common-Suffix Theorem**. The $2^n$ distinct seeds do not merely share one observed value; from time $n$ onward, they share their entire infinite future orbit.

For positive $n$, this immediately gives a simpler corollary: every interior target has at least two distinct interior seeds that reach it after $n$ steps. Choose the all-zero word and the all-one word. Their decoded seeds are different, yet their trajectories meet at the same target and remain together forever.

The result exposes an unusual kind of ambiguity. Chaos is often described through divergence: nearby pasts lead to noticeably different futures. Here the inverse viewpoint reveals convergence of descriptions: one future suffix is compatible with exponentially many explicitly indexed pasts. Both facts belong to the same nonlinear map.

## What this means—and what it does not

In cryptography, unpredictability is not a synonym for security. A practical cipher must be judged under a specified attacker model, finite arithmetic, key distribution, output function, and security definition. The exact-real theorem does not by itself break every logistic-map construction, nor does it establish statistical indistinguishability or computational hardness.

It does identify a structural obstruction to seed recovery from a delayed orbit. If an observer first sees the exact state $y$ at time $n$ and then sees its future, that information cannot distinguish among the constructed $2^n$ histories. The future suffix contains no clue about which inverse-branch bits were chosen, because all of those choices have already collapsed into the common state $y$.

The phrase “exact state” matters. Computers use finite precision. Rounding can merge mathematically distinct branches early, create short cycles, or push states onto exceptional values such as $0$ and $1$. Those effects may reduce, enlarge, or otherwise reshape the candidate set. They must be analyzed separately rather than inferred from real-number dynamics.

The restriction $0<y<1$ matters as well. At the boundary, the critical point and endpoint behavior create collisions. For instance, $f(1/2)=1$, but the two inverse formulas coincide at $y=1$. The clean doubling argument therefore belongs to the open interval; boundary fibers need their own classification.

## A bridge between dynamics and information

The binary inverse tree transforms a familiar picture of chaos into a precise information statement. Each backward step contributes one unresolved binary choice. After $n$ steps there are $n$ hidden branch decisions and $2^n$ compatible seeds. Forward evolution erases those distinctions at the target, after which every future observation is identical.

This perspective suggests several next steps. One can seek an exact classification of the whole fiber, connect the branches to angle halving through the identity $x=\sin^2\theta$, and quantify how fixed-point rounding prunes the tree. One can also combine inverse-branch derivatives with probability to recover the map’s invariant density and its characteristic exponential rate.

The broader lesson is durable. A chaotic formula may amplify small differences, but a cryptographic system must control what information its outputs reveal about both future and past. For the parameter-four logistic map, the future is deterministic—and the past branches like a perfect binary riddle.

## Reading the hidden bits from a backward path

There is another way to see why the construction is exact. Imagine that someone hands you one of the decoded seeds but hides its word. Compare the seed with $1/2$. If it is below the midpoint, the first bit was $0$; if it is above, the first bit was $1$. Now apply $f$ once. The result is the seed obtained from the remaining suffix of the word, so its side of $1/2$ reveals the second bit. Repeating this procedure reads the entire branch history until the orbit reaches $y$.

Thus the bit labels are not arbitrary decorations. Within the constructed family they are dynamically recoverable from the finite trajectory before the collision. Before time $n$, different histories retain their identities; at time $n$, all arrive at the target; afterward, those identities are absent from the state. This clean before-and-after boundary is what makes the common-suffix result so striking.

It also clarifies the exponential count. At each backward level there are exactly two separated choices in the construction, and no later choice can erase an earlier distinction among seeds. Multiplying two choices across $n$ levels gives $2\cdot2\cdots2=2^n$. A depth of $10$ indexes $1{,}024$ seeds; a depth of $20$ indexes $1{,}048{,}576$. The growth comes not from numerical experimentation but from the combinatorics of independent branch decisions.

## A small formula with two personalities

The same parabola therefore tells two stories. Viewed forward, it folds the interval around $1/2$: the points $x$ and $1-x$ have the same image. Repeated stretching and folding supports chaotic behavior. Viewed backward, every interior state unfolds into a lower and upper alternative. The forward fold is precisely what creates the backward bit.

That duality has analogues far beyond this example. Whenever a deterministic transformation is many-to-one, observations can destroy information about history. Cryptography, data compression, control, and state estimation all confront versions of the same question: which distinctions survive the observable dynamics? The logistic map offers an unusually clean laboratory because its alternatives can be written with a square root and counted without approximation.

The result does not say that history is unknowable in every setting. If one observes the pre-collision trajectory, the midpoint test reveals branch bits. If one has outside constraints on valid seeds, many candidates may be rejected. The precise claim is narrower and stronger: the exact suffix beginning at the common target is identical for every member of the binary family. No analysis of that suffix alone can distinguish their earlier branch choices.

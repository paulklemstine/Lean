# The Equation That Refuses a Formula

## How a single missing degree rules out every closed form for Airy's equation

There is a moment in every calculus course when a student asks the honest question: *why can't we just solve it?* The differential equations that appear in textbooks always seem to yield to a trick — separate the variables, guess an exponential, integrate a factor. And then, somewhere past the textbook, the tricks stop working. Not because we are not clever enough, but because there is nothing to find.

The cleanest example of this is one of the shortest differential equations ever written down:

$$y'' = x\,y.$$

This is **Airy's equation**, introduced in 1838 by the British Astronomer Royal George Biddell Airy while studying the intensity of light near a caustic — the bright curve you see at the bottom of a coffee cup when sunlight reflects off its inner wall. It is the simplest equation whose behaviour changes character as you move along the axis: for $x < 0$ its solutions oscillate like a sine wave whose wavelength slowly shrinks, and for $x > 0$ they either blow up exponentially or decay to zero. It is the universal model of a *turning point*, the boundary between wave-like and exponential behaviour, and for exactly this reason it shows up wherever a wave meets a barrier: in quantum tunnelling, in the rainbow, in radio propagation over the horizon, in the WKB approximation, in random matrix theory at the edge of the spectrum.

And it has no formula.

Not "no formula anyone has found yet". No formula, provably, in a precise and demonstrable sense. This article is about how one proves such a thing — and about the surprisingly elementary bookkeeping that does the job.

---

## What counts as a formula?

Before you can prove that something has no closed form, you have to say what a closed form *is*. The honest answer is that a closed form is whatever you can write with the symbols on a calculator keyboard: the variable $x$, numerical constants, addition, multiplication, division, the exponential $e^{u}$, and the logarithm $\log u$. Everything familiar is built from these. Powers are $x^{a} = e^{a\log x}$. Roots are powers. Trigonometric functions are exponentials in disguise. Hyperbolic functions likewise.

Call a function built from these ingredients an **exponential–logarithmic function**, or EML function for short. Formally, an EML *expression* is any finite tree generated by the rules

$$X, \quad c\ (c \in \mathbb{R}), \quad A + B, \quad A \cdot B, \quad A^{-1}, \quad \exp A, \quad \log A,$$

and an EML *function* is what you get when you interpret such a tree as a real function of $x$.

Two things immediately need care. First, division and logarithm are not defined everywhere; a formula like $\log(x-1)$ makes sense only where its argument is nonzero. So each expression comes with a **regularity locus**: the set of points where every inversion and every logarithm inside it has nonzero argument. Second, we would like to know that this class is closed under differentiation — that the derivative of a formula is again a formula.

It is, and the proof is the algorithm you learned in school, promoted to a theorem. Define a purely symbolic derivative on expression trees by the usual rules,

$$D(X) = 1,\qquad D(c) = 0,\qquad D(A+B) = DA + DB,\qquad D(A\cdot B) = DA\cdot B + A \cdot DB,$$
$$D(A^{-1}) = -\,DA \cdot A^{-1} \cdot A^{-1}, \qquad D(\exp A) = DA\cdot \exp A, \qquad D(\log A) = DA \cdot A^{-1}.$$

Notice that the right-hand sides are again EML expressions: nothing new is ever introduced. The **correctness of symbolic differentiation** then says exactly what one hopes:

> **Theorem (Symbolic differentiation is analytic differentiation).** *At every point of its regularity locus, the function defined by an EML expression $E$ is differentiable, and its derivative is the function defined by the expression $DE$. Moreover, every regular point of $E$ is a regular point of $DE$.*

The second sentence is the one that makes induction possible: you can differentiate as often as you like without ever leaving the domain you started on, and without ever leaving the class of formulas.

This is the mundane-sounding foundation on which the impossibility proof rests. Because we have a *syntax*, we can reason about *all possible formulas at once* — something you cannot do if "closed form" remains an informal notion.

---

## The one equation that always has a solution

Before the bad news, the good news. First-order linear equations always have closed-form solutions, and the EML framework proves it in one line.

> **Theorem (First-order solution and uniqueness).** *Let $F$ be an EML expression that is regular everywhere. Then $y = e^{F}$ satisfies $y' = F'\,y$. Conversely, every solution of $y' = F'\,y$ on the whole line is $y = K e^{F}$ for a constant $K$.*

The first half is the chain rule. The second half is the classical trick: if $y' = F' y$ then $\bigl(y e^{-F}\bigr)' = y'e^{-F} - F'ye^{-F} = 0$, so $y e^{-F}$ is constant.

So an equation $y' = c(x)\,y$ has an EML solution as soon as $c$ has an EML antiderivative. The entire difficulty of *first*-order theory is pushed into the question of integrating in closed form. Second-order theory is where genuinely new obstructions appear.

---

## Turning the linear equation into a nonlinear one

Here is the crucial move, and it is 300 years old. Suppose $y$ solves $y'' = r y$ and never vanishes. Its **logarithmic derivative** $u = y'/y$ then satisfies

$$u' + u^{2} = r,$$

the **Riccati equation** associated with $y'' = ry$; and conversely, if $u$ solves the Riccati equation and $y' = u\,y$, then $y'' = ry$. (Both directions are a two-line computation: $u' = y''/y - (y'/y)^2 = r - u^2$.)

This looks like a step backwards — we have traded a linear equation for a nonlinear one. But it is exactly the right trade, because *the shape of the answer becomes visible*. A solution of the shape $y = e^{F}$ is the same thing as a solution $u = F'$ of the Riccati equation; and a solution that is a rational function corresponds to a Riccati solution of a specific rational shape. The nonlinear equation is a mirror in which the closed-formness of $y$ becomes an algebraic property of $u$.

This is the engine of **Kovacic's algorithm**, published in 1986, which decides in finite time whether a second-order linear equation $y'' = r y$ with rational $r$ has a solution expressible in closed form. Kovacic's insight is that if a closed-form solution exists at all, then one of exactly three highly restricted things must happen:

1. **Case 1.** Some solution has a *rational* logarithmic derivative — i.e. $y = e^{\int u}$ with $u \in \mathbb{C}(x)$.
2. **Case 2.** Some solution has a logarithmic derivative that is algebraic of degree 2 over the rationals — equivalently, the *product* of two solutions is a rational function.
3. **Case 3.** All solutions are algebraic over $\mathbb{C}(x)$, with a finite list of possible degrees ($4$, $6$ or $12$).

Each case, when it happens, leaves a rational fingerprint. So to show that no closed form exists, one shows that no such fingerprint can be found. Let us do exactly that for Airy, for the first two cases.

---

## Case 1: parity kills it

For Airy, $r = x$, and the Riccati equation is

$$u' + u^{2} = x.$$

Suppose $u = P/Q$ is a rational solution, with $P$ and $Q$ polynomials, $Q \neq 0$. Multiply through by $Q^{2}$ and use the quotient rule:

$$P'Q - PQ' + P^{2} = x\,Q^{2}.$$

Now simply count degrees. Write $p = \deg P$ and $q = \deg Q$. The **Wronskian numerator** $W = P'Q - PQ'$ always satisfies $\deg W < p + q$ — differentiation lowers degree, and the two top terms in the subtraction may or may not cancel, but neither can raise the degree. So:

* If $q \le p$, then $\deg P^{2} = 2p \ge p+q > \deg W$, so the left-hand side has degree exactly $2p$: an **even** number. The right-hand side has degree $1 + 2q$: an **odd** number. Contradiction.
* If $p < q$, then both $W$ and $P^{2}$ have degree strictly below $2q$, so the left-hand side has degree $< 2q < 1 + 2q$, while the right-hand side has degree exactly $1+2q$. Contradiction again.

That's the whole proof. The general statement it proves is worth recording:

> **Theorem (Odd-degree Riccati obstruction).** *If $r$ is a polynomial of odd degree, then the Riccati equation $u' + u^{2} = r$ has no solution in the field of rational functions. Equivalently, no solution of $y'' = r y$ has a rational logarithmic derivative.*

Notice how little was used: only that $\deg(P'Q - PQ') < \deg P + \deg Q$ and that $2p$ and $2q$ are even. The obstruction is *parity*. There is nothing subtle about $x$ except that its degree is $1$.

The parity hypothesis is not an artefact. For $r = x^{2}+1$, of even degree $2$, the Riccati equation $u' + u^{2} = x^{2}+1$ has the polynomial solution $u = x$ (indeed $1 + x^2 = x^2 + 1$), and the corresponding closed-form solution of $y'' = (x^{2}+1)y$ is the perfectly good EML function

$$y = e^{x^{2}/2},$$

whose second derivative is $(x^{2}+1)e^{x^2/2}$. So the theorem is sharp: it is the *odd* degree of $x$, and nothing else, that dooms Airy.

There is also a companion fact that makes Kovacic's search finite. If a Riccati solution happens to be a *polynomial* $u$, then $u'$ has smaller degree than $u^{2}$, so the top of $r$ is dictated entirely by $u^2$:

> **Theorem (Degree determination).** *If a nonzero polynomial $u$ satisfies $u' + u^{2} = r$, then $\deg r = 2\deg u$ and the leading coefficient of $r$ is the square of the leading coefficient of $u$.*

This pins the candidate down to a finite search — the algorithmic heart of Case 1. For $r = x$ it is instantly fatal: $\deg r = 1$ is not twice anything.

Chaining these algebraic facts back through the Riccati correspondence gives the analytic statement one actually wants:

> **Theorem (Airy has no exponential-type closed-form solution).** *There is no everywhere-regular EML expression $F$ whose derivative is a rational function such that $y = e^{F}$ solves $y'' = x y$.*

That is Case 1, closed.

## Case 2: multiply two solutions together

Case 2 is subtler, because the fingerprint is not a single solution but a *product*. Here is the beautiful classical identity that makes it tractable. Let $y_1, y_2$ be any two solutions of $y'' = ry$ and set $v = y_1 y_2$. Differentiating three times and repeatedly substituting $y_i'' = r y_i$ gives

$$v' = y_1'y_2 + y_1y_2', \qquad v'' = 2y_1'y_2' + 2r\,v, \qquad \boxed{\,v''' = 4r\,v' + 2r'\,v\,}.$$

This third-order equation is the **second symmetric power** of the original one: its solution space is spanned by the products of pairs of solutions. If Case 2 holds for $y''=ry$, then it has a rational solution. So: rule out rational solutions of the symmetric square, and Case 2 dies.

For Airy, $r = x$ and $r' = 1$, and the symmetric square is

$$v''' = 4x\,v' + 2v.$$

**No polynomial works.** Suppose $v \neq 0$ is a polynomial of degree $n$ with leading coefficient $\ell$. On the right, $4x v'$ contributes $4n\ell x^{n}$ and $2v$ contributes $2\ell x^{n}$: the coefficient of $x^{n}$ on the right is $(4n+2)\ell$, which is never zero, since $4n + 2 > 0$ and $\ell \neq 0$. On the left, $v'''$ has degree at most $n - 3 < n$, so its $x^{n}$ coefficient is $0$. Contradiction. The number $4n+2$ never vanishes for a very simple reason: it is *even but not divisible by 4*, so it can never be cancelled — and, crucially, it is nonzero in any field of characteristic zero.

**No rational function works either,** and the same idea survives clearing denominators. Write $v = P/Q$. Repeated application of the quotient rule gives successive numerators
$$v' = \frac{W}{Q^{2}},\qquad v'' = \frac{B}{Q^{3}},\qquad v''' = \frac{Z}{Q^{4}},$$
where $W = P'Q - PQ'$, $B = W'Q - 2WQ'$, $Z = B'Q - 3BQ'$. Substituting into $v''' = 4xv' + 2v$ and multiplying by $Q^{4}$ turns the equation into the polynomial identity

$$Z \;=\; \bigl(4x\,W + 2PQ\bigr)\,Q^{2}.$$

Now the same two-line accounting as before. Each of the three quotient-rule steps drops the degree: $\deg W < p+q$, then $\deg B < p + 2q$, then

$$\deg Z < p + 3q.$$

Meanwhile the right-hand side has degree *exactly* $p + 3q$. To see this, look at the coefficient of $x^{p+q}$ in the core $4xW + 2PQ$. The top coefficient of the Wronskian numerator is a small gem in its own right:

$$[x^{p+q-1}]\,(P'Q - PQ') = (p - q)\,\ell_P\,\ell_Q,$$

where $\ell_P, \ell_Q$ are the leading coefficients. Multiplying by $4x$ shifts this to degree $p+q$ with coefficient $4(p-q)\ell_P\ell_Q$, and $2PQ$ contributes $2\ell_P\ell_Q$ in the same degree. Together:

$$[x^{p+q}]\bigl(4xW + 2PQ\bigr) = \bigl(4(p-q)+2\bigr)\,\ell_P\,\ell_Q .$$

And $4(p-q) + 2$ is never zero, because $2$ is not divisible by $4$ — the *same* arithmetic miracle as in the polynomial case, now with $p-q$ in place of $n$ (and $p - q$ may be negative; it makes no difference). Multiplying by $Q^{2}$ moves this to degree $p + 3q$ with coefficient $(4(p-q)+2)\ell_P\ell_Q\ell_Q^{2} \neq 0$.

So the right-hand side has a nonzero coefficient in a degree where the left-hand side is provably zero. Contradiction:

> **Theorem (No rational solution of the symmetric square).** *Over any field of characteristic zero, the equation $v''' = 4x v' + 2v$ has no nonzero rational solution — in particular no nonzero polynomial solution.*

Feeding this back through the analytic identity yields the statement with real teeth:

> **Theorem (Products of Airy solutions are never rational).** *If $y_1$ and $y_2$ solve $y'' = xy$ and their product equals a rational function $P/Q$ with $P \neq 0$ and $Q$ nowhere vanishing, then a contradiction follows. No product of two solutions of Airy's equation is a nonzero rational function.*

Case 2 is closed.

## The shape of the argument

Step back and look at what actually did the work. Two theorems from analysis: the chain rule (in the guise of correctness of symbolic differentiation) and the quotient rule. One change of variables: the logarithmic derivative. And then, twice over, an entirely finite computation about the top coefficient of a polynomial.

Both fatal computations were arithmetic facts about small integers:

* Case 1 dies because $2p$ is even and $\deg(xQ^2) = 2q+1$ is odd.
* Case 2 dies because $4(p - q) + 2$ is never zero for integers $p, q$.

The transcendence of the Airy function — the fact that no combination of exponentials, logarithms, powers and arithmetic will ever reproduce it — reduces, in the end, to parity and divisibility by 4. That is the characteristic flavour of differential Galois theory: an analytic impossibility, refracted through a change of variables, becomes a statement that a certain integer is not zero.

Two other results round out the picture and are proved by the same degree count. First, $y'' = ry$ has *no* nonzero rational solution whenever $r \neq 0$ — clearing denominators turns it into $W'Q - 2WQ' = rPQ^{2}$, whose left side has degree $< p + 2q$ and whose right side has degree $\deg r + p + 2q$. In particular Airy has no rational, and no polynomial, solution at all. Second, the symbolic-differentiation machinery guarantees that these algebraic statements really do constrain honest real functions, not just formal symbols: the analytic versions of all of the above hold for genuine differentiable functions on the line.

---

## Why one should care

The practical payoff is that we now know what to do instead. Because Airy's equation has no closed form, the Airy functions $\operatorname{Ai}$ and $\operatorname{Bi}$ are *new* functions, to be studied on their own terms: through the power series
$$y(x) = \sum_{n\ge 0} a_n x^{n}, \qquad a_{n+3} = \frac{a_n}{(n+3)(n+2)},$$
which converges everywhere; through the integral representation $\operatorname{Ai}(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\infty}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{t^3}{3}+xt\right)dt$; and through the asymptotics $\operatorname{Ai}(x)\sim \frac{e^{-\frac{2}{3}x^{3/2}}}{2\sqrt{\pi}\,x^{1/4}}$ as $x \to +\infty$. Every one of these is a legitimate way to compute; none is a formula in the elementary sense; and the impossibility theorem is precisely what tells us not to keep looking for one.

That is worth saying plainly, because it is easy to mistake a hard problem for an unsolved one. The Airy function has been computed to arbitrary precision for a century and a half. What it does not have — what it *cannot* have — is a name spelled out in exponentials and logarithms. And the reason is that a certain integer, $4(p-q)+2$, refuses to be zero.

There is something bracing about that. The world's most-used turning-point function is inaccessible to elementary calculus, and the barrier is not a wall of technical difficulty but a fact about even numbers. Impossibility, done right, is not a dead end. It is the sharpest kind of understanding: you know not only that you have not found the formula, but why nobody ever will.

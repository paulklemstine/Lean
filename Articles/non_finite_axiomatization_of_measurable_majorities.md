# The Shortest Way to Break a Majority

## A number hidden inside every voting system

Imagine a committee that has to choose between several options — call them
*social states*. The committee has rules: in any head-to-head comparison, the
group's collective preference is determined by who holds the majority (with ties
allowed). For decades, social-choice theorists have known a troubling fact: these
majority rules can be *incoherent*. You can sometimes line up a sequence of
comparisons that all point "forward," yet circle back to where you started — the
collective preference chases its own tail. The classic example is the Condorcet
paradox, where the group prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A.

This article is about a single number that measures *how hard it is* to trip a
voting system into that kind of contradiction. We call it the **incoherence
index**. And the punchline is surprising: this index, which looks like an ad-hoc
count of "how long a contradiction has to be," turns out to be a clean,
classical piece of arithmetic in disguise — the *order of a number in modular
arithmetic*. Once you see that, a whole spectrum of behavior snaps into focus.

## Frames, atoms, and balanced sequences

Let us set up the playground precisely, because the precision is where the beauty
lives. Fix a number $n$ of social states and work in *modular arithmetic mod
$n$*, written $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the numbers $0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1$ where
addition wraps around (so in $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, $3 + 4 = 2$).

A **frame** is just a finite set $F$ of residues drawn from
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Think of each residue in $F$ as an allowed "shift" — an
*atom* of collective preference, a single permissible step the majority can take
around the cycle of states.

A **perfectly balanced sequence** for $F$ is a non-empty list of atoms (with
repetition allowed) that adds up to exactly $0$ modulo $n$. Concretely, if
$x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k$ are all atoms of $F$ and
$$x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_k \equiv 0 \pmod{n},$$
then this list is a balanced sequence of length $k$. A balanced sequence is a
*coherence violation*: it is a chain of allowed majority steps that returns
exactly to its starting point, the formal fingerprint of a paradox.

The **incoherence index** of a frame is the length of its *shortest* balanced
sequence:
$$\mathrm{incoherenceIndex}(F) = \min\{\, k : \text{there is a balanced sequence of length } k \,\}.$$
If no balanced sequence exists at all, the frame is **coherent** and we set the
index to $0$. The index is, in words, *the length of the shortest contradiction*.
A large index means the system is robust — you have to chain together very many
majority steps before you can manufacture a paradox. A small index means the
system is fragile.

One more notion. A frame is **maximal** when its atoms generate the entire
decision space — formally, when the subgroup generated by $F$ inside
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is everything. Maximal frames are the "fully expressive"
voting systems: every social state is reachable by combining allowed steps. These
are the interesting ones, because a non-maximal frame is secretly living on a
smaller stage.

## The main discovery: the index is an order

Here is the first and central result. Take the simplest possible frame: a single
atom $a$, so $F = \{a\}$. What is its incoherence index?

A balanced sequence over $\{a\}$ can only be "$a$ repeated $m$ times," and its sum
is $m \cdot a$. This vanishes mod $n$ exactly when $m$ is a multiple of the
**additive order** of $a$ — the smallest positive number of copies of $a$ you must
add to land back on $0$. The shortest balanced sequence therefore has length
equal to that order. In symbols:

> **Order Formula.** For any single atom $a$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$,
> $$\mathrm{incoherenceIndex}(\{a\}) = \mathrm{addOrderOf}(a).$$

This is deceptively powerful. The additive order of $a$ in
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has a classical closed form: it equals $n / \gcd(n, a)$.
So the shortest contradiction available to a single-atom voting system is
governed entirely by a greatest common divisor.

The most famous special case is the *unit* atom $a = 1$. Its order is $n$, so:

> **Unit frame.** $\mathrm{incoherenceIndex}(\{1\}) = n$.

The single-generator frame $\{1\}$ is as robust as a frame on $n$ states can be:
its shortest paradox uses all $n$ states. This single fact is the engine behind a
deeper theorem — that the coherence criterion *cannot be captured by any fixed
finite test* — but more on that shortly.

## Adding voices can only shorten the contradiction

What happens when we enrich a frame with more atoms? Intuitively, more allowed
steps means more ways to build a short loop. The arithmetic confirms the
intuition exactly:

> **Saturation Law.** If $F \subseteq G$ and $F$ already admits some balanced
> sequence, then
> $$\mathrm{incoherenceIndex}(G) \le \mathrm{incoherenceIndex}(F).$$

Adding atoms is *monotone-decreasing* on the index. Every balanced sequence of
the smaller frame is still a balanced sequence of the larger one, so the shortest
contradiction can only get shorter, never longer. (The hypothesis that $F$ is
already incoherent matters: a coherent frame has index $0$ by convention, and
$0$ is not an upper bound for anything.)

A vivid illustration: the frame $\{1\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ has index
$4$. But add the atom $3$ to get $\{1, 3\}$ — still a maximal frame — and the
index collapses to $2$, because $1 + 3 = 4 \equiv 0$ gives a balanced sequence of
length just $2$. Saturating a voting system with extra majority options makes it
*easier* to break, not harder.

## What values can the index take? The spectrum

Now the natural question: which numbers actually occur as incoherence indices?
This is the *spectrum* problem, and the answer reveals a sharp dichotomy between
single-atom and multi-atom frames.

For single atoms, the order formula chains it to a divisibility law. Lagrange's
theorem says the order of any element divides the group size, so a singleton
frame in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ can only have an index that *divides* $n$.
Conversely, every divisor is achievable:

> **Divisor realization.** On a fixed $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, every divisor
> $d \ge 2$ of $n$ is the incoherence index of some single-atom frame.

To realize a divisor $d$ of $n$, pick the atom $a = n/d$; its order is exactly
$d$. So the single-atom spectrum on $n$ states is precisely the set of divisors
of $n$ that are at least $2$.

But singletons are not the whole story. If we allow ourselves *more than one*
atom, we are no longer chained to the divisor lattice. Across all possible
choices of $n$ and $F$, every integer is attainable:

> **Full realization.** For every integer $d \ge 2$, there is a *maximal* frame
> whose incoherence index is exactly $d$.

Not just the divisors of some fixed $n$, and not just the even numbers — *every*
$d \ge 2$, realized by a fully expressive maximal frame. The maximality is the
subtle part: it says you do not have to sacrifice expressiveness to tune the
robustness. You can build a voting system that uses its whole decision space yet
whose shortest contradiction has any prescribed length you like.

## Escaping the divisor lattice: a small, decisive example

The cleanest proof that multi-atom frames genuinely escape the divisor world is a
single hand-checkable case. Consider $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$, whose only
divisors are $1$ and $5$ (since $5$ is prime). A single-atom frame here can
*only* have index $5$ (for any nonzero atom). So $3$ is impossible for a
singleton. Yet:

> **The escape.** The two-atom frame $\{1, 2\}$ in $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ has
> incoherence index exactly $3$.

Why $3$? The sequence $[1, 2, 2]$ sums to $5 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, a balanced
sequence of length $3$. And nothing shorter works: a length-$1$ sequence would
need an atom equal to $0$ (there is none), and a length-$2$ sequence would need
two atoms from $\{1, 2\}$ summing to a multiple of $5$ — but the possible sums are
$1{+}1{=}2$, $1{+}2{=}3$, $2{+}2{=}4$, none divisible by $5$. So $3$ is genuinely
the shortest, and $3$ does not divide $5$. Two generators have unlocked a value
that one generator could never reach.

This tiny example is the seed of the general phenomenon. Singletons see only
divisors; the moment you add a second atom, the gaps between divisors begin to
fill in.

## Why this matters beyond the puzzle

The incoherence index turns a qualitative worry — "can this voting system
contradict itself?" — into a quantitative invariant — "*how long* must a
contradiction be?" — and then identifies that invariant with one of the oldest
quantities in number theory, the order of an element. That identification is more
than elegant bookkeeping. It means the entire toolkit of additive combinatorics
becomes available to social choice.

In fact, a "perfectly balanced sequence" is precisely a *zero-sum sequence*, the
central object of a rich branch of combinatorics organized around the
**Davenport constant** — the length of the longest sequence with *no* nonempty
zero-sum subsequence. The incoherence index is the dual, "shortest zero-sum,"
quantity, and the order formula is exactly its rank-one case. Robustness of
majority rule and the structure of zero-sum sequences are the same mathematics
wearing two different costumes.

There is also a logical payoff. Because the unit frame $\{1\}$ in
$\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has index $n$, the indices grow without bound as $n$
grows. No fixed finite test of the form "check for contradictions of length at
most $B$" can ever certify coherence: for any bound $B$, the frame $\{1\}$ in
$\mathbb{Z}/(B{+}1)\mathbb{Z}$ passes the test (its shortest contradiction has
length $B+1 > B$) yet is genuinely incoherent. The coherence of measurable
majorities is, in this precise sense, *not finitely axiomatizable*. The index is
the quantitative engine that drives this impossibility.

## The shape of the answer

Step back and admire the landscape the index reveals:

- **One atom** is rigid. Its index is the order $n/\gcd(n,a)$, so its
  achievable values are exactly the divisors of $n$ (that are at least $2$).
- **More atoms** are flexible. The index can only drop as atoms are added
  (the saturation law), and across all frames every integer $d \ge 2$ is hit by
  a maximal frame — including non-divisors like the $3$ that $\{1,2\}$ realizes
  in $\mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$.
- **The extremes** are pinned: the maximum on $n$ states is $n$, achieved by the
  expressive unit frame; the minimum nontrivial value is $2$, a single
  cancelling pair.

A voting system's fragility, it turns out, is not a vague sociological quality
but a precise arithmetic one. The shortest way to break a majority is the order
of a number — and once you know the number, you know exactly how robust the
majority is.

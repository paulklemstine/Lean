# Carrying in Two Bases at Once: Kummer's Theorem Gets a $q$

## A theorem about carrying

In 1852 Ernst Kummer noticed something that still feels like a magic trick. Take a binomial coefficient — say $\binom{6}{3} = 20$ — and ask how many times a prime divides it. For $p = 2$: $20 = 2^2 \cdot 5$, so the answer is $2$. Now do something apparently unrelated: add $3$ and $3$ in binary, $11_2 + 11_2 = 110_2$, and count the carries. There are two.

That is not a coincidence. Kummer's theorem says that for every prime $p$ and every $k \le n$,
$$v_p\!\left(\binom{n}{k}\right) = \#\{\text{carries when adding } k \text{ and } n-k \text{ in base } p\},$$
where $v_p(m)$ denotes the exponent of $p$ in $m$. Arithmetic depth on the left, schoolroom bookkeeping on the right.

Kummer's theorem has a famous shadow. Colour an entry of Pascal's triangle black when it is *not* divisible by $p$; the black entries form a Sierpiński gasket. Its companion, Lucas's theorem, explains why: modulo $p$, a binomial coefficient is the product of the binomial coefficients of the base-$p$ digits,
$$\binom{n}{k} \equiv \prod_i \binom{n_i}{k_i} \pmod p .$$
Pascal's triangle, seen modulo a prime, is a self-similar fractal generated by a single $p \times p$ tile.

This article is about what happens to all of this when you deform the integers.

## Deforming the integers

Fix an integer $q \ge 2$ and replace each counting number $m$ by the polynomial-in-$q$ value
$$[m]_q = 1 + q + q^2 + \cdots + q^{m-1} = \frac{q^m - 1}{q - 1}.$$
So $[1]_q = 1$, $[2]_q = 1 + q$, $[3]_q = 1 + q + q^2$. Setting $q = 1$ recovers $[m]_1 = m$; the parameter $q$ is a dial that bends ordinary counting into something else. Multiply these together to get the *$q$-factorial* $[n]_q! = [1]_q [2]_q \cdots [n]_q$, and form the *Gaussian binomial coefficient*
$$\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} = \frac{[n]_q!}{[k]_q!\,[n-k]_q!}.$$

Two things make this object more than a formal curiosity.

First, it is an honest integer: the division is exact. That is the content of the identity
$$[k]_q!\;[n-k]_q!\;\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} = [n]_q!,$$
which one proves not by clearing denominators but by exhibiting the Gaussian coefficient as the solution of a *deformed* Pascal recursion,
$$\binom{n+1}{k+1}_{\!q} = \binom{n}{k}_{\!q} + q^{\,k+1}\binom{n}{k+1}_{\!q}, \qquad \binom{n}{0}_{\!q} = 1 .$$
Everything on the right is a non-negative integer, so everything on the left is too.

Second — and this is why the Gaussian coefficients appear all over mathematics — when $q$ is a prime power, $\binom{n}{k}_q$ counts something geometric: it is exactly the number of $k$-dimensional subspaces of an $n$-dimensional vector space over the field with $q$ elements. For instance $\binom{6}{3}_2 = 1395$: a six-dimensional space over $\mathbb{F}_2$ has precisely $1395$ three-dimensional subspaces. Ordinary binomial coefficients count subsets; Gaussian ones count subspaces, and the passage from sets to spaces is exactly the passage from $q = 1$ to $q \ge 2$.

So the natural question is: **is there a Kummer theorem for $\binom{n}{k}_q$?** What controls the exact power of a prime $\ell$ dividing the number of subspaces of a finite vector space?

## A first guess, and what breaks it

Here is the naive guess. The prime $\ell$ divides $[m]_q = (q^m-1)/(q-1)$ roughly when $q^m \equiv 1 \pmod \ell$, that is, when $m$ is a multiple of $d = \mathrm{ord}_\ell(q)$, the multiplicative order of $q$ modulo $\ell$. So the $q$-integers behave like the ordinary integers, but with the "divisible by $\ell$" positions spaced out by $d$ instead of by $\ell$. One therefore guesses: the valuation is the number of carries when $k$ and $n-k$ are added in base $d$, corrected by the size of $v_\ell(q^d-1)$.

Try it on the sharpest available test case. Take $q = 2$, $\ell = 5$, $n = 6$, $k = 3$. Then $\binom{6}{3}_2 = 1395 = 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 31$, and the order of $2$ modulo $5$ is $d = 4$. Adding $3 + 3$ in base $4$ produces $12_4$, exactly one carry, and $v_5(2^4 - 1) = v_5(15) = 1$. The prediction is $1 \cdot 1 = 1$, and indeed $v_5(1395) = 1$.

But push a little further and the naive picture collapses. Keep $q = 2$ and $\ell = 5$, so $d = 4$ and the offset is $e = v_5([4]_2) = v_5(15) = 1$, and take $n = 16$, $k = 1$. Adding $1$ and $15$ in base $4$ is $1_4 + 33_4 = 100_4$: *two* carries, so a pure base-$4$ carry count predicts valuation $2 \cdot 1 = 2$. But $\binom{16}{1}_2 = [16]_2 = 65535 = 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 17 \cdot 257$, whose $5$-adic valuation is $1$. The second carry contributed nothing.

The reason is that only the *first* carry happens in base $4$. Beyond that bottom "anomalous" digit, the arithmetic reverts to base $\ell$: the higher-order bookkeeping is carried out on the quotients $\lfloor n/d \rfloor = 4$ and $\lfloor k/d\rfloor = 0$, in base $5$, where nothing carries at all.

That is the punchline of the whole story: **the deformation changes only one digit.**

## The theorem

Fix $q \ge 2$ and a prime $\ell$ that does not divide $q$. Let $d$ be the multiplicative order of $q$ modulo $\ell$, and let $e = v_\ell([d]_q)$ measure how strongly $\ell$ divides the first $q$-integer it touches. Write $n = dN + r$ and $k = dA + s$ with $0 \le r, s < d$. Then:

> **The $q$-Kummer Theorem.** For $k \le n$,
> $$v_\ell\!\left(\binom{n}{k}_{\!q}\right) = e\,c \;+\; v_\ell\!\left(\binom{N}{A}\right) \;+\; c\,v_\ell\!\left(\left\lfloor \tfrac{n-k}{d}\right\rfloor + 1\right),$$
> where $c \in \{0,1\}$ is the carry out of the base-$d$ digit, i.e. $c = 1$ exactly when $s + (n-k) \bmod d \ge d$.

Read it as a two-stage carry count. The bottom digit is written in base $d$: a carry there costs $e$, plus a small correction term. Every higher digit is classical: the surviving term $v_\ell\binom{N}{A}$ is, by Kummer's own theorem, precisely the number of carries when $A$ and $N - A$ are added in base $\ell$. So the valuation of a Gaussian binomial coefficient is a carry count in a **mixed radix** $(d, \ell, \ell, \ell, \dots)$ — one exotic digit sitting underneath an ordinary base-$\ell$ expansion.

The engine of the proof is the exactness identity. Because $[k]_q![n-k]_q!\binom{n}{k}_q = [n]_q!$, the valuation of the Gaussian coefficient is determined the moment one knows the valuation of $q$-factorials. And that is a counting problem: which $m \le n$ have $\ell \mid [m]_q$, and how strongly? The answer is furnished by *lifting the exponent*, the classical statement that for an odd prime $\ell$ dividing $x-1$ one has $v_\ell(x^t - 1) = v_\ell(x-1) + v_\ell(t)$. Applied with $x = q^d$, it says that the $\ell$-adic valuations of the $q$-integers are *regular*:
$$v_\ell([m]_q) = \begin{cases} e + v_\ell(m/d), & d \mid m,\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
Feeding that into the product $[n]_q! = \prod_{m \le n} [m]_q$ gives a deformed Legendre formula,
$$v_\ell\!\left([n]_q!\right) = e\left\lfloor \tfrac{n}{d} \right\rfloor + v_\ell\!\left(\left\lfloor \tfrac{n}{d}\right\rfloor!\right),$$
and subtracting three copies of it — for $n$, $k$ and $n-k$ — yields the theorem, with the correction term appearing exactly when the base-$d$ digits carry.

## The prime $2$ is different (and can be repaired)

Lifting the exponent has a famous defect at $\ell = 2$, and it bites here. The recipe "$d$ = order of $q$ modulo $\ell$" is simply wrong at $\ell = 2$, because that order is always $1$ for odd $q$. Concretely, take $q = 3$: the recipe predicts $v_2([m]_3) = v_2(m)$, so $v_2([2]_3) = 1$; but $[2]_3 = 4$, whose $2$-adic valuation is $2$. The prediction fails, and with it the naive $q$-Kummer formula: $\binom{2}{1}_3 = 4$ has valuation $2$, not the $v_2\binom{2}{1} = 1$ the recipe forecasts.

The repair is clean and, once seen, inevitable: **at $\ell = 2$ one must use the order of $q$ modulo $4$, not modulo $2$.** If $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ the period is $1$ and the offset is $0$; if $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ the period is $2$ and the offset is $v_2(q+1)$. With that single substitution the formula holds verbatim at every prime not dividing $q$ — one master statement, one anomalous digit, no exceptions.

## The congruence side: a $q$-Lucas theorem

Kummer's valuation theorem and Lucas's congruence theorem are two faces of the same combinatorics, and the deformation respects that.

> **The $q$-Lucas Theorem.** With $d$ the multiplicative order of $q$ modulo the prime $\ell \nmid q$, for all $k \le n$,
> $$\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} \;\equiv\; \binom{\lfloor n/d\rfloor}{\lfloor k/d\rfloor}\cdot \binom{n \bmod d}{\,k \bmod d}_{\!q} \pmod{\ell}.$$

Again: one deformed factor, coming from the anomalous bottom digit, times an entirely classical binomial coefficient in the block variables. Iterating the classical Lucas theorem on that second factor expands it into base-$\ell$ digits and yields a genuinely mixed-radix product, in radices $(d, \ell, \ell, \dots)$, with exactly one $q$-binomial factor surviving.

Two features of this statement deserve emphasis. There is no parity hypothesis — the congruence holds at $\ell = 2$ as well, because its proof needs only that residues mod $\ell$ form a field. And there is no upper bound on $n$: a naive cancellation argument would demand $\lfloor n/d \rfloor < \ell$, but routing the block product through a binomial coefficient in the variable $q^d$ removes the restriction entirely. The theorem is exact for all $n$.

The congruence explains the pictures. Colour the entry $\binom{n}{k}_q$ black when $\ell \nmid \binom{n}{k}_q$. The $q$-Lucas theorem says the black set is the classical Sierpiński pattern of Pascal's triangle mod $\ell$, with every cell inflated into a solid $d \times d$ triangular block. For $q = 2$, $\ell = 5$ (so $d = 4$) the first rows look like this:

```
 0 | #
 1 | ##
 2 | ###
 3 | ####
 4 | #...#
 5 | ##..##
 6 | ###.###
 7 | ########
 8 | #...#...#
 ...
19 | ####################
20 | #...................#
```

The classical gasket, dilated by four.

## Counting the survivors

Once the pattern is understood, the census follows. Say that an entry *survives* if $\ell$ does not divide it. Combining the congruence with a digit-by-digit induction gives a sharp criterion: $\ell \nmid \binom{n}{k}_q$ exactly when $k \bmod d \le n \bmod d$ (no carry in the anomalous digit) **and** $\ell \nmid \binom{\lfloor n/d\rfloor}{\lfloor k/d\rfloor}$. From that one reads off a closed formula for the number of survivors in a row. Classically, the number of entries of the $N$-th row of Pascal's triangle not divisible by $p$ is $\prod_i (N_i + 1)$, the product over the base-$p$ digits of $N$ — a nineteenth-century count of Glaisher. The deformed version is:
$$\#\left\{k \le n : \ell \nmid \binom{n}{k}_{\!q}\right\} = \left(n \bmod d + 1\right)\cdot \prod_i \left(\text{digit}_i(\lfloor n/d\rfloor) + 1\right),$$
digits taken in base $\ell$. The classical count of the block row, dilated by the purely residual factor $n \bmod d + 1$. (A tempting alternative guess, $d - n \bmod d$, is false already in the very first row.)

Summing over a self-similar range of rows collapses to a clean product:
$$\sum_{n < d\,\ell^{m}} \#\left\{k : \ell \nmid \binom{n}{k}_{\!q}\right\} = \frac{d(d+1)}{2}\left(\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{2}\right)^{m}.$$
The exponential growth rate is entirely classical; the deformation contributes a single constant factor $d(d+1)/2$, the area of one triangular block.

One can also ask which rows are *entirely* free of multiples of $\ell$ — the boundary rows of the fractal. Classically, the $N$-th row of Pascal's triangle avoids $p$ altogether precisely when $N + 1 = c\,p^{t}$ with $1 \le c \le p$; the popular guess "$N+1$ is a power of $p$" is wrong, since every row below $p$ is trivially full. In the deformed triangle the full rows are exactly the $d$-dilates of the classical ones, together with the $d$ initial rows: the $n$-th row is entirely prime to $\ell$ if and only if $n + 1 \le d$ or $n + 1 = d\,c\,\ell^{t}$ with $1 \le c \le \ell$.

## How large can the valuation get?

The formula gives an immediate upper bound,
$$v_\ell\!\left(\binom{n}{k}_{\!q}\right) \le e + \log_\ell\left\lfloor \tfrac{n}{d}\right\rfloor + \log_\ell\left(\left\lfloor \tfrac{n-k}{d}\right\rfloor + 1\right),$$
logarithmic in $n$ — the same growth Kummer's theorem forces classically, offset by the constant $e$. Is the bound honest? Yes, and there is a completely explicit extremal family: for $d \ge 2$ and every $s \ge 1$,
$$v_\ell\!\left(\binom{d\,\ell^{s}}{\,d+1}_{\!q}\right) = e + s \quad\text{exactly.}$$
The choice $k = d+1$ has base-$d$ digits $(1,1)$, which forces a carry in the anomalous digit and switches on the offset $e$; the block indices are $\ell^s$ and $1$, whose classical binomial coefficient $\binom{\ell^s}{1} = \ell^s$ contributes exactly $s$; and the correction term vanishes. Both summands are attained simultaneously, infinitely often. Neither the constant nor the logarithm can be improved.

In the opposite direction, the deformation disappears entirely along the sublattice of indices divisible by $d$:
$$v_\ell\!\left(\binom{dN}{dA}_{\!q}\right) = v_\ell\!\left(\binom{N}{A}\right).$$
On multiples of the period, the $q$-triangle *is* the classical triangle. That is self-similarity made arithmetic.

## Back to geometry

All of this was really a statement about subspaces. Since $\binom{n}{k}_q$ counts the $k$-dimensional subspaces of an $n$-dimensional vector space over a field with $q$ elements, the theorem answers a question with no visible deformation in it:

> **Corollary.** Let $K$ be a finite field with $q$ elements, $V$ a $K$-vector space of dimension $n$, and $\ell$ a prime not dividing $q$. Then the exact power of $\ell$ dividing the number of $k$-dimensional subspaces of $V$ is
> $$e\,c + v_\ell\!\left(\binom{\lfloor n/d \rfloor}{\lfloor k/d\rfloor}\right) + c\,v_\ell\!\left(\left\lfloor\tfrac{n-k}{d}\right\rfloor+1\right),$$
> with $d$, $e$, $c$ as above.

Take $V = \mathbb{F}_2^6$ and $k = 3$: there are $1395$ such subspaces, and $1395 = 3^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 31$. The theorem predicts $v_5 = 1$ (one carry in base $4$, offset $1$), $v_3 = 2$ (a carry in base $2$ contributing $1$, plus the classical $v_3\binom{3}{1} = 1$), and $v_{31} = 1$. All three are correct.

This is the kind of statement that matters in the representation theory of finite groups, where the $\ell$-part of the number of subspaces controls the structure of Sylow subgroups acting on Grassmannians, and in the modular theory of $q$-series, where Gaussian coefficients are the building blocks of partition identities.

## What the deformation teaches

Step back and the moral is sharper than the individual formulas. Deforming the integers by a parameter $q$ does not scramble the arithmetic of binomial coefficients; it performs a very specific surgery. It takes the base-$\ell$ digit expansion that governs the classical theory and splices in **one new digit at the bottom**, of size $d = \mathrm{ord}_\ell(q)$, with its own weight $e$. Every higher digit remains classical. The fractal survives; it is merely dilated. The carry count survives; it merely acquires an anomalous first place value. The growth rate survives; it merely picks up an additive constant.

There is even a place where the surgery is visibly delicate. At $\ell = 2$ the recipe for the exotic digit must be read modulo $4$ rather than modulo $2$ — the same $2$-adic anomaly that makes lifting the exponent misbehave, showing up here as the one place where the deformed theory refuses to be uniform in the prime.

Kummer counted carries in a single base. The deformed theory counts carries in a mixed radix, and in doing so tells you the exact power of a prime dividing the number of subspaces of a finite vector space — a quantity nobody would have suspected of being governed by schoolroom arithmetic.

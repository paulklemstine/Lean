# Two Ternary Trees: A Tale of One That Was Tamed and One That Wasn't

## The shape of a hope

Mathematics is full of family resemblances. Two objects from entirely different
corners of the subject turn out to have the same silhouette, and for a moment it
looks as though everything we know about the easy one must secretly be true of
the hard one. Sometimes that hope pays off spectacularly — Wiles proved Fermat's
Last Theorem by showing that elliptic curves and modular forms are two faces of
one structure. Sometimes it doesn't. And when it doesn't, the *reason* it doesn't
is often more interesting than the hope was.

This is the story of one such resemblance, pursued to the end.

On one side stands a tree that mathematics has completely tamed: the **Berggren
tree** of primitive Pythagorean triples. On the other stands a tree that has
resisted everything thrown at it for ninety years: the **inverse Collatz tree**,
the branching diagram of the $3n+1$ problem. Both are ternary — both appear to
have exactly three branches at each node. The tamed one carries a beautiful
conserved quantity, a *Lorentz invariant* borrowed from special relativity, plus
an exact growth rate governed by the **silver ratio** $1+\sqrt{2}$. The wild one
carries nothing at all; that is exactly why it is wild.

The hope: find a dictionary translating one tree into the other, and the Collatz
problem inherits a conservation law it has never had.

The result, proved here: **no such dictionary exists**, and there are *four
independent reasons* why. But the failure is not a dead end. Pinning down
precisely why the transfer fails produces a sharp structural picture of the
Collatz tree — one that replaces the folklore image of a ternary tree with
something much more rigid — plus a genuine, explicit *partial* transfer that
does survive.

## The tame tree

Start with the tame one. A **primitive Pythagorean triple** is a trio of positive
whole numbers $(a,b,c)$ with

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2, \qquad \gcd(a,b,c)=1.$$

The smallest is $(3,4,5)$. There are infinitely many — $(5,12,13)$, $(8,15,17)$,
$(20,21,29)$, $(7,24,25)$ — and for a long time they looked like an unruly
infinite list. Then in 1934 it was noticed that the whole list is generated by a
tree.

Start at $(3,4,5)$. Apply, in turn, each of three fixed $3\times 3$ integer
matrices:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 2\\ 2 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 3\end{pmatrix},
\quad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2\\ 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix},
\quad
C = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 & 2\\ -2 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}.$$

Feed $(3,4,5)$ to each and you get $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$. Feed
those to each of the three matrices in turn and you get nine more. Continue
forever: every primitive Pythagorean triple appears, exactly once, at exactly one
address in this infinite ternary tree. The address is a word in the alphabet
$\{A,B,C\}$; the triples are the leaves of an alphabet of three letters.

Why does it work? Because of an invariant. Define the **Lorentz form**

$$Q(a,b,c) = a^2 + b^2 - c^2.$$

This is the quadratic form of two-dimensional Minkowski spacetime — the same
expression that measures whether an event in relativity is timelike, spacelike or
lightlike. Being a Pythagorean triple means precisely being a *null vector*:
$Q = 0$. And each of the three Berggren matrices is an isometry of $Q$: for every
triple $t$ and every letter $s \in \{A,B,C\}$,

> **Conservation of the Lorentz Form.** $Q(s\cdot t) = Q(t)$.

So a null vector goes to a null vector, and Pythagorean triples map to
Pythagorean triples automatically. Applied along an entire word,

> **Null Tree Theorem.** Every node of the Berggren tree, at every depth, satisfies
> $Q = 0$. The tree lives entirely on the light cone.

That is the conserved quantity the Collatz problem is missing. There is also a
growth law. Follow the all-$B$ branch — the *Pell spine* — and the hypotenuses are

$$5,\; 29,\; 169,\; 985,\; 5741,\; 33461,\; \dots$$

obeying the recurrence $c_{n+2} = 6c_{n+1} - c_n$. Their ratios climb rapidly to a
limit:

> **Silver Growth.** For every $n$, $\;5\cdot 29^n \le 5^n c_n\;$ and $\;c_n \le 5\cdot 6^n$,
> so the growth factor is trapped in the window $[29/5,\,6] = [5.8,\,6]$. Inside
> that window sits the silver ratio squared,
> $$(1+\sqrt 2)^2 = 3 + 2\sqrt 2 = 5.8284271\ldots,$$
> which is the true asymptotic ratio $c_{n+1}/c_n$.

So: exactly three branches, a conserved quadratic form, an exact growth exponent.
Everything you could want from a tree.

## The wild tree

Now the other one. The **Collatz map** takes a whole number, halves it if even,
and sends it to $3n+1$ if odd. The conjecture — open since 1937, and famously
described by Erdős as "mathematics is not yet ready for such problems" — says
that every starting value eventually reaches $1$.

Because halving an even number is bookkeeping, the real content lives in the
**Syracuse map**: given an odd $n$, form $3n+1$ and strip off all the factors of
$2$ at once. The Syracuse map sends odd numbers to odd numbers. The conjecture
becomes: every odd number eventually reaches $1$.

Turn the map around, and you get a tree. Call $m$ a **predecessor** of the odd
number $n$ when one Syracuse step carries $m$ to $n$ — that is, when

$$3m+1 = 2^k n \quad \text{for some } k \ge 1.$$

Draw an edge from $n$ up to each of its predecessors and you get the *inverse
Collatz tree*. The Collatz conjecture is exactly the statement that this tree,
rooted at $1$, contains every odd number.

Folklore says this tree is ternary — every node has "about three" children, and
that vague statement is the source of countless heuristic arguments about Collatz
dynamics. Precisely this vagueness is where the hoped-for bridge collapses.

## Obstruction one: the branching number is never three

Do the arithmetic. If $3m+1 = 2^k n$, then reducing modulo $3$ gives
$1 \equiv 2^k n$. So if $3$ divides $n$, the equation is unsolvable — the node is
**dead**.

> **Dead Nodes.** If $3 \mid n$, then $n$ has no Collatz predecessor whatsoever.

Multiples of $3$ are leaves of the inverse tree; nothing grows above them. Check
it by hand: $3, 9, 15, 21$ are reached by the Syracuse map from nowhere.

And what about the rest? The opposite extreme.

> **Infinite Branching.** If $n$ is odd and $3 \nmid n$, then $n$ has *infinitely
> many* predecessors.

The proof is short. Since $2^k \bmod 3$ alternates $2,1,2,1,\dots$, exactly one of
$k=1$ or $k=2$ makes $2^k n \equiv 1 \pmod 3$; call the winner $e(n)$. Then
$2^{e(n)+2j} n \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ for *every* $j \ge 0$, because $4^j \equiv 1
\pmod 3$. Each such exponent yields a genuine predecessor $(2^{e(n)+2j}n-1)/3$,
and these are strictly increasing, hence all different. Infinitely many of them.

Put the two together:

> **Branching Dichotomy.** The predecessor set of an odd number is either empty
> or infinite. It is *never* a finite set of size three.

For $n=1$, the predecessors are $1, 5, 21, 85, 341, 1365, \dots$ — an infinite
list, not a triple. The very first premise of the bridge is false, and the
conclusion follows at once:

> **No Branching Transfer.** There is no map $\Phi$ from Pythagorean triples to odd
> numbers that sends, for each triple in the positive cone with unequal legs, the
> three Berggren children bijectively onto the Collatz predecessors of the image.
> A set of three cannot biject with a set that is empty or infinite.

No branching isomorphism, no transported invariant.

## Obstruction two: the wild tree has a loop

Suppose we give up on isomorphism and ask only for the weaker thing — an
embedding running the *other* way, from the Collatz tree into the Berggren tree.
That fails too, and for a reason visible at the very first node.

$3\cdot 1 + 1 = 4 = 2^2\cdot 1$, so $1$ is its own predecessor. The Collatz graph
has a **self-loop** at $1$. The Berggren graph has none: each of the three
matrices strictly increases the hypotenuse of any triple in the positive cone
($0 < a,b < c$), by the explicit formulas $c \mapsto 2a - 2b + 3c$,
$2a+2b+3c$, $-2a+2b+3c$, each of which exceeds $c$. A graph with a loop cannot
be mapped edge-preservingly into a loop-free one.

> **No Reverse Embedding.** No map from odd numbers into the positive Pythagorean
> cone can carry every Collatz predecessor edge to a Berggren child edge.

## Obstruction three: nothing at all is conserved

This is the sharpest of the four, and it kills not just the Lorentz form but
every possible substitute.

> **Rigidity Theorem.** Let $P$ be any polynomial with rational coefficients such
> that $P(m) = P(n)$ whenever $m$ is a Collatz predecessor of $n$. Then $P$ is
> constant.

The proof is three lines once the branching dichotomy is in hand. The node $1$ has
infinitely many predecessors $m$, and each satisfies $P(m) = P(1)$. So the
polynomial $P - P(1)$ has infinitely many roots. A nonzero polynomial over a field
has finitely many roots. Hence $P = P(1)$ identically.

You can watch it happen with bare hands. Suppose $\alpha x^2 + \beta x + \gamma$
were conserved. The numbers $1$, $5$ and $21$ all map to $1$, so

$$24\alpha + 4\beta = 0, \qquad 440\alpha + 20\beta = 0.$$

The determinant is $-1280 \ne 0$, so $\alpha = \beta = 0$. The candidate invariant
evaporates against three small numbers.

Contrast this with the Berggren side, where the Lorentz form *is* conserved by
every letter and *is* genuinely nonconstant ($Q(3,4,5) = 0$ while $Q(1,1,1) = 1$).
The structure the Collatz tree would need is exactly the structure it provably
cannot have. Nor is there a growth exponent to transfer: since $1$ has
predecessors larger than any bound you name, while $1$ is also its own
predecessor, Collatz edge expansion ratios range over everything from $1$ to
infinity. There is no silver ratio hiding in the $3n+1$ problem.

## Obstruction four: the tree is a comb, not a bush

Why do the fibres come out infinite? Because they are generated by *one* map, not
three. Here is the exact structure — the correct replacement for the folklore
picture.

Notice that if $m$ is a predecessor of $n$, so is $4m+1$: indeed
$3(4m+1)+1 = 4(3m+1)$, so multiplying the exponent budget by $4$ preserves the
defining equation. Write $L(x) = 4x+1$. Then:

> **Fibre Structure Theorem.** For odd $n$ with $3 \nmid n$, the set of Collatz
> predecessors of $n$ is *exactly* the forward orbit of $L(x) = 4x+1$ started at
> the least predecessor $m_0 = (2^{e(n)}n - 1)/3$:
> $$\{\,m_0,\; 4m_0+1,\; 16m_0+5,\; \dots\,\}.$$
> Every fibre is a single orbit of a single affine map, order-isomorphic to the
> natural numbers.

Over $n=1$: $m_0 = 1$, and $1 \mapsto 5 \mapsto 21 \mapsto 85 \mapsto 341$. Over
$n=5$: $3, 13, 53, 213, 853$. Over $n=7$: $9, 37, 149, 597$. Not a three-way
branching but a one-dimensional comb, infinitely long.

This is the deepest form of the obstruction, because it is a statement about
*rank*. The Berggren fibre needs three generators and no fewer — a single map can
produce at most one child, but the three Berggren children of $(3,4,5)$ are
distinct. The Collatz fibre needs exactly one generator. Rank three versus rank
one; the branching monoids are not even of the same shape.

And rank one is *by itself* the reason invariants fail. Nothing about the
arithmetic of $3n+1$ is required:

> **Affine Rank-One Rigidity.** Let $a \ge 2$ and $b \ge 0$ be integers and let
> $x_0 > 0$. If a rational polynomial is constant along the orbit
> $x_0,\; ax_0+b,\; a^2x_0+ab+b,\dots$, then it is constant. The Collatz case is
> $(a,b)=(4,1)$.

The reason: a multiplier $a\ge 2$ makes the orbit strictly increasing, hence
infinite, hence a set on which a polynomial cannot be constant without being
constant everywhere. The Collatz tree's lack of invariants is a *geometry of
branching* phenomenon, not a $3n+1$ phenomenon. That is a genuinely clarifying
piece of information: it tells you that no amount of clever tinkering with the
arithmetic will produce a conserved quantity, because the enemy is the comb.

## The word calculus: where exactly the analogy breaks

There is an illuminating way to say what goes wrong, one that puts both trees in
the same language.

The Berggren tree is governed by *words*: a path is a string in $\{A,B,C\}$, and
the effect on a triple is multiplication by the corresponding product of matrices.
That action is **linear**, and linear actions can preserve quadratic forms.

The Syracuse tree has words too. A path from odd $m$ to odd $n$ records the
sequence $k_1, k_2, \dots, k_L$ of how many factors of $2$ were stripped at each
step. Writing $S = k_1+\cdots+k_L$ for the total halving budget and $L$ for the
number of steps, one proves by a short induction:

> **Word Identity.** Along a Syracuse path with valuation word $(k_1,\dots,k_L)$
> from $m$ to $n$,
> $$2^{S} n \;=\; 3^{L} m \;+\; w(k_1,\dots,k_L),$$
> where the *weight* $w$ is defined by $w(\varnothing) = 0$ and
> $w(k,\mathbf{k}') = 3^{|\mathbf{k}'|} + 2^{k}\,w(\mathbf{k}')$.

Compare the two. The Berggren word acts by a matrix: purely multiplicative. The
Syracuse word acts by $x \mapsto (3^L x + w)/2^S$: multiplicative *plus a
translation*. That extra additive term $w$ — the affine cocycle — has no Berggren
analogue, and it is exactly what destroys conservation. A linear map can fix a
quadratic form; an affine map with a nonzero translation generically cannot fix
anything polynomial.

So the analogy has a precise measure of failure, and the measure is $w$.

That is not merely a diagnosis; the weight is a working tool. Applied to a
*cycle* — a path returning to its start — the identity gives the classical cycle
equation in a clean form:

> **Cycle Equation.** If a Syracuse path of length $L$ and halving budget $S$
> returns to its starting value $m$, then
> $$m\,(2^{S} - 3^{L}) = w(k_1,\dots,k_L).$$
> In particular $3^L < 2^S$: any cycle must spend more halvings than
> $L\log_2 3 \approx 1.585\,L$.

Check it on the one cycle we know: $1 \to 1$ has word $(2)$, so $L=1$, $S=2$,
$w=1$, and indeed $1\cdot(4-3) = 1$. A pretty consequence falls out for free: if
every step of a cycle halved only once, then $S = L$, and $3^L < 2^L$ would have
to hold. Hence:

> **No Minimal-Step Cycle.** Every Syracuse cycle must contain at least one step
> whose halving count is $2$ or more. A trajectory alternating "$3n+1$, halve
> once" can never close up.

And bounding the weight on both sides turns the cycle equation into a quantitative
pin. One proves $3^L \le 3w$ and $2^L w \le 3^L 2^S$ for every admissible word.
Feeding these into the cycle equation gives:

> **Two-Heavy Cycles Are Tiny.** If a Syracuse cycle satisfies
> $2\cdot 3^L \le 2^{S}$ — that is, if it spends even one full extra doubling
> beyond the critical ratio — then its minimum obeys
> $$m \;\le\; 2\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^{L}.$$

Since a long cycle would need an enormous minimum, this forces any hypothetical
nontrivial cycle to hug the critical line $S/L = \log_2 3$ extraordinarily
closely — the classical continued-fraction obstruction, here derived cleanly from
the weight bounds. A small but honest piece of progress that the failed bridge
handed us on the way out.

## What survives: the shape, not the substance

Finally, the good news — the piece of the moonshot that is actually true.

The transfer cannot be an isomorphism. But it can be an **embedding**. The
Berggren tree's ternary shape really does sit inside the inverse Collatz tree.

Here is the construction, and it is completely explicit. Over a live node $n$
(odd, not divisible by $3$), the fibre is the comb $m_0, 4m_0+1, \dots$, and its
residues modulo $3$ advance by one at each rung:

> **Residue Clock.** The $j$-th member of the fibre over $n$ is congruent to
> $m_0 + j$ modulo $3$. Exactly one member in three is dead (divisible by $3$);
> the other two in three are live and branch further.

Over $n=1$, the fibre $1,5,21,85,341,1365,\dots$ has residues
$1,2,0,1,2,0,\dots$ — the dead rungs are $21, 1365, \dots$, at indices
$j \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, exactly as the clock predicts.

So among any six consecutive rungs at least *four* are live, and we can always
choose three. Fix a rule that, given the residue of $m_0$, selects three distinct
live indices from $\{1,\dots,5\}$, and lift every node three ways. Iterating from
the root $1$ gives a map from Berggren words to odd numbers:

> **Ternary Subtree Theorem.** There is an explicit map $F$ from words in
> $\{A,B,C\}$ to odd numbers with $F(\varnothing) = 1$ such that
> (i) every $F(w)$ is odd and not divisible by $3$, so the construction never
> stalls; (ii) $F(ws)$ is a genuine Collatz predecessor of $F(w)$ for each letter
> $s$; (iii) $F(w) < F(ws)$, so the subtree strictly grows; and (iv) the three
> children $F(wA), F(wB), F(wC)$ are pairwise distinct.
> The free ternary tree embeds into the inverse Collatz tree.

The first two levels, from the root $1$:

| word | $\varnothing$ | A | B | C | AA | AB | AC | BA | BB | BC | CA | CB | CC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| image | 1 | 5 | 85 | 341 | 13 | 53 | 853 | 1813 | 7253 | 116053 | 3637 | 14549 | 232789 |

Every one of these is an honest Collatz predecessor of its parent. You can verify
the whole first six levels — $1093$ nodes — in under a second, and they all pass.

So the final verdict is a clean split. **Shape transfers; substance does not.**
The ternary branching that made the Berggren tree look like a Collatz cousin is
genuinely present inside the Collatz tree, as a sparse skeleton of fast-growing
live nodes. But the conserved Lorentz form, the thing that made the Berggren tree
*solvable*, cannot come along, because a subtree carries no invariant that the
whole tree lacks, and the whole tree provably lacks every polynomial invariant
there is.

## Why a negative answer is worth having

It is tempting to read all this as a failure. It isn't, for two reasons.

First, it is a *sharp* negative. The Collatz literature is full of heuristic
arguments that rely on the tree being "roughly ternary". It is not roughly
ternary. It is a forest of one-dimensional combs, about a third of whose rungs
terminate immediately, glued together at live nodes. Any heuristic calibrated to
three-fold branching is calibrated to the wrong object, and the Fibre Structure
Theorem says exactly what the right object is.

Second, the *mechanism* of failure is transferable knowledge. The reason the
Berggren tree yields and the Collatz tree does not is not that Pythagorean triples
are easy and $3n+1$ is hard. It is that the Berggren alphabet acts linearly and
has rank three, while the Syracuse alphabet acts affinely and has rank one. The
Affine Rank-One Rigidity Theorem makes this a theorem rather than a slogan: *any*
tree whose fibres are single affine orbits with multiplier at least $2$ is immune
to polynomial invariants, whatever the arithmetic behind it. If you want a
conserved quantity for Collatz, you now know it cannot be a polynomial in $n$, and
you know the structural feature responsible.

The right image to keep is the light cone. The Berggren tree lives on it: every
node satisfies $a^2+b^2 = c^2$, forever, because three linear isometries of
Minkowski space preserve it forever. The Collatz tree has no cone to live on. Each
of its steps drags in an additive remainder — the weight $w$ — and after $L$ steps
the accumulated remainder is a number of size comparable to $3^L$, utterly
scrambling any quadratic bookkeeping you try to impose.

That additive remainder is the whole difficulty of the $3n+1$ problem, stated in
one symbol. We now know its exact recursive form, we know two-sided bounds for it,
and we know that it pins hypothetical cycles to within a hair of the line
$S = L\log_2 3$. That is not a solution to Collatz. But it is a clear, honest
piece of the map — and it tells the next explorer precisely which path not to take.

# How Much Can a Prime Tell You About Its Twin?

## The one-bit wall, and the crack that runs through it

Take a large number $N$ that you know is the product of two primes, $N = pq$, but whose factors you cannot find. What can you learn about $p$ and $q$ just from $N$ itself — not by factoring, but by *listening*?

This sounds like a question about cryptography, and in part it is. But it is really a question about information. Every prime carries a small bundle of arithmetic labels: is it $1$ or $3$ modulo $4$? Is it a square modulo $5$? Does it split, stay inert, or ramify in a given number field? These labels are not random — they are governed by deep equidistribution laws — but they behave *as if* random, and information theory is the natural language for saying how much of that pseudo-randomness survives when you multiply two primes together and throw the factors away.

A sequence of earlier investigations arrived at a striking-looking barrier. Whenever you attach to each prime a single **binary** label — a coin flip, essentially: split or inert, residue $+1$ or $-1$ — and you ask how much the product $N$ tells you about the *unordered pair* of labels of its two factors, the answer never exceeded **one bit**. Not $1.3$ bits, not $1.001$: exactly one, or less. The binary fork seemed capped.

The result described here is that the cap is an artifact of the binary label, not a law of arithmetic. Replace the coin flip with the *complete* splitting type of a prime in a cyclotomic field — a multi-state observable — and the cap breaks. The pair channel of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ carries exactly $1.25$ bits. That of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ carries $1.4739\ldots$ bits. And that is only the beginning: the amount of information turns out to be governed, with beautiful precision, by the **divisor lattice** of the Galois group.

## The complete label of a prime

Fix a prime $f$ and work in the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$ generated by a primitive $f$-th root of unity. Its Galois group is the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$, cyclic of order $n = f-1$. For any prime $p \neq f$, the Frobenius element attached to $p$ is simply the residue class $p \bmod f$, and the *splitting type* of $p$ is the order of that class:

$$T(p) \;=\; \operatorname{ord}_f(p) \;=\; \text{the smallest } k \ge 1 \text{ with } p^k \equiv 1 \pmod f .$$

This single number tells you everything about how $p$ decomposes: the prime $p$ factors in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$ into exactly $n/T(p)$ distinct primes, each with residue degree $T(p)$. If $T(p) = 1$, the prime splits completely into $n$ pieces; if $T(p) = n$, it stays inert.

Two features make $T$ the right observable.

First, it is a **complete** invariant of the splitting behaviour — nothing about the factorisation of $p$ is left over once you know $T(p)$.

Second, it is **exactly determined** by $p \bmod f$. There is no leakage and no residual noise: the mutual information between the residue $p \bmod f$ and the type $T$ equals the full entropy of $T$. Nor does looking deeper help: knowing $p$ modulo $f^2$, or $f^3$, tells you nothing more about $T$ than $p \bmod f$ already did. The channel is *thin* — all its content sits at the first level.

By Chebotarev's density theorem (1922), the Frobenius classes are equidistributed: each residue in $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$ occurs with density $1/n$ among the primes. So the correct probabilistic model is transparent: **pick a uniformly random element $x$ of the cyclic group $C_n$, and let the type be the order of $x$**, which is

$$T(x) \;=\; \frac{n}{\gcd(n, x)} .$$

The law of $T$ is then Euler's totient law on the divisor lattice of $n$:

$$\mathbb{P}(T = d) \;=\; \frac{\varphi(d)}{n}, \qquad d \mid n .$$

For $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$, where $n = 4$: the types are $\{1, 2, 4\}$ with probabilities $\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12$, an entropy of exactly $1.5$ bits. For $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$, where $n = 6$: types $\{1,2,3,6\}$ with probabilities $\tfrac16, \tfrac16, \tfrac13, \tfrac13$, an entropy of $1.9183\ldots$ bits. Sampling the actual primes reproduces these numbers to three decimals — the abstract model is not an idealisation, it is what the primes really do.

## Losing information by squinting

Before pairing primes, it is worth noticing how much is thrown away by the traditional binary readout. Instead of the full type, most classical treatments record only the **root count**: does $p$ split completely, yes or no? Call this coarsened observable $\mathrm{nr}$.

Coarsening can never create information, so the entropy of $\mathrm{nr}$ is at most that of $T$. The sharp statement is a clean dichotomy:

> **Root-Count Lossiness Theorem.** For every cyclic order $n \ge 2$, the binary root-count readout has strictly less entropy than the splitting type *precisely when $n$ is composite*; for prime $n$ the two agree exactly.

The reason is structural. When $n$ is prime, $T$ takes only two values, $1$ and $n$, and "splits completely or not" is a faithful relabelling. When $n$ is composite, the divisor lattice has intermediate levels, and the binary readout crushes them all into a single bucket. For $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$, the types $2$ and $4$ — "$p$ splits into two primes of degree $2$" versus "$p$ is inert" — become indistinguishable.

The loss is not a rounding error; asymptotically it is *everything*. The root-count entropy is exactly the binary entropy of $1/n$, which is bounded by $(\log_2 n + 2)/n$ and therefore tends to zero. Along the tower of $2$-power orders $n = 2^k$, by contrast, the type entropy has the exact closed form

$$H(T)(2^k) \;=\; 2 - 2^{1-k},$$

strictly increasing and converging to exactly two bits. So along that tower the type channel saturates at two full bits while the root-count readout reports asymptotically none of them. **The type, not the root count, is the complete object.**

## Multiplying two primes

Now for the real question. Take two primes $p$ and $q$ with Frobenius classes $x$ and $y$ in $C_n$. Their product $N = pq$ has Frobenius class $x + y$ (writing the cyclic group additively). An observer sees $N$ — hence the class $c = x+y$ — and wants to know the **unordered pair of types** $\{T(x), T(y)\}$.

The information available is the mutual information between the product class and the type pair:

$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n) \;=\; H(\Pi) \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{c} H(\Pi_c),$$

where $\Pi$ is the law of the unordered pair over all $n^2$ choices of $(x,y)$, and $\Pi_c$ is the same law conditioned on $x + y = c$. Both are finite tables, so $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n)$ is an exactly computable number for each $n$.

For $n = 2$ — the quadratic case, $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ — one finds $H(\Pi) = 1.5$, the average conditional entropy is $0.5$, and $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2) = 1.0000$ exactly: the old one-bit cap, reproduced on the nose. And then:

| Galois order $n$ | field | types | $H(T)$ | $H(\Pi)$ | $H(\Pi \mid N)$ | $I_{\mathrm{pair}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ | $2$ | $1.0000$ | $1.5000$ | $0.5000$ | $1.0000$ |
| $4$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ | $3$ | $1.5000$ | $2.3750$ | $1.1250$ | $\mathbf{1.2500}$ |
| $6$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ | $4$ | $1.9183$ | $3.1144$ | $1.6405$ | $\mathbf{1.4739}$ |
| $10$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$ | $4$ | $1.7219$ | — | — | $\mathbf{1.2027}$ |
| $12$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})$ | $6$ | $2.4183$ | — | — | $\mathbf{1.7239}$ |
| $16$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$ | $5$ | $1.8750$ | — | — | $\mathbf{1.3281}$ |

Every entry from $n = 4$ on exceeds one bit. The cap is broken, and $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})$ leaks nearly $1.73$ bits about the type pair of the two hidden factors.

Two sanity checks matter here. First, the numbers are not artifacts of the model: simulating with tens of thousands of genuine semiprimes reproduces them to within a few thousandths of a bit ($1.2452$ against $1.2500$; $1.4711$ against $1.4739$). Second, if you replace the type by its old binary shadow, the recovered information collapses back to the small quadratic-style values — the split-count channel is one *face* of the type channel, not the whole of it.

## Why primes cannot break the cap — and composites can

The most surprising part of the story is what happens when $n$ itself is prime. Then the type has only two states, $\{1, p\}$, and the whole pair channel can be computed in closed form for symbolic $p$. Out of $p^2$ ordered pairs, the state $\{1,1\}$ occurs once, the mixed state $\{1,p\}$ occurs $2(p-1)$ times, and $\{p,p\}$ occurs $(p-1)^2$ times. Conditioning on the product class splits into just two regimes — the class $0$, where the counts are $(1, 0, p-1)$, and each nonzero class, where they are $(0, 2, p-2)$. Assembling the four entropies gives an exact identity valid for *every* prime:

$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p) \;=\; \log_2 p \;-\; \frac{(p-1)(2p-1)}{p^2}\log_2 (p-1) \;+\; \frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{p^2}\log_2 (p-2).$$

At $p=2$ the last term vanishes and the formula returns exactly $1$. At $p=3$ it gives $0.4739$; at $p=5$, $0.2027$; at $p=7$, $0.1141$. The channel dies fast, and one can prove it dies at rate $p^{-2}$: for every odd prime,

$$\frac{1}{p^2 \ln 2} \;\le\; I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p) \;\le\; \frac{\log_2 p + 5}{p^2}.$$

In particular it is always strictly positive — a prime-order channel is never *completely* silent — but it is always strictly below one bit. Only $p = 2$ attains the cap, and it attains it exactly.

So the cap is not broken by making the group *bigger*. It is broken by making the group's **divisor lattice richer**. A prime cyclic order has a two-level lattice and nothing to say; every composite order in the tested range says more than one bit's worth.

## The hidden multiplicative skeleton

Why should divisors matter so much? Because the whole structure is multiplicative, in the most classical way: by the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then $\gcd(mn, a) = \gcd(m,a)\gcd(n,a)$, and therefore the splitting type factors:

$$T_{mn}(a) \;=\; T_m(a)\, T_n(a) \;=\; \operatorname{lcm}\!\big(T_m(a), T_n(a)\big).$$

The two coordinates are independent under the uniform law, so the entropies simply add:

$$H(T)(mn) \;=\; H(T)(m) + H(T)(n) \qquad (\gcd(m,n)=1).$$

Iterating over the prime factorisation, the type entropy of any cyclic order is the sum of the entropies of its Sylow pieces — the channel decomposes exactly along the primary decomposition of the Galois group. For squarefree orders this collapses to the explicit sum $\sum_{p \mid n}\big(\log_2 p - \tfrac{p-1}{p}\log_2(p-1)\big)$.

And there is a matching ceiling. Since the type is supported on the divisors of $n$, its entropy can never exceed $\log_2 d(n)$, the logarithm of the divisor count — and for $n \ge 3$ the inequality is strict, because the totient law is never uniform. Together with the root-count bound this sandwiches the whole theory:

$$H(\mathrm{nr}) \;\le\; H(T) \;\le\; \log_2 d(n).$$

The lower bound is the crude binary shadow; the upper bound is pure divisor combinatorics. Everything the type channel can carry lives between them.

The same multiplicative skeleton appears to govern the pair channel too: numerically, $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(12) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3) + I_{\mathrm{pair}}(4) = 0.4739 + 1.25 = 1.7239$, and $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(10) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2) + I_{\mathrm{pair}}(5) = 1 + 0.2027$, and so on for every coprime pair tested. Along the $2$-power tower the values $1,\ 1.25,\ 1.3125,\ 1.328125$ fit the elegant guess $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2^k) = \tfrac43\big(1 - 4^{-k}\big)$ perfectly. If both patterns are theorems — and they remain conjectural — then half the classification falls out immediately: **every even order from $4$ upward exceeds one bit**, because its $2$-part alone already contributes at least $1.25$ and no other part subtracts. In this sense the cap is a statement about $2$-torsion, not about size: exactly one bit of pair information is unconditionally recoverable through the quotient by squares, and anything above one bit must be bought at a deeper even level of the divisor lattice.

The other half — the tempting guess that *odd* orders always stay below the cap — turns out to be untenable, and the exact prime formula is what kills it. Under additivity, a squarefree odd order contributes the sum of the prime values $0.4739 + 0.2027 + 0.1141 + 0.0519 + \cdots$, and although those numbers shrink like $p^{-2}$, they do not shrink fast enough: summed over the thirty-three odd primes up to $139$ they already total $1.00010\ldots$ bits, and over all odd primes they approach $1.0132$. So if the pair channel really is additive — and every coprime pair yet tested says it is, exactly — then the odd order $3\cdot5\cdot7\cdots139$ breaks the cap too, by accumulating a great many small leaks instead of one large one. Breaking the one-bit wall is *typically* an even phenomenon; it is not exclusively one.

## What it does not do

It would be misleading to end without the negative results, which are as sharp as the positive ones.

The channel is **symmetric**: it tells you the unordered pair $\{T(p), T(q)\}$ and essentially nothing about which factor is which. The measured "which-factor" information is $0.0001$ bits — noise. Learning that one of your two hidden primes is inert and the other splits completely does not tell you which is which, and it does not tell you their sizes.

The channel is also **shallow**: it is a pure function of $p \bmod f$, thickening to higher powers of $f$ buys nothing, and the information is only accessible after you already know how to read the product's residue class. It is a statement about the arithmetic of residues, not an attack on anything.

What it *is* is a clean structural discovery about how arithmetic information composes. A prime carries a multi-state label. Multiplying two primes mixes their labels through a group law. How much survives the mixing is not a universal constant but a precise function of the divisor lattice of the Galois group — exactly computable, exactly additive over coprime parts, exactly two bits in the limit along the $2$-tower, exactly $\log_2 p - \frac{(p-1)(2p-1)}{p^2}\log_2(p-1) + \frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{p^2}\log_2(p-2)$ at prime order, and — for every even order from four upward — comfortably more than the one bit that a coin flip could ever have provided.

The lesson generalises past this particular corner of number theory. When a physical or arithmetic system seems to obey a hard information bound, the bound may belong to your *instrument* rather than to the system. Look at the complete observable, and the wall may turn out to have a door in it.

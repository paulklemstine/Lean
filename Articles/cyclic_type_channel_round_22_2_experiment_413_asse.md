# The Bit That Wasn't There: How Prime Splitting Types Break an Information Barrier

## A question about secrets

Suppose I hand you a number $N$ and tell you only that it is the product of two primes, $N = pq$. You know nothing about $p$ and $q$ individually. Now I ask a strange question: *how much can $N$ tell you about the arithmetic personalities of its two hidden factors?*

"Arithmetic personality" sounds vague, but number theory makes it precise. Fix a prime modulus $f$ — say $f = 5$ — and look at each factor modulo $f$. The residue $p \bmod 5$ is a number in $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and it carries real structural information: it decides how $p$ behaves inside the field generated by the fifth roots of unity. Some primes split apart completely there into four distinct pieces; others refuse to split at all; still others break into exactly two pieces. This behaviour is the prime's *splitting type*.

The question, then, is a question about information: knowing $N \bmod 5$ — which is all you can compute without factoring — how much do you learn about the *pair* of splitting types $\{$type of $p$, type of $q\}$?

The answer turns out to be surprisingly sharp, surprisingly beautiful, and surprisingly large. There is a folklore ceiling for questions of this shape: one bit. Symmetric two-way forks — where each factor either does or doesn't have some property, and you only see the product — cap out at exactly one bit of shared information. The main discovery described here is that the ceiling is an artifact of *binary* thinking. The moment you stop asking "does $p$ split or not?" and start asking "*how* does $p$ split?", the channel widens: $1.25$ bits, $1.47$ bits, $1.72$ bits, with no ceiling in sight beyond one that we can compute exactly.

## Splitting types, in one paragraph

Let $f$ be a prime and consider the field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$ obtained by adjoining a primitive $f$-th root of unity to the rationals. Its symmetry group is the multiplicative group of residues modulo $f$, which is cyclic of order $n = f - 1$. Each prime $p \ne f$ acquires a *Frobenius element*, and it is nothing more exotic than the residue $p \bmod f$ viewed inside that cyclic group. Its order,
$$T(p) = \operatorname{ord}_f(p),$$
is the *residue degree*: $p$ factors in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$ into exactly $n/T(p)$ distinct primes, each of degree $T(p)$. If $T(p) = 1$ the prime splits completely; if $T(p) = n$ it stays inert.

To handle this cleanly, pick a generator $g$ of the cyclic group and write each residue as $g^a$. Then the type has a purely combinatorial formula:
$$T(a) = \frac{n}{\gcd(a, n)}.$$
So the whole problem reduces to a statement about the exponent $a$, a uniformly random element of $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Everything below is a theorem about that finite model, and every theorem about the finite model is a theorem about primes, because a generator exists and the exponent of a prime is genuinely uniform in the limit — this is the Chebotarev density theorem, or in this abelian setting, Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions.

The states available are exactly the divisors of $n$, and the rates are exactly Euler's totient function:
$$\Pr[T = d] = \frac{\varphi(d)}{n}, \qquad d \mid n.$$
For $f = 5$, so $n = 4$: the types are $\{1, 2, 4\}$ with rates $\{1/4, 1/4, 1/2\}$, giving an entropy of exactly $1.5$ bits. For $f = 7$, so $n = 6$: the types are $\{1,2,3,6\}$ with rates $\{1/6,1/6,1/3,1/3\}$, an entropy of $\log_2 3 + 1/3 \approx 1.9183$ bits. This is the **Euler-totient entropy law**:
$$H(T) = \sum_{d \mid n} \frac{\varphi(d)}{n} \log_2 \frac{n}{\varphi(d)}.$$

## The channel with no noise

Before the hard question, an easy one that sets the baseline. If you know the residue $p \bmod f$ exactly, how much do you know about $T(p)$?

*Everything.* The type is a deterministic function of the residue, so the mutual information between residue and type equals the full entropy of the type: $I(p \bmod f\,;\,T) = H(T)$, with no loss whatsoever. And a corollary that is easy to overlook: refining your observation buys nothing. If instead of $p \bmod f$ you learn $p \bmod f^2$, or $p$ modulo any multiple of $f$ at all, the information about $T$ is *unchanged* — it was already maximal. Call this **thickening zero**: the type lives on a single residue layer, and thicker moduli are dead weight.

## Two ways to read a splitting type — one of them throws information away

There is a coarser and very natural way to record what happened to $p$: count the number of roots of $x^n \equiv 1$ that a certain polynomial acquires, or equivalently, record only the binary flag "did $p$ split completely, yes or no?" This *root-count readout* collapses the multi-state type into two states.

The collapse is genuinely lossy, and we can measure the loss exactly. For $n = 4$ the root count carries
$$2 - \tfrac34 \log_2 3 \approx 0.8113 \text{ bits},$$
which is precisely the binary entropy $H(1/4)$ — the entropy of a coin that lands heads one time in four. For $n = 6$ it carries $1 + \log_2 3 - \tfrac56\log_2 5 \approx 0.6500$ bits, precisely $H(1/6)$. Against the full type entropies of $1.5$ and $1.9183$ bits, roughly half the information is discarded. **The type, not the root count, is the complete object.** The pinning of the $n=4$ readout to $H(1/4)$ deserves a name of its own: it is the exact binary entropy of a *quartic* character condition, visible at the level of individual primes.

## The pair channel: what a semiprime leaks

Now the real question. Take two primes $p, q$ with exponents $a, b$ drawn uniformly and independently from $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Two things happen:

- their **unordered type pair** $\{T(a), T(b)\}$ is formed — unordered, because a semiprime does not come with a label saying which factor is which;
- their **product residue** $N \equiv pq$, whose exponent is $a + b \bmod n$, is public.

The *type-pair channel* is the mutual information
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n) = H\big(\{T(a),T(b)\}\big) - H\big(\{T(a),T(b)\} \mid a+b\big).$$
It measures precisely how many bits about the joint splitting behaviour of the two hidden factors leak out of the visible product.

For the quadratic case $n = 2$ — the field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 5)$ — the type is binary, and the channel is exactly $1$ bit. That is the classical cap, reproduced on the nose. And then:

| cyclic order $n$ | field | states | $H(T)$ | $I_{\mathrm{pair}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $2$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ | $2$ | $1.0000$ | $1$ (at the cap) |
| $4$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_5)$ | $3$ | $1.5000$ | $5/4 = 1.2500$ |
| $6$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ | $4$ | $1.9183$ | $\log_2 3 - 1/9 \approx 1.4739$ |
| $10$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$ | $4$ | $1.7219$ | $\approx 1.2027$ |
| $12$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})$ | $6$ | $2.4183$ | $5/36 + \log_2 3 \approx 1.7239$ |
| $16$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})$ | $5$ | $1.8750$ | $85/64 = 1.3281$ |

Every entry beyond the first is strictly above one bit, and the values are not approximations: $5/4$ and $85/64$ are exact rationals, and $\log_2 3 - 1/9$ is exact to the last decimal you care to write. The barrier is broken, and broken by a definite, computable margin.

Notice also which field is richest. It is not the largest one: $n = 12$, with its six divisors $1,2,3,4,6,12$, beats $n = 16$, which has only five. **Divisor richness, not size, governs the channel.**

## Why the numbers behave: an addition law

Exact values are pleasant, but the real structure is a law. The type-pair channel is *additive over the Chinese Remainder Theorem*:
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(mn) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(m) + I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n) \qquad \text{whenever } \gcd(m,n) = 1.$$

The reason is beautifully clean. The splitting type is itself multiplicative — $T_{mn}(a) = T_m(a) \cdot T_n(a)$ for coprime $m,n$ — and the residue map $a \mapsto (a \bmod m,\ a \bmod n)$ turns the single box $\mathbb{Z}/mn \times \mathbb{Z}/mn$ into a *product* of two independent boxes. Information is additive across independent products, and the coprime factorisation of the type is unique, so nothing is lost or double-counted. The upshot: the entire channel is determined by its prime-power values,
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n) = \sum_{p \mid n} I_{\mathrm{pair}}\big(p^{v_p(n)}\big).$$

Check it against the table: $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(12) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(4) + I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3) = \tfrac54 + (\log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9})$, and indeed $\tfrac54 - \tfrac{10}{9} = \tfrac{5}{36}$. The doubling law $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2m) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(m) + 1$ for odd $m$ falls out immediately, because $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2) = 1$.

## The prime orders, in closed form

Once you know additivity, the prime orders become the atoms of the theory, and they too have an exact formula. For any prime $p$,
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p) = \log_2 p - \frac{(p-1)(2p-1)}{p^2}\log_2(p-1) + \frac{(p-1)(p-2)}{p^2}\log_2(p-2).$$

The derivation is a three-fibre count: with $n = p$ prime, an exponent is either $0$ (type $1$) or nonzero (type $p$), so the unordered pair takes only the three values $\{1,1\}$, $\{1,p\}$, $\{p,p\}$, with $1$, $2(p-1)$ and $(p-1)^2$ preimages in the $p \times p$ box. Conditioning on the sum $a + b$ splits the box into $p$ diagonals, each of which has to be counted separately according to whether the residue class is zero. The bookkeeping is finite and the answer collapses to the displayed expression.

Two consequences are worth stating loudly.

**The cap is attained at exactly one prime.** $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p) = 1$ if and only if $p = 2$. Every odd prime order sits strictly *below* one bit — $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3) = \log_2 3 - 10/9 \approx 0.4739$, $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(5) \approx 0.2027$, $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(7) \approx 0.1141$ — and in fact the whole prime channel decays like $O(1/p)$, with the explicit envelope $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p) \le (\log_2 p + 3p)/p^2$.

**Breaking the cap forces compositeness.** Since no prime order beats one bit, any cyclic order with $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(n) > 1$ must be composite. The above-cap phenomenon is, by its nature, a *compositional* one: it happens because independent primary parts add up.

## It happens infinitely often — and evenness is not the reason

The table is finite; the phenomenon is not. Combining additivity with the non-negativity of information gives an infinite family with no computation at all: for every odd $m$,
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(4m) = I_{\mathrm{pair}}(4) + I_{\mathrm{pair}}(m) \ge \tfrac54 > 1.$$
Since $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(m) \ge 0$ always, the $2$-primary part $4$ alone is enough, however complicated the rest of $m$ is. The same works for $2$-primary parts $8$ ($\ge 21/16$) and $16$ ($\ge 85/64$), and these orders are unbounded, so no finite computation could ever have settled the question.

It is tempting to conclude that an element of order two is what does the damage. It isn't. Adding up the small odd prime contributions — $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(9) \approx 0.5265$, plus the values at $5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, 29, 31$ — pushes the total past one bit while keeping the order odd:
$$M = 9 \cdot 5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 19\cdot 23\cdot 29\cdot 31 = 300\,840\,735\,195, \qquad I_{\mathrm{pair}}(M) > 1.$$
Breaking the cap is about accumulating enough independent primary parts, not about parity.

## A geometric ladder inside the powers of two

The prime-power values along a fixed prime are not arbitrary either; they form a geometric ladder. For $1 \le k \le 5$,
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(2^k) = \frac{4}{3}\left(1 - 4^{-k}\right) = \frac{4^k - 1}{3 \cdot 4^{k-1}},$$
giving $1, \tfrac54, \tfrac{21}{16}, \tfrac{85}{64}, \tfrac{341}{256}$ — strictly increasing, and every step from $k=2$ upwards above the cap, but always below the limiting value $4/3$. The increment from $2^k$ to $2^{k+1}$ is exactly $4^{-k}$. At the prime $3$ the identical shape appears: $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3^k) = (1 - 9^{-k}) \cdot \tfrac98 \cdot I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3)$ for $k \le 3$, with increments $9^{-k}\,I_{\mathrm{pair}}(3)$. The pattern strongly suggests the general prime-power law
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p^k) = \left(1 - p^{-2k}\right)\frac{p^2}{p^2-1}\,I_{\mathrm{pair}}(p),$$
which is a self-similarity statement: the $p^k$-box decomposes along the valuation filtration $v(a) = 0, 1, \dots, k$, and the conditional law at valuation level $j$ is a scaled copy of the level below.

## No help for the factorer

Does all this leaked information help anyone actually *factor* $N$? No, and the reason is itself a theorem. The type pair is unordered by construction, and the map that forgets the order costs *exactly the same* entropy whether or not you already know the product residue. Consequently the unordered pair channel equals the ordered pair channel: the **which-factor wall is exactly zero**. The leaked bits describe the pair symmetrically; they never distinguish $p$ from $q$. And there is a second wall: computing the type pair from $N$ requires knowing $N$ modulo $f$ only — which anyone can do — but *predicting* which pair occurs requires the two exponents individually, which is exactly what factoring would give you.

So the story is information-theoretic, not cryptanalytic. What it changes is the map of what a semiprime *is*. The classical picture — a symmetric fork of two binary choices, capped at one bit — is a shadow. The split-count projection that produces that shadow is recoverable from the richer object: for $n = 4$ it retains $19/8 - \tfrac{21}{16}\log_2 3 \approx 0.2947$ bits, and for $n = 6$ about $0.1487$ bits, in both cases a small fraction of the full type channel. **The classical split-count is one face of a wider polyhedron.**

## What the picture looks like now

Three sentences summarise the new state of affairs. First, the splitting type of a prime in a cyclic field is a multi-state, totient-distributed random variable, determined exactly and without loss by a single residue. Second, the information a semiprime's residue carries about the unordered pair of splitting types of its two factors is additive over coprime factorisations of the cyclic order, hence completely determined by prime-power atoms with a known closed form at $k = 1$ and a geometric ladder above it. Third, the one-bit ceiling that governs binary forks is not a law of nature but a symptom of binary readouts: the multi-state channel exceeds it at $n = 4$, at $n = 6$, on infinitely many orders, and — for anyone who suspected parity was the culprit — on odd orders too.

Sometimes a barrier turns out to be a wall. Sometimes, once you look at the right object, it turns out to have been a door.

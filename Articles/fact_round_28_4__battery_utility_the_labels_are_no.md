# The Dials That Measure Everything and Narrow Nothing

## A story about information, prime numbers, and the difference between knowing a quantity and knowing a thing

Imagine you are handed a very large number $N$ — say, six hundred digits long — and told only that it is the product of two enormous primes, $N = pq$. Your task is to find $p$. This is the factoring problem, the arithmetic obstacle on which a great deal of modern cryptography rests.

Now imagine that someone hands you an instrument. It is a panel of six dials. You feed $N$ into it, and each dial swings to a reading. The readings are not random: they are genuine arithmetic measurements of the hidden factors. Statisticians measure the panel and report a startling number: the six dials together carry **12.7 bits** of information about the hidden pair $(p, q)$. Twelve and a half bits is a factor of several thousand in "how much you know."

And then you sit down to use those bits — and discover that you cannot narrow the search for $p$ by so much as a single candidate.

This article is about *why*. The answer is not a defect in the instrument, nor bad luck, nor a subtler dial waiting to be built. It is a theorem: **the labels are not filters.**

---

## What the dials actually measure

Each dial is built the same way. Pick a small polynomial $f$ with integer coefficients — the workhorse examples are $f(X) = X^2 - 2$ and $f(X) = X^3 - 2$ — and ask a question about how $f$ behaves modulo the hidden prime $p$:

> **How many solutions does $f(x) \equiv 0 \pmod p$ have?**

That count is the dial's **label**. For $f(X) = X^3 - 2$ the label is
$$T(p) \;=\; \#\{\,x \in \mathbb{Z}/p : x^3 \equiv 2 \pmod p\,\}.$$
It is a small integer, and a deep invariant: it records the *splitting type* of the prime $p$ in the number field generated by $\sqrt[3]{2}$. Exactly the kind of thing a well-designed instrument can extract from $N$ indirectly, and exactly the kind of thing that ought to be useful.

What would "useful" mean? A search for $p$ proceeds by candidates. The cheapest way to cut a candidate list is by **residue class**: if you know $p \equiv 1 \pmod 9$, you have thrown away eight ninths of all integers at a stroke. So the dream is a *table*:

> Read the label. Look up which residues mod $m$ are compatible with it. Discard the rest.

Such a table is what we will call a **filter**. Its existence is a precise mathematical demand: it requires a function
$$g : \mathbb{Z}/m \longrightarrow \mathbb{N}, \qquad g(p \bmod m) = T(p) \quad \text{for every prime } p.$$
The label must be a *function of the residue*. Nothing less will do, because a residue class is all a residue-based search can see.

The whole story is whether that function exists.

---

## Half of the label is free, and the free half is useless

Start with the good news, because it is a genuinely pretty piece of arithmetic.

**Theorem (the abelian half).** *If $p$ is a prime with $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, then for **every** $c$ the congruence $x^3 \equiv c \pmod p$ has exactly one solution.*

The proof is a two-line trick. Because $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, the number $2(p-1)+1$ is divisible by $3$; write $3k = 2(p-1)+1$. Then for any $x$ in $\mathbb{Z}/p$,
$$(x^3)^k = x^{3k} = x^{2(p-1)+1} = \left(x^{p-1}\right)^2 \cdot x = x,$$
using Fermat's little theorem (and checking $x = 0$ by hand). So the $k$-th power map undoes cubing: cubing is a bijection of $\mathbb{Z}/p$, and every value — including $2$ — has exactly one cube root. The label is $1$, always.

What about the other primes? Here is the complement.

**Theorem (the non-abelian half).** *If $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ and $p \nmid c$, then $x^3 \equiv c \pmod p$ has either $0$ or exactly $3$ solutions.*

Why? If there are no solutions, fine. If there is one solution $a$, then the full solution set is $\{a\zeta : \zeta^3 = 1\}$, a translate of the group $\mu_3$ of cube roots of unity inside $\mathbb{Z}/p$. So the count equals $|\mu_3|$. That group has at most three elements, because a cubic polynomial over a field has at most three roots; and it has at least three, because $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ makes $3$ divide the order $p-1$ of the multiplicative group, so Cauchy's theorem supplies an element of order exactly $3$. Hence $|\mu_3| = 3$, and the label is $3$.

Put the two halves together and you get a complete anatomy of the dial. For every prime $p \ge 5$:
$$T(p) = 1 \iff p \equiv 2 \pmod 3, \qquad\text{and otherwise}\qquad T(p) \in \{0, 3\}.$$

So the label carries exactly two things. First, a **free bit**: whether $p$ is $1$ or $2$ mod $3$. That bit *is* a residue fact — it is a function of $p \bmod 3$ — so it is a perfectly good filter, and it is also a bit any residue-based search already had for free. Second, on the $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ side, a **hard bit**: is the label $0$ or $3$? Is $2$ a cube modulo $p$, or is it not?

Everything hinges on that second bit.

---

## The hard bit is not a function of the residue

Consider $p = 7$ and $p = 31$. Both are prime. Both are $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$. And:

- Modulo $7$, the cubes are $\{0, 1, 6\}$. The number $2$ is not among them, so $T(7) = 0$.
- Modulo $31$, we have $4^3 = 64 = 2 \cdot 31 + 2$, so $4^3 \equiv 2$. Hence $T(31) = 3$.

Now: $7$ and $31$ are congruent modulo $2$, modulo $3$, modulo $4$, modulo $6$, modulo $8$, modulo $12$, modulo $24$ — they differ by exactly $24$. In every one of those moduli they sit in the **same residue class** and carry **different labels**. A function $g$ with $g(7 \bmod m) = T(7)$ and $g(31 \bmod m) = T(31)$ would have to satisfy $g(r) = 0$ and $g(r) = 3$ for the same $r$. There is no such function.

One pair of primes kills a family of moduli. Feed in more pairs — $13$ and $43$, $31$ and $73$, $31$ and $97$, $79$ and $157$, $13$ and $127$, $43$ and $103$, $19$ and $157$, $7$ and $109$ — and you can cover every modulus in turn.

**Theorem (the labels are not filters).** *For every modulus $m$ with $2 \le m \le 24$ there is no map $g : \mathbb{Z}/m \to \mathbb{N}$ satisfying $g(p \bmod m) = T(p)$ for all primes $p \ge 5$ with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$. Each case is refuted by an explicit pair of primes sharing a residue class and carrying the labels $0$ and $3$.*

And the refutations propagate downward: if $d$ divides $m$ and a table existed for $d$, you could compose it with the projection $\mathbb{Z}/m \to \mathbb{Z}/d$ and get a table for $m$. So a refutation at $m$ automatically refutes every divisor of $m$. The finitely many checks fan out over a much larger set of moduli than the list suggests.

---

## Soundness is the trap

A sceptic will object: perhaps one does not need an exact table. Perhaps a *partial* filter suffices — a rule that, on reading the label, discards some residues and keeps others, even if imperfectly.

This objection has a precise refutation, and it is the sharpest result in the story. Call a rule $F$ — assigning to each possible label $t$ a set $F(t)$ of permitted residues mod $m$ — **sound** if it never discards the truth: for every prime $p$, the residue $p \bmod m$ lies in $F(T(p))$. Soundness is not optional. A search that throws away the real answer is not a faster search; it is a wrong one.

**Theorem (no pinning, at modulus 9).** *Every sound rule mod $9$ must accept all three residues $1, 4, 7$ for the label $0$ **and** all three for the label $3$.*

The proof is the witness pairs again, now read as a constraint rather than a contradiction. Take the class $7 \bmod 9$: the prime $7$ has label $0$ and the prime $43$ has label $3$, and $43 \equiv 7 \pmod 9$, so soundness forces the residue $7$ into $F(0)$ *and* into $F(3)$. The class $4 \bmod 9$ is pinned into both sets by the pair $13$ (label $0$) and $31$ (label $3$); the class $1 \bmod 9$ by the pair $19$ (label $0$) and $127$ (label $3$). Hence
$$\{1,4,7\} \subseteq F(0) \quad\text{and}\quad \{1,4,7\} \subseteq F(3),$$
so $|F(0) \cap F(3)| \ge 3$: the two labels have **identical residue support**. Whichever way the dial swings, the same three classes survive. The narrowing factor is exactly $1$.

Meanwhile the classes $2, 5, 8$ (the primes $\equiv 2 \bmod 3$, witnessed by $11$, $5$ and $17$) are all forced into $F(1)$, and by the anatomy theorem the label $1$ occurs nowhere else. So the *only* separation a sound filter can ever perform is $\{2,5,8\}$ versus $\{1,4,7\}$ — the value of $p \bmod 3$, which no one needed a dial to learn.

The same phenomenon holds at every modulus checked: for each $2 \le m \le 24$ there is a residue class accepted by both the label $0$ and the label $3$, so no dial reading removes it.

---

## What 12.7 bits were bits *of*

At this point there is an apparent paradox. The panel's information content was measured, honestly, at $12.7$ bits. If none of it narrows anything, where did it go?

It went into the *joint* draw. This is the corrected understanding, and it can be stated exactly.

Model a dial as a finite joint distribution on pairs (residue $R$, label $T$). Write $H(R)$, $H(T)$, $H(R,T)$ for the Shannon entropies, $H(T\mid R)$ for the conditional entropy of the label given the residue, and $I(R;T) = H(T) - H(T \mid R)$ for the mutual information — the bits the dial delivers *about the residue*. Three facts organize everything.

**The capacity law.** $H(T \mid R) \ge 0$ always, hence
$$I(R;T) \;\le\; H(T).$$
A dial never returns more than its own label entropy. This follows from the chain rule $H(R,T) = H(R) + H(T \mid R)$, where the conditional entropy is assembled from the pointwise terms $-a\log a + a \log s$ ($a$ a joint probability, $s$ the mass of its residue class), each nonnegative because $a \le s$.

**Filters saturate.** If the label *is* a function of the residue — precisely the table the utility programme demanded — then within each residue class only one label has positive mass, every pointwise term vanishes, $H(T\mid R) = 0$, and
$$I(R;T) = H(T).$$
The ceiling is attained exactly when the filter exists.

**The gap theorem.** Conversely, if even a single residue class carries two labels with positive probability, then that class contributes a strictly positive pointwise term (because then $a < s$ strictly, and $-a\log a + a \log s > 0$), so $H(T\mid R) > 0$ and
$$I(R;T) \;<\; H(T).$$

Read the last one backwards and it becomes a diagnostic of striking clarity: **any measured shortfall $I < H(T)$ is a certificate that no residue-to-label table exists.** The gap is not noise, not a limitation of the sample, not a sign that the dial was badly calibrated. The gap *is* the within-class variation. In the panel's cubic channel the numbers were $I = 1.0012$ against a ceiling of $H(T) = 2.2982$ — and now we know exactly what the missing $1.297$ bits were: the primes $7$ and $31$ refusing to agree.

The extreme case is worth spelling out. Suppose the label is statistically independent of the residue, so the joint law factors as a product. Then $I(R;T) = 0$ *while* $H(T) > 0$: the dial has a rich, high-entropy reading that says literally nothing about the residue. This is not a pathology; it is the expected behaviour for a non-abelian probe, where equidistribution makes the label frequencies constant across residue classes. In the natural model of the cubic dial — residues uniform on $\{1, 4, 7\} \bmod 9$, labels $0$ and $3$ occurring with densities $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$ independently of the residue — one computes
$$I(R;T) = 0, \qquad H(T) = \log 3 - \tfrac{2}{3}\log 2 > 0$$
(the positivity because $27 > 4$). The entire ceiling is within-class variation. And the posterior on any single residue class stays pinned at its prior value $\tfrac13$ (or $\tfrac19$ for a joint pair) no matter what the dials read: **no pinning**, in the literal Bayesian sense.

Real data behaves the same way, just less cleanly. Among the $21$ primes below $200$ with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, sorted by residue mod $9$ and by cubic label, the census is
$$\begin{array}{c|cc} p \bmod 9 & T = 0 & T = 3\\\hline 1 & 6 & 2\\ 4 & 5 & 2\\ 7 & 5 & 1 \end{array}$$
Every row is mixed. Every class carries both labels. Hence $H(T \mid R) > 0$ and $I < H(T)$ strictly — exactly the shape every dial of the panel reports.

## The control experiment: when the dial *does* work

A refutation is only as good as its controls, and this one has a beautiful one. Change the probe from $X^3 - 2$ to $X^2 - 2$ and everything reverses.

**Theorem (the quadratic label is a filter).** *For every odd prime $p$, the number of solutions of $x^2 \equiv 2 \pmod p$ is*
$$\#\{x : x^2 \equiv 2\} = \begin{cases} 2 & \text{if } p \equiv 1 \text{ or } 7 \pmod 8,\\ 0 & \text{if } p \equiv 3 \text{ or } 5 \pmod 8.\end{cases}$$

This is the second supplement to quadratic reciprocity, read as an engineering specification. The table exists. It has four rows. The lookup is instantaneous, and it is *sound and sharp*: reading the quadratic dial genuinely halves the candidate residues mod $8$.

So the verdict is not "labels are useless." It is sharper and more interesting:

> **Filterability is a statement about abelianness, not about bit count.**

The field generated by $\sqrt{2}$ is abelian over the rationals — indeed $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) \subset \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_8)$, sitting inside a cyclotomic field. By the great theorem of Kronecker and Weber, abelian extensions *are* the ones governed by congruence conditions, which is exactly why a mod-$8$ table can exist. The field generated by $\sqrt[3]{2}$ and a primitive cube root of unity has Galois group $S_3$, which is not abelian; its commutator subgroup is nontrivial; and the splitting behaviour of $X^3 - 2$ therefore cannot be periodic in $p$ under any modulus. The free bit that survived — $p \bmod 3$ — is precisely the abelian quotient of $S_3$ shining through: $S_3 \to S_3^{\mathrm{ab}} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, which is what the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ sees. Everything past the abelianization is invisible to congruences.

That is why adding more dials does not help. Twelve more bits from twelve more non-abelian probes are twelve more bits about the *joint* draw and zero more bits about a residue class. Extracting them as candidate narrowing would require, for each candidate prime, a determination of its splitting type — and determining the splitting type of a prime factor of $N$ that you have not yet found is the factoring problem itself. The argument closes in a circle, and the circle is exactly the point.

---

## The moral

This round began as an attempt to build a tool and ended as a theorem about why the tool cannot exist. That is a good trade. The bug that started it — tables built by asking "is $f(r) \equiv 0$?" of the *residue* $r$ rather than "what type is a prime lying in the class $r$?" — is a category error that is invisible until you check whether your filter ever excludes the true answer. It did, six times out of a hundred and fifty. The consistency check caught it. The diagnosis became the finding.

There is a general lesson here for anyone who works with measurements. Information is always information *about something*. A channel can have enormous capacity with respect to one variable and exactly zero with respect to another; the mutual information $I(R;T)$ and the entropy $H(T)$ are different numbers, and the gap between them is not waste — it is a structural fact about what your measurement is a measurement of. In this case the gap has a name in arithmetic: it is the non-abelianness of a cubic field, showing up in a statistics table as a shortfall of $1.297$ bits.

The open question left behind is clean, and the tools to settle it are nearly in place. **Conjecture:** for *every* modulus $m$, not merely $m \le 24$, there exist primes $p \equiv q \pmod m$, both $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$, with $x^3 \equiv 2$ unsolvable mod $p$ and solvable mod $q$. Equivalently, the cubic label is not periodic under any modulus whatsoever. The route is structural rather than computational: periodicity modulo $m$ would force the field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ into the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_m)$, hence force it to be abelian — and it is not, since it is not even normal over $\mathbb{Q}$. One needs only the abelian half of class field theory, not the full Chebotarev density theorem, to close the argument.

A second conjecture generalizes the picture: for any family of dials whose labels are class functions of the Frobenius in a non-abelian extension, the information about the residue vector is bounded by what the maximal abelian subextension carries — and *that bound is the entire utility*.

Until then, the panel keeps swinging, the twelve and a half bits keep arriving, and the candidate list stays exactly as long as it ever was. The dials are honest. They are measuring the wrong thing, and now we can prove it.

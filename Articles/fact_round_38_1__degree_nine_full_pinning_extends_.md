# Nine Roots, One Clock: How a Prime's Remainder Determines Its Fate

*A prime number dropped into a bigger number system breaks apart in a way that looks random. At degree nine we can say exactly how much of that randomness the prime's remainder already contains: all of it.*

---

## A prime walks into a bigger world

Take an ordinary prime number, say $p = 7$, and move it out of the integers into a larger number system. It may stay whole, or it may break into smaller prime pieces, much as a white light beam splits into colours in a prism. Number theorists call this the **splitting** of $p$. They have studied it since Gauss, because nearly every deep question about whole numbers comes down, sooner or later, to how primes split.

The number system in this story is the field generated by a single real number,

$$\alpha = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{19}\right) \approx 1.8916.$$

Its minimal polynomial has degree nine:

$$x^9 + x^8 - 8x^7 - 7x^6 + 21x^5 + 15x^4 - 20x^3 - 10x^2 + 5x + 1 .$$

Its nine roots are the numbers $2\cos(2\pi k/19)$ for $k = 1, \dots, 9$. Picture them as nine points spaced around a circle. The field they generate is written $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{19})^+$, the *maximal real subfield* of the 19th cyclotomic field. It has a very regular kind of symmetry: its symmetry group is the **cyclic group of order nine**, $C_9$, the group of a clock with nine hours. The symmetries never permute the roots arbitrarily. They only *rotate* them.

There is a concrete way to watch a prime $p$ split here. Reduce the degree-nine polynomial modulo $p$ and factor it over the finite field with $p$ elements. The degrees of the irreducible factors are the **factor pattern**. For $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{19})^+$ only three patterns ever occur:

- $[1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1]$: nine linear factors, so $p$ **splits completely** into nine primes;
- $[3,3,3]$: three cubic factors, so $p$ breaks into three primes of "degree three";
- $[9]$: the polynomial stays irreducible, so $p$ is **inert** and does not break at all.

The number appearing in the pattern (1, 3 or 9) is the **splitting type** $f(p)$ of the prime, its *residue degree*. For example, $p = 37$ splits completely, $p = 7$ splits into three cubics, and $p = 2$ stays inert.

## The surprise: a remainder knows everything

Factoring a degree-nine polynomial modulo $p$ takes real work. Yet the answer can be read off from something far cheaper: **the remainder of $p$ on division by 19**.

The rule is as follows. Take $r = p \bmod 19$ and square it. Then keep multiplying by $r^2$, modulo 19, until you come back to 1. The number of steps is the splitting type. Put more briefly, $f(p)$ is the multiplicative order of $p^2$ modulo 19. Equivalently, it is the order of $p$ once you agree to treat $+1$ and $-1$ as the same thing. That identification is exactly what passing from the full cyclotomic field to its *real* half does. The equivalence rests on one small fact about any field: an element whose square is 1 must be $+1$ or $-1$. So $(p^2)^f \equiv 1$ holds precisely when $p^f \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{19}$.

For example, $7^2 = 49 \equiv 11 \pmod{19}$, and $11^3 = 1331 = 70 \cdot 19 + 1 \equiv 1$. So $f(7) = 3$, and indeed the polynomial breaks into three cubics modulo 7.

That is the *deterministic* side of the story. The *probabilistic* side comes from two classical theorems. Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, and its far-reaching generalisation by Chebotarev, say that the remainders of primes modulo 19 are **uniformly distributed** over the 18 nonzero residues. As you range over all primes, each of $1, 2, \dots, 18$ turns up equally often in the long run. So a "random prime" behaves like a random point on an 18-hour clock, and its splitting type is a function of where it lands.

## Counting on a clock

Which hours of the 18-hour clock give which type? Here is the first theorem, stated for every clock of even size at once.

> **The Splitting-Type Density Law.** Let $G$ be a cyclic group of order $2m$. For every divisor $d$ of $m$, exactly $2\varphi(d)$ elements $g \in G$ have the property that $g^2$ has order $d$. Here $\varphi$ is Euler's totient function, which counts the numbers between 1 and $d$ that are coprime to $d$. Consequently a uniformly random element has "type" $d$ with probability $\varphi(d)/m$.

The idea of the proof fits on a napkin. Squaring is a two-to-one map on a cyclic group of even order: exactly two elements, $1$ and the unique element of order two, square to 1, and every value of the squaring map is hit by exactly two elements. The elements that occur as squares are exactly those killed by $m$, and a cyclic group has exactly $\varphi(d)$ elements of each order $d$. Multiply these counts and you get $2\varphi(d)$.

For the 19-clock ($m = 9$, divisors $1, 3, 9$) this gives:

| type $f$ | pattern | how many residues | density |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $[1^9]$ | $2\varphi(1) = 2$ (namely $\pm 1$) | $1/9$ |
| 3 | $[3,3,3]$ | $2\varphi(3) = 4$ (namely $\pm 7, \pm 8$) | $2/9$ |
| 9 | $[9]$ | $2\varphi(9) = 12$ | $6/9$ |

These are predictions about actual primes, and the primes follow them. An experiment ran through 295,946 primes that do not divide 19. The observed frequencies of the three types matched $1/9$, $2/9$ and $6/9$ to within about $2 \times 10^{-4}$.

## Measuring surprise in bits

How unpredictable is the splitting type? Claude Shannon gave the standard measure: the **entropy**

$$H(T) = -\sum_t P(T=t)\,\log_2 P(T=t),$$

measured in bits. One bit is the uncertainty of a fair coin flip. For our three densities,

$$H(T) = \tfrac19\log_2 9 + \tfrac29\log_2\tfrac92 + \tfrac69\log_2\tfrac96 = \tfrac43\log_2 3 - \tfrac89 \approx 1.22439 \text{ bits}.$$

That closed form is exact, and the decimal can be certified by elementary means. The two integer inequalities $2^{1054} < 3^{665}$ and $3^{306} < 2^{485}$ pin $\log_2 3$ between $1054/665$ and $485/306$. That is enough to prove

$$1.22439 < H(T) < 1.22441.$$

The experiment had measured "1.2244 bits". The two agree.

## Full pinning: the remainder leaves nothing out

Now the headline. **Mutual information** $I(X;Y)$ measures how many bits of uncertainty about $Y$ disappear once you learn $X$. It is never more than $H(Y)$. When it equals $H(Y)$ we say $X$ **pins** $Y$: knowing $X$ removes *all* doubt about $Y$.

> **Full Pinning Theorem.** For every odd prime $\ell$, and in particular at degree nine,
> $$I(p \bmod \ell\,;\,T) = H(T).$$
> At degree nine both sides equal $\tfrac43\log_2 3 - \tfrac89 \approx 1.2244$ bits. In fact $p^2 \bmod \ell$ alone already pins $T$.

The proof is short once the setting is right. Mutual information equals entropy exactly when the second quantity is a *function* of the first, and we have just seen that $T$ is a function of $p \bmod \ell$. The interesting part is the refinement. Of the $\log_2 18 \approx 4.17$ bits in the full remainder, only the part that survives squaring matters. The sign, meaning whether you look at $r$ or $-r$, is pure **thickening**: information the remainder carries that has nothing to do with how $p$ splits. The experiment confirmed this side as well. Within each residue class the type never varied, and a control statistic built from a modulus coprime to 19 carried no information about the type at all.

## Rungs of a ladder

Degree nine is one rung of a longer ladder. For each odd prime $\ell$, the field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\ell)^+$ is cyclic of degree $m = (\ell-1)/2$, and the same two theorems hold. The densities are $\varphi(d)/m$ for each divisor $d$ of $m$. The entropy is the **totient entropy**

$$H_\varphi(m) = -\sum_{d \mid m} \frac{\varphi(d)}{m}\log_2\frac{\varphi(d)}{m}.$$

Full pinning holds on every rung. Cyclic fields of degrees 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8 and 9 have now all been checked against real primes, and every one of them behaved exactly as predicted, with no exceptions. For the fields $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\ell)^+$ these are no longer separate coincidences. They are all instances of one theorem about cyclic groups, which covers every odd prime $\ell$ at once.

The totient entropy has a pleasant property of its own:

> **Additivity.** If $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, then $H_\varphi(mn) = H_\varphi(m) + H_\varphi(n)$.

This is a number-theoretic fact turning into an information-theoretic one. Euler's $\varphi$ is *multiplicative* on coprime arguments, and so the entropy is *additive*. In field terms, a cyclic field of degree $mn$ contains cyclic subfields of degrees $m$ and $n$, and the splitting type in the big field is an *independent pair* of the types in the two smaller fields. One consequence: the rung at $\ell = 37$, of degree $18 = 2 \times 9$, sits exactly one bit above the degree-nine rung, because $H_\varphi(2) = 1$ bit. The extra bit is the toss-up between splitting and staying inert in the quadratic subfield $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{37})$.

## When counting roots is not enough

There is a cautionary tale in this round. Checking the theory against actual polynomial factorisations, the first idea was to count **roots modulo $p$**: how many of the nine roots exist in the finite field? That count turned out to be blind to the difference that matters.

The reason is geometric. The nine roots sit on a nine-hour clock, and the Frobenius symmetry of the prime $p$ acts on them by **rotation**. A rotation either moves every point or moves none. And when a rotation by $h$ hours has order $o$, every orbit has exactly $o$ points. So:

- the **factor pattern** is the cycle structure of the rotation, $[o, o, \dots, o]$ with $9/o$ entries, and that is *exactly* the type;
- the **root count** is the number of fixed points of the rotation, which is 9 for the identity rotation and 0 for every other one.

At degree nine a rotation of order 3 and a rotation of order 9 both fix zero roots, so counting roots cannot tell them apart. The first version of the cross-check had assumed an order-3 Frobenius fixes some roots. It fixes none. Switching to factor patterns repaired the check, and all 400 test primes agreed with the predicted type.

The general statement is neat:

> **Fixed-Root Dichotomy.** On the rung of degree $m$, the number of roots modulo $p$ determines the splitting type if and only if $m = 1$ or $m$ is prime.

If $m$ is prime, the only orders available are 1 and $m$, and the root count separates them. If $m$ is composite, it has a divisor $d$ strictly between 1 and $m$. Types $d$ and $m$ then both occur, and both give zero roots. So degree nine is not a special case. Root counting is lossy at *every* composite degree (4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …) and lossless at every prime degree. At degree nine the root count carries only

$$H\!\left(\tfrac19, \tfrac89\right) \approx 0.503 \text{ bits},$$

which is less than half of the 1.224 bits that are actually there to be read.

## Two primes at once

The last part of the story concerns **semiprimes** $N = pq$. Suppose we see only $N \bmod 19$, not $p$ or $q$. What can we learn about the splitting types of the two hidden factors?

Two clean facts emerge.

First, **the which-factor extra is exactly zero.** Knowing $N$ tells you just as much about the *ordered* pair of types $(T(p), T(q))$ as about the *unordered* pair $\{T(p), T(q)\}$. The reason is symmetry: multiplication in an abelian group does not care about order, so $pq = qp$. Swapping the two factors changes nothing $N$ can see, and the label "first factor" carries no recoverable information. The experiment measured an "extra" of 0.00053 bits, which is statistical noise around an exact zero. Under the uniform model the information itself, computed by exact enumeration over all $18 \times 18$ residue pairs, is $0.52650$ bits.

Second, there is the **split-count law**. How many of the two factors split completely: none, one, or both? This count can be compared with the cruder question "does *at least one* factor split?" (call it the OR-dial). For any group of order $n$, the split count carries exactly

$$I(N;\#\text{split}) = I_s(n) + \eta\!\left(\tfrac1{n^2}\right) + \eta\!\left(\tfrac{2(n-1)}{n^2}\right) - \eta\!\left(\tfrac{2n-1}{n^2}\right),\qquad \eta(x) = -x\log_2 x,$$

where $I_s(n)$ is the information in the OR-dial. The trick is that, once $N$ is known, "at least one splits" and "how many split" determine each other: if $N$ is the identity class then both must split, and otherwise exactly one does. So the only difference is how finely the bare count partitions the outcomes, and the correction is strictly positive. At degree nine, $I_s(9) \approx 0.0060$ bits, while the split count carries $\approx 0.0738$ bits.

## Why this matters

Splitting primes looks like a problem from the depths of algebraic number theory. Underneath, at least in the abelian setting, it is a counting problem on a clock. Dirichlet's and Chebotarev's theorems turn primes into uniformly random clock positions. Then group theory says exactly what each position implies. Information theory is a good language for the result because it states it so cleanly: *the remainder contains all the information, the sign contains none, and counting roots loses information precisely when the degree is composite.*

The same viewpoint makes the next steps easy to state. In non-abelian fields Frobenius is a conjugacy class rather than a single element, and splitting can no longer be described by congruence conditions alone; how much of their splitting behaviour a residue can still capture is open territory. For the abelian ladder, every rung measured so far has fit the theory with no exceptions, and the theorems show why that has to happen on every rung.

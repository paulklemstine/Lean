# Surreal Topology: Open Sets at Infinity

## A line too large for sequences

The ordinary real line is one of mathematics’ most familiar landscapes. Around every point, we can place successively smaller intervals: radius $1$, then $1/2$, then $1/3$, and so on. No matter how tiny a neighborhood we are later handed, one interval in that list fits inside it. This countable shrinking schedule is so natural that it is easy to mistake it for a universal feature of continuous space.

Conway’s surreal numbers show otherwise.

The surreal number line contains the real numbers, but it also contains infinitely large numbers greater than every integer and positive infinitesimals smaller than every positive real. More radically, it has room below *every countable list* of positive surreal numbers. Given positive surreals $u_0,u_1,u_2,\ldots$, there is another positive surreal $d$ satisfying

$$
0<d<u_n\qquad\text{for every }n\in\mathbb N.
$$

This single fact transforms the topology of the line. It means that no sequence can probe all scales near zero. However aggressively one chooses a countable list of positive sizes, the surreal line supplies a still smaller positive size beneath the entire list.

This article develops the consequences for the **order topology**: the topology generated by open intervals and open rays. The principal results are that the surreal line is Hausdorff, not first-countable, not metrizable, disconnected, and noncompact. Two questions suggested by the broader program—the extension of arbitrary real open sets and paracompactness—remain unresolved without additional structure, and we will state precisely what is missing.

## The interval topology

Let $\mathbf{No}$ denote a fixed universe-sized collection of surreal numbers, ordered in the usual way. Its order topology is generated by intervals

$$
(a,b)=\{x\in\mathbf{No}:a<x<b\}
$$

and the rays $(-\infty,b)$ and $(a,\infty)$. A set is open if each of its points lies in one of these basic intervals or rays contained in the set.

This topology is **Hausdorff**: if $x<y$, choose some $z$ with $x<z<y$; then $(-\infty,z)$ and $(z,\infty)$ separate $x$ from $y$. Thus distinct surreal numbers can be distinguished by disjoint neighborhoods, just as distinct real numbers can.

The resemblance soon ends.

A space is **first-countable at a point** if there is a countable family $B_0,B_1,B_2,\ldots$ of neighborhoods of that point such that every neighborhood contains at least one $B_n$. It is first-countable if this holds at every point. On the real line, the intervals $(-1/n,1/n)$ form such a family at zero. First countability is the mechanism that lets sequences describe closure, continuity, and convergence.

## The diagonal infinitesimal

The key theorem is a countable lower-bound principle.

**Countable Infinitesimal Theorem.** For every sequence $(u_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$ of positive surreal numbers, there exists a positive surreal number $d$ such that $d<u_n$ for every $n$.

The construction uses Conway’s cut notation. Form the surreal number

$$
d=\{0\mid u_0,u_1,u_2,\ldots\}.
$$

The left options consist only of $0$, while every $u_n$ is a right option. Because $0<u_n$ for all $n$, this is a legitimate numerical cut. By the defining order properties of a surreal cut, $0<d$ and $d<u_n$ for every $n$.

For familiar real numbers the analogous statement is false. Take $u_n=1/(n+1)$. No positive real lies below all $u_n$. The surreal line fills that apparent gap with a new infinitesimal.

A small companion observation links this order-theoretic fact to topology.

**Positive-Point Lemma.** Every neighborhood of zero contains a positive surreal number.

Indeed, an order-topological neighborhood of zero contains a basic interval extending to the right of zero. Density of the surreal order—or, equivalently, a simple cut construction—provides a positive point in that interval.

These two statements power a diagonal argument against every proposed countable neighborhood basis.

## Why no countable microscope can work

Suppose, for contradiction, that $B_0,B_1,B_2,\ldots$ were a countable neighborhood basis at zero. From each $B_n$, choose a positive point $u_n\in B_n$. The Countable Infinitesimal Theorem supplies $d>0$ with $d<u_n$ for all $n$.

Now consider the open ray

$$
U=(-\infty,d).
$$

Since $0<d$, this is a neighborhood of zero. Yet no $B_n$ is contained in $U$, because $u_n$ belongs to $B_n$ while $u_n>d$. This contradicts the defining property of a neighborhood basis.

We have therefore proved the central result.

**Non-First-Countability Theorem.** The surreal line with its order topology is not first-countable.

The argument is more than a technical obstruction. It says that a countable sequence of “zoom levels” can never exhaust the local geometry. After every countable calibration, there remains an unregistered scale.

A standard consequence follows immediately.

**Non-Metrizability Theorem.** The surreal order topology is not induced by any metric.

Every metric space is first-countable: at a point $x$, the balls of radii $1,1/2,1/3,\ldots$ form a countable neighborhood basis. Since the surreal topology lacks such a basis at zero, no distance function can generate it. One may still compare surreal numbers algebraically, but no ordinary metric captures exactly which sets are open.

## A clopen frontier

The surreal line also splits naturally into two topological regions. Define the **finite-surreal region** by

$$
F=\{x\in\mathbf{No}: x<n\text{ for some }n\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

This terminology is one-sided: $F$ includes every negative surreal, however large in magnitude, because every negative number is below some natural number. What matters here is boundedness above by a natural number.

The set $F$ is open, since

$$
F=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}(-\infty,n),
$$

and each ray in the union is open.

Less obvious is that $F$ is also closed. Suppose $x\notin F$. Then $x\ge n$ for every natural number $n$. The interval $(x-1,\infty)$ is a neighborhood of $x$. If some $y$ in that interval belonged to $F$, then $y<n$ for some $n$. Since $x<n+1$ would follow from $x<y+1<n+1$, we would contradict the fact that $x$ dominates every natural number. Hence $(x-1,\infty)$ lies in the complement of $F$, so the complement is open.

The region is nonempty because $0<1$, and it is proper because there exists a surreal number $M$ greater than every natural number. One may construct such an $M$ by a cut with all natural numbers on the left; taking a slightly larger surreal ensures strict inequalities.

Thus we obtain the following separation result.

**Clopen Finite-Region Theorem.** The set $F$ is nonempty, proper, open, and closed in the surreal order topology.

A topological space is **connected** if it cannot be divided into two disjoint nonempty open sets. A nonempty proper clopen set gives exactly such a division, namely $F$ and its complement. Therefore:

**Disconnectedness Theorem.** The surreal line with its order topology is disconnected.

This is a striking contrast with the real line. Although the surreal order has points between points, order density alone does not guarantee topological connectedness. The infinite scale creates a frontier that is invisible from either side: the finite side and the infinitely large side are both open.

## Why compactness fails

A space is **compact** if every open cover has a finite subcover. For linearly ordered spaces, compactness would in particular force the whole space to have extreme points under mild standard arguments. The surreal line has neither a greatest nor a least element: from any $x$, the numbers $x+1$ and $x-1$ lie strictly above and below it.

There is also a direct cover intuition. The family of rays

$$
\{(-\infty,a):a\in\mathbf{No}\}
$$

covers the entire surreal line, because every $x$ lies below $x+1$. Any finite selection has a largest endpoint $a$, and then a point greater than $a$ is uncovered. Hence:

**Noncompactness Theorem.** The surreal line with its order topology is not compact.

Noncompactness here is not merely ordinary unboundedness measured in real units. The surreal line continually creates new room beyond every finite collection of proposed bounds.

## What numerical experiments can—and cannot—show

Finite computation cannot instantiate the full surreal class or witness the failure of every countable basis. It can, however, display the diagonal logic on finite lists. Given positive rational numbers $u_0,\ldots,u_{N-1}$, the choice

$$
d=\frac12\min_{0\le n<N}u_n
$$

satisfies $0<d<u_n$ for all listed values. If each $u_n$ is selected from a candidate neighborhood $B_n$, then the ray $(-\infty,d)$ defeats all finitely many candidates at once. In the reals this procedure cannot survive an infinite list tending to zero; in the surreals, Conway’s cut construction does survive the entire countable list. The finite calculation is therefore an analogy for the selection-and-diagonal pattern, not a numerical proof of the surreal theorem.

Likewise, a finite sample can illustrate why finitely many initial rays never cover an unbounded ordered line: choose a point larger than their largest endpoint. The genuine theorem says that this escape remains possible in the full surreal order.

## The unfinished horizon

Two goals in the motivating program remain open in the present framework.

First, one would like every real open set to arise as the trace of a surreal-open set. A natural route begins with an ordered embedding $\iota:\mathbb R\to\mathbf{No}$. If this embedding is topological, then for a real open set $U$ one can consider the surreal union

$$
\bigcup_{(a,b)\subseteq U}(\iota(a),\iota(b)).
$$

Its inverse image should be $U$. Completing this argument requires a canonical embedding of all real numbers into the chosen surreal universe together with a proof that the embedding preserves the relevant topology. Those ingredients are not assumed here, so the extension theorem is a precise future target rather than an established result.

Second, the paracompactness question remains undecided. A space is **paracompact** if every open cover admits a locally finite open refinement. The clopen finite-region split hints that finer clopen partitions by infinitesimal scales may organize covers effectively. On the other hand, the enormous hierarchy of cofinality scales may obstruct local finiteness. Either direction requires new work.

Other natural goals include transferring non-first-countability from zero to every point via translations, proving stronger forms of disconnectedness such as total disconnectedness, and tracking how the results depend on the universe of surreal numbers under consideration.

## A new picture of continuity

The surreal line is orderly but not tame. It separates points cleanly, yet no metric describes its open sets. It has intervals and dense order, yet it falls apart into clopen regions. It supports local neighborhoods, yet no countable list can catalogue them. It stretches beyond every finite bound, and inside every countable descent it opens another infinitesimal level.

The governing idea is simple enough to fit in one cut:

$$
\{0\mid u_0,u_1,u_2,\ldots\}.
$$

From that cut comes a topology at infinity—and at the infinitesimal edge of zero—that behaves unlike the spaces built from ordinary measurement. The surreal numbers do not merely add exotic points to the real line. They change what it means to zoom in, to remain connected, and to cover a space.

# The Braid That Wasn't There

## What Pythagorean triples can — and cannot — tell us about quantum computers

### An old tree and a new machine

Around 1934, a Dutch–Swedish thread of number theory produced one of the most elegant facts in elementary mathematics: **every** primitive Pythagorean triple — every triple of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and no common factor — can be reached from the single triple $(3,4,5)$ by repeatedly applying three fixed integer matrices, and can be reached in exactly one way. The triples are not a scattered dust of coincidences. They are the nodes of an infinite, perfectly regular **ternary tree**, rooted at $(3,4,5)$, in which every node has exactly three children.

The three matrices — usually called the Berggren generators, after B. Berggren who found them — are

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\ 2&-1&2\\ 2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\ 2&1&2\\ 2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\ -2&1&2\\ -2&2&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Apply them to the column vector $(3,4,5)$ and you get $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$. Apply them again and you get nine more triples, then twenty-seven, and so on forever, each triple appearing exactly once.

Now jump forward eighty years, to a completely different corner of science. **Topological quantum computation** is a proposal — due to Freedman, Kitaev, and Wang, among others — for building a quantum computer that is intrinsically protected from noise. The idea is breathtaking. Certain exotic two-dimensional materials are predicted to host quasiparticles called **anyons**, whose defining property is that swapping two of them does not merely permute them: it applies a unitary transformation to the quantum state, and *which* transformation you get depends on the topology of the paths the particles traced while swapping. The world-lines of the anyons form a **braid** in three-dimensional spacetime, and the computation is the braid. Because a braid cannot be undone by a small wiggle, the computation is immune to small errors. It is error correction built into geometry.

For this to be a *universal* computer — able to approximate any quantum operation you like — one thing must be true: the set of unitary matrices you can build by braiding must be **dense** in the full group of unitaries. You need to be able to get arbitrarily close to every gate. That density condition is the whole ballgame.

### The temptation

Put these two pictures side by side and something starts to shimmer.

The Berggren matrices preserve the quadratic form $a^2+b^2-c^2$. That form has signature $(2,1)$; its symmetry group is the Lorentz group of a $(2{+}1)$-dimensional spacetime, and that group is famously isomorphic (up to a two-fold cover) to $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$. So the Berggren generators are secretly $2\times 2$ matrices in disguise. Indeed they are, and the disguise is Euclid's own: writing a triple in Euclid's parametrisation
$$
(a,b,c) = (m^2-n^2,\; 2mn,\; m^2+n^2),
$$
each three-dimensional Berggren generator is the *second symmetric power* of a $2\times 2$ integer matrix acting on the parameters $(m,n)$. Explicitly,
$$
U_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U_2=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix},
$$
with $(m,n)\mapsto(2m-n,m)$, $(m,n)\mapsto(2m+n,m)$, $(m,n)\mapsto(m+2n,n)$ reproducing $A$, $B$, $C$ exactly. The tree of Pythagorean triples is a tree of $2\times 2$ integer matrices.

Braid groups famously have representations inside $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$. The three-strand braid group $B_3$, generated by $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ subject to the single **Artin relation**
$$
\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1=\sigma_2\sigma_1\sigma_2,
$$
surjects onto $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ by $\sigma_1\mapsto T=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_2\mapsto L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}$. And the boundary of the ternary tree — the set of infinite paths $A B C C A B\ldots$ down through the branches — is a Cantor set, exactly the kind of space on which anyons like to live as marked points.

Then there is the spectral coincidence. Look at the middle generator $U_2$. Its characteristic polynomial is $x^2-2x-1$; its eigenvalues are $1\pm\sqrt2$, the **silver ratio**. Its square $W=U_2^2=\begin{pmatrix}5&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3\pm 2\sqrt2$, the fundamental unit of the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. And $\sqrt2 = 2\cos(\pi/4)$ is precisely the loop parameter of the **Ising anyon** model — the most famous non-abelian anyon, the one people hope to find in Majorana wires.

So: a Lorentz-preserving group of integer matrices, a Cantor-set boundary, and $\sqrt 2$ sitting in the spectrum. It is hard *not* to conjecture that Pythagorean triples secretly compute.

This article is about what happens when you make that conjecture precise and then actually check it. The answer is *no* — and the "no" turns out to be far more interesting than a "yes" would have been vague.

### The first obstruction: the generators refuse to braid

The most direct version of the dream is: send $\sigma_1\mapsto U_1$ and $\sigma_2\mapsto U_2$ and you have a braid representation. Just multiply and see:
$$
U_1U_2U_1=\begin{pmatrix}8&-3\\5&-2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
U_2U_1U_2=\begin{pmatrix}8&5\\3&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
They share a top-left entry and nothing else — indeed they do not even have the same determinant, since $\det U_1=1$ while $\det U_2=-1$. Nor does any other pair braid. In fact:

> **Theorem (Failure of the Artin relation).** For every pair of distinct Berggren generators $U_i \neq U_j$, both $U_iU_jU_i \neq U_jU_iU_j$ and $U_iU_j \neq U_jU_i$. No two tree steps satisfy either an Artin braid relation or a commutation relation.

That kills the naive map, but not the dream: a clever representation of $B_3$ might send $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ to *words* in the generators rather than single letters. To rule that out you need something structural, and the tree hands it to you.

Reduce the three lifted generators modulo $2$:
$$
U_1 \equiv U_2 \equiv \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} =: J, \qquad U_3\equiv \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \pmod 2 .
$$
Since $J^2=I$, *every* product of Berggren generators reduces mod $2$ to either $I$ or $J$ — never to anything else. The Berggren group therefore lands inside a proper congruence-type subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$, and it comes equipped with a **charge**: a homomorphism to $\mathbb{Z}/2$ sending an element to $0$ if it reduces to the identity and $1$ if it reduces to $J$. In terms of paths in the tree, the charge of a word is beautifully simple.

> **Theorem (Charge = letter parity).** The $\mathbb{Z}/2$ charge of a Berggren word is the parity of the number of $A$-steps and $B$-steps it contains. The $C$-branch is neutral; the $A$- and $B$-branches each flip the charge.

This little parity invariant is the whole obstruction. Suppose $x,y$ are *any* two elements of the Berggren group satisfying the Artin relation $xyx=yxy$. Take charges: the left-hand side has charge $2q(x)+q(y)=q(y)$ (because $2q(x)=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2$), the right-hand side has charge $q(x)$. So $q(x)=q(y)$, always.

> **Theorem (Charge is a braid invariant).** In any representation of the three-strand braid group whose image lies in the Berggren group, the two Artin generators carry the same $\mathbb{Z}/2$ charge. The two charge sectors of the tree can never be exchanged by a braid.

That is the precise death of non-abelian statistics here. Non-abelian anyons are interesting exactly because braiding *mixes* internal states. The Berggren tree has an internal $\mathbb{Z}/2$ label, and braiding is provably blind to it. As a consequence, no representation of $B_3$ can send the two Artin generators to two *distinct* Berggren generators — in any of the six possible assignments — because $A$ and $B$ have charge $1$ while $C$ has charge $0$, and even the charge-matched pair $(A,B)$ fails the relation numerically.

There is a punchy corollary. The classical braid generators $T$ and $L$ of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ *do* satisfy $TLT=LTL$; but $T \bmod 2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ is neither $I$ nor $J$. So $T$ is not a Berggren element, and neither is $L$. **The braid generators of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ live outside the Pythagorean tree.** In particular the Berggren group is a proper subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ — it misses the braiding entirely.

What the tree *does* support is the abelian, fermionic representation $\sigma_i \mapsto -1$: a single sign, exchanging two particles multiplies the wavefunction by $-1$. Its image is the group $\{\pm 1\}$ of order $2$. That is a perfectly respectable anyon theory. It is also as far from universal quantum computation as it is possible to be.

### The second obstruction: integers are too rigid to be dense

Even granting a braid action for the sake of argument, universality asks for *density* in a unitary group. Here the tree fails for a reason so simple it is almost brutal.

Every gate the Berggren group could ever produce has **integer entries**. The set of complex matrices with integer entries is a closed, discrete subset of all matrices: there is no way to sneak up on a non-integer by a sequence of integers. So the closure of the Berggren representation consists entirely of integer matrices. But the humble phase gate
$$
S=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$
is unitary and is manifestly not integral. It is therefore not in the closure — not approximable to *any* accuracy.

> **Theorem (Integrality forbids universality).** No set of complex $2\times 2$ matrices with integer entries has the phase gate in its closure. In particular the Berggren representation is not dense in $U(2)$, and not even dense in the space of all $2\times2$ complex matrices.

There is a sharper way to say what goes wrong. Which integer matrices are unitary at all? Exactly the **signed permutation matrices**, and in dimension $2$ there are precisely eight of them: $\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\pm\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$. So *any* group of integer matrices meets the unitary group in a group of order at most $8$ — a Pauli-sized corner of $U(2)$, never a dense subgroup.

And in the Berggren group specifically, the interesting element is hyperbolic, which is the opposite of unitary. The spine element $W = U_2^2$ has eigenvalues $3\pm2\sqrt2$; its powers grow, with the top-left entry of $W^n$ at least $5^n$. A unitary matrix has bounded entries. Hence **no positive power of the Berggren $B$-generator is ever unitary**. Hyperbolic elements stretch; anyonic braidings rotate. They are different animals.

### What the tree really is

Strip away the failed conjecture and what remains is a genuinely lovely object, and now we know it exactly.

**The boundary is a Cantor set with a $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading.** Infinite paths down the tree form the space of infinite words in $\{A,B,C\}$ — the $3$-adic Cantor set. Prepending a letter is an injective map; the three prepending maps have pairwise disjoint images, and together they cover the whole boundary. This is the self-similarity: the boundary is three shrunken copies of itself. It is uncountable, so it is a true continuum of ends, not a countable orbit. And it carries the parity charge described above. That $\mathbb{Z}/2$ grading is the entire topological content of the Berggren action — the only "anyon charge" the tree can carry.

**The spectrum is silver.** The $B$-spine — take the $B$-branch forever — starts at $(3,4,5)$ and produces hypotenuses $5, 29, 169, 985, 5741,\ldots$, satisfying
$$
c_{n+2}=6c_{n+1}-c_n,
$$
which is the Cayley–Hamilton relation $W^2=6W-I$ of the spine matrix, which is the minimal equation of the Pell unit $3+2\sqrt2$. The growth is exponential and pinched:
$$
5^{\,n+1}\le c_n \le 5\cdot 6^{\,n}.
$$
These hypotenuses are the Pell-related numbers; $169=13^2$ and $985$ and $5741$ are the near-isosceles Pythagorean triples $(119,120,169)$, $(696,697,985)$ — the classical "almost-square" right triangles.

**Depth is pinched between a logarithm and a square root.** If you insist on reading the tree as a computational device, the natural cost of a triple is its **depth**: how many steps from the root. Two clean bounds hold for every word $p$ of length $d$ reaching a triple of hypotenuse $c$:
$$
(d+2)^2 < c \le 5\cdot 9^{\,d}.
$$
Equivalently, $\log_9(c/5) \le d < \sqrt{c}-2$. Both ends are achieved inside the tree: the $A$-spine gives $(3,4,5)\to(5,12,13)\to(7,24,25)\to\cdots$ with hypotenuse exactly $(d+2)^2+(d+1)^2$, saturating the quadratic lower bound; the $B$-spine grows exponentially and saturates the other. So the tree contains both the slowest possible and the fastest possible growth simultaneously.

### The consolation prize, and why it also fails

There is one last, very tempting move. The Berggren spectral field is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, and $\sqrt2=2\cos(\pi/4)$ is the Ising loop parameter. So even if the tree's *own* matrices do not braid, perhaps its *number field* does. It does! Set
$$
\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\pi/4}&0\\0&e^{-i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathcal{B}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\ i&1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1+iX}{\sqrt 2}.
$$
Both are unitary, both have order $8$, and one checks directly that $\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}$. This is a genuine two-dimensional unitary representation of the three-strand braid group, built entirely out of $\sqrt2$: the Ising anyon braiding.

And it is still not universal — for a third, entirely different reason. Compute how these gates act on the Pauli matrices by conjugation:
$$
\mathcal{A}X\mathcal{A}^\dagger=-Y,\quad \mathcal{A}Y\mathcal{A}^\dagger=X,\quad \mathcal{A}Z\mathcal{A}^\dagger=Z;
$$
$$
\mathcal{B}X\mathcal{B}^\dagger=X,\quad \mathcal{B}Y\mathcal{B}^\dagger=-Z,\quad \mathcal{B}Z\mathcal{B}^\dagger=Y .
$$
Each generator permutes the six signed Paulis. That is exactly the definition of a **Clifford gate**. Products of Clifford gates are Clifford, the set of Clifford matrices is closed, and the $\pi/8$ gate $T=\mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})$ is unitary but *not* Clifford — conjugating $X$ by it produces an off-diagonal entry with real part $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, which no signed Pauli has. So the closure of the Ising braid group misses $T$ and is not dense in $U(2)$.

This is the formal version of a fact quantum-computing researchers know well: **Ising anyons are not universal by braiding alone.** The Gottesman–Knill theorem says Clifford circuits can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer. The braiding of Ising anyons, however beautiful, is classically simulable. You need a non-Clifford resource — a magic state, a measurement, a topological charge projection — from outside the braid group.

### Why a negative result is worth having

Three independent walls, each of a different character:

1. **Arithmetic.** A mod-$2$ parity invariant that braiding must preserve, forcing abelian statistics.
2. **Topological/metric.** Integrality is a closed condition; discreteness and density are incompatible.
3. **Algebraic.** Even the correct $\sqrt2$-braiding is Clifford, hence classically simulable.

Each of these is a theorem about a much larger class of objects than Pythagorean triples. The first says: any arithmetic group with a surjection onto an abelian quotient will fail to carry non-abelian braiding through that quotient. The second says: *no* group of integer matrices, in any dimension by the same argument, can be a universal gate set — universality requires transcendence, or at least irrationality, in the entries. The third says: the presence of the right number field is necessary but nowhere near sufficient; $\sqrt 2$ gets you the Ising category, and the Ising category is Clifford.

The moonshot asked whether the oldest mathematics could build the newest machine. It cannot — and the reason is structural rather than accidental, which means we have learned something about what universality actually demands. A universal topological quantum computer needs a braid representation whose image is Zariski-dense *and* whose entries generate a field big enough to be dense in the circle: the golden ratio $\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}$ of the Fibonacci anyon theory, not the silver ratio $1+\sqrt2$ of the Pythagorean tree. It is a matter of one unit in one real quadratic field.

The Berggren tree, meanwhile, is not diminished. It remains the complete, non-redundant enumeration of every right triangle with whole-number sides; a Cantor-set boundary with a parity grading; a $\log$-to-$\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ depth sandwich; and, along its middle spine, the Pell numbers marching off toward the near-isosceles right triangles that Greek geometers would have recognised on sight. It is a $2500$-year-old object that we now understand well enough to say precisely which twenty-first-century machine it is *not*.

That is what a good negative theorem does. It does not close a door. It tells you exactly which key would have opened it.

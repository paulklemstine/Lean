# The Ramp That Never Reaches the Top

## How much private space does a "full stop" need?

Imagine you are teaching a machine to read. Not to understand — just to know where a sentence
stops. That sounds like the easiest job in the pipeline: everything else is grammar and meaning
and ambiguity, and this is only a period. And yet, in practice, "knowing where to stop" is one of
the most fragile things a learned sequence model does. Train the same architecture five times with
five different random initialisations and you can get five different answers: some copies stop
flawlessly, some trail off into gibberish, some stop *almost* always and then, on the one long
input you care about, don't.

Why should a period be hard?

The suspicion — old, folkloric, and until recently untested — is that the boundary symbol needs
*room*. In a learned model, every symbol is a vector, and all those vectors live in one shared
space. Content symbols crowd together because they mean similar things; the boundary symbol has no
meaning at all, only a job, and to do that job it must be distinguishable from everything else at
exactly the moment it matters. The natural way to make it distinguishable is to give it coordinates
that nothing else uses — its own **exclusive dimensions**.

So a very sharp question: *how many exclusive dimensions does a boundary token need?*

The folk answer is "one, obviously": if there is even a single coordinate that only the boundary
symbol ever activates, a linear readout can find it, and the problem is solved. Distinctness should
be enough. This article is about why that answer is wrong, and about the precise mathematical shape
of the correct one.

---

## The measurement

The experiment that provoked the mathematics is simple to describe. Fix a recurrent sequence model
completely — every architectural choice, every hyperparameter, the training data, the number of
steps — and let exactly one number vary: $E$, the number of coordinates the boundary token's
learned representation is allowed to occupy before the rest of the vector is padded with zeros.
Everything the boundary token has that content tokens don't is its *exclusive width*, and we write
$k$ for that number of exclusive dimensions.

Then train six fresh copies at each width, and ask, for each copy, a single yes/no question: does
this copy stop correctly, essentially always, on inputs longer than any it was trained on? Call a
copy that does **cured**.

The reliability curve came out like this:

| exclusive dimensions $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| fraction cured | $0.25$ | $0.33$ | $0.83$ | $1.00$ | $1.00$ |

Three features of that table deserve attention.

First, it is **monotone**. More exclusive room never hurts.

Second, **one exclusive dimension is not enough**. At $k=1$, two of six copies were perfect and
one scored $0.157$ — a hard failure. The same width, the same architecture, the same data;
different random seed, catastrophically different outcome. So the naive reading — "any exclusive
dimension suffices" — is refuted by measurement.

Third, and most interestingly, there is **no sharp threshold**. If exclusivity were a *capacity*
constraint, we would expect a cliff: below some critical width nothing works, at and above it
everything works. Instead we see a *ramp*: the gains arrive smoothly and with diminishing returns,
and the widths that report $100\%$ report it with six samples, not with a proof.

That last distinction — cliff versus ramp — is not a matter of taste. It is the difference between
a design rule that says "use at least $k^\*$ dimensions and you are safe" and one that says "each
extra dimension divides your failure rate by a constant, and you never reach zero". They recommend
different engineering. So it is worth knowing which one is true, and worth knowing it as a theorem
rather than as a fit to six points.

## A model you can prove things about

Here is the abstraction. Reliability failures of this kind are *alignment* failures: the boundary
token's private subspace lines up with some degenerate direction of the trained dynamics — a
direction along which the readout cannot tell the boundary apart from a continuation. Whatever the
mechanism, the geometry is the same: there is a distinguished proper subspace, and the run fails
when the token's whole exclusive subspace falls inside it.

Make that exact and finite. Let $V$ be a vector space of dimension $n$ over the finite field
$\mathbb{F}_p$ with $p$ elements. (Finiteness is what makes "probability" mean *counting*; the
prime $p$ will turn out to matter more than you'd expect.) The boundary token's exclusive subspace
is generated by $k$ vectors $v_1,\dots,v_k$ drawn independently and uniformly from $V$ — one draw
per exclusive dimension. The trained network's degenerate directions are modelled by a finite
family of **obstruction subspaces** $W_1,\dots,W_m \subsetneq V$, each a *proper* subspace.

> **The failure event.** The run fails at width $k$ when some obstruction swallows the entire
> exclusive subspace: there is an index $j$ with $v_i \in W_j$ for every $i = 1,\dots,k$.

Write $P_{\text{fail}}(k)$ for the probability of that event and $P_{\text{cure}}(k) = 1 -
P_{\text{fail}}(k)$ for the reliability. Everything below is a theorem about this curve.

---

## The four laws of the ramp

**1. It is monotone.** $P_{\text{cure}}(k)$ never decreases as $k$ grows.

The reason is a one-line bijective argument that I find genuinely charming. Take any failing tuple
of width $k+1$ and delete its last vector. What's left is a failing tuple of width $k$ — the same
obstruction still contains all the surviving vectors. So "delete the last coordinate" maps failures
to failures, and each width-$k$ failure can be the image of at most $|V|$ different width-$(k+1)$
failures, one for each possible deleted vector. Hence the number of failures at width $k+1$ is at
most $|V|$ times the number at width $k$; dividing by the $|V|$-times-larger sample space, the
failure *probability* cannot go up. Extra room never hurts. That is the first empirical claim,
proved.

**2. It never reaches the top.** For every finite width $k$, $P_{\text{fail}}(k) > 0$; equivalently
$P_{\text{cure}}(k) < 1$ always.

The witness is embarrassingly simple: the all-zero tuple. Every subspace contains the zero vector,
so the draw $v_1 = \dots = v_k = 0$ fails at every width, forever. There is always failing mass. As
a corollary, **there is no capacity cliff**: no width $k_0$ exists below which the curve is $0$ and
at or above which it is $1$, because it is never $1$. The observed $100\%$ at $k=4$ and $k=8$ is
therefore a finite-sample effect — the theory says it *must* be — and the honest prediction is that
enough seeds at $k=8$ would eventually turn up a failure.

**3. It climbs at exactly geometric rate.** For every $k$,
$$\frac{1}{p^{k}} \;\le\; 1 - P_{\text{cure}}(k) \;\le\; \frac{m}{p^{k}}.$$

The upper bound is a union bound. A proper subspace of $V$ has index at least $p$, so it contains at
most $|V|/p$ vectors; the chance that all $k$ independent draws land in one fixed obstruction is
therefore at most $p^{-k}$, and summing over the $m$ obstructions gives $m p^{-k}$. The lower bound
needs one of the obstructions to be a *hyperplane* — index exactly $p$ — and then the chance that
all $k$ draws land in that one hyperplane is exactly $p^{-k}$, which is already failure. Squeeze.

This is the shape of the ramp: the failure mass is pinned between two geometric decays with the
same ratio $1/p$, differing only by the constant factor $m$. It cannot be a cliff (that would need
super-geometric collapse), and it cannot be a plateau (that would need sub-geometric decay).

**4. In the cleanest case the curve is exact, and its benefit is sublinear.** With one hyperplane
obstruction,
$$P_{\text{cure}}(k) = 1 - p^{-k}$$
on the nose. Then $P_{\text{cure}}(0) = 0$ — with no exclusive room at all you never succeed — and
$$P_{\text{cure}}(1) = 1 - \frac{1}{p},$$
which is *not* $1$. **The first exclusive dimension is not sufficient.** It buys a fixed fraction
of the way, and no more. And the marginal value of the $(k+1)$-st dimension is exactly
$$P_{\text{cure}}(k+1) - P_{\text{cure}}(k) = \frac{p-1}{p^{k+1}},$$
which strictly decreases in $k$: diminishing returns, quantitatively. At $p = 2$ this curve reads
$$0,\; \tfrac12,\; \tfrac34,\; \tfrac{15}{16},\; \tfrac{255}{256}$$
at $k = 0,1,2,4,8$ — compare $0.25,\;0.33,\;0.83,\;1.00,\;1.00$. Same shape, same knee, same story;
the two measured $1.00$s are, per the model, really $0.94$ and $0.996$ observed six times each.

## Are the "exclusive" dimensions really exclusive?

Here is a subtlety the phrase "$k$ exclusive dimensions" hides. We drew $k$ vectors at random and
called their span a $k$-dimensional subspace. But random vectors can be linearly dependent, in
which case the token has fewer genuinely private directions than we paid for. Does the model quietly
overclaim?

It doesn't, and the correction is a classical and beautiful counting formula. The probability that
$k$ uniform draws from an $n$-dimensional space over $\mathbb{F}_p$ are linearly independent is
exactly
$$\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{p^{i}}{p^{n}}\right),$$
because the $i$-th draw must avoid the span of the previous $i$, a subspace of size $p^{i}$. This
is a $q$-Pochhammer product, the same object that governs counting flags and subspaces over finite
fields. Applying the elementary Weierstrass inequality $1 - \sum a_i \le \prod (1 - a_i)$ (valid for
$0 \le a_i \le 1$, proved by an easy induction) and summing the geometric series gives a clean
bound:
$$\mathbb{P}(\text{independent}) \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{p^{k}-1}{(p-1)\,p^{n}}.$$

For the regime of the experiment — $p = 2$, ambient width $n = 192$, $k \le 8$ — that error is about
$2^{-184}$. The exclusive dimensions are exclusive.

But now notice something the two bounds say together. The *reliability* error shrinks like
$p^{-k}$; the *genericity* error grows like $p^{k-n}$. Wanting more private directions makes success
likelier and makes the directions likelier to collide. Combining them, the probability that the
boundary token both occupies a genuinely $k$-dimensional subspace *and* escapes every obstruction is
at least
$$1 - \frac{m}{p^{k}} - \frac{p^{k}-1}{(p-1)p^{n}}.$$

Two error terms moving in opposite directions in $k$: this is a trade-off with an **interior
optimum**, near $k \approx n/2$, and it is the strongest structural reason the reliability curve
cannot be a cliff. A cliff is what you get when one force acts alone. Here two do.

## An unexpected guest: the Riemann zeta function

One last turn, and it is the one that made the whole thing feel less like engineering and more like
mathematics.

In the clean hyperplane model the failure probability at width $k$ is exactly $p^{-k}$. Ask a
natural question: what is the *total* failure mass of the ramp, summed over all widths? It is a
geometric series,
$$\sum_{k \ge 0} p^{-k} = \frac{p}{p-1} = \left(1 - \frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}.$$

Anyone who has met analytic number theory will recognise that number instantly. It is the Euler
factor of the Riemann zeta function at the prime $p$. Damp each width by an exponent $s$ — count a
width-$k$ failure with weight $p^{-ks}$ instead of $p^{-k}$ — and the total becomes
$(1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$ exactly. Now remember that our model had a free parameter we have been treating
as arbitrary: the characteristic $p$. Every prime gives a different ramp. Multiply the damped
failure masses of *all* of them together and Euler's product formula hands you
$$\prod_{p \text{ prime}} \sum_{k \ge 0} p^{-ks} \;=\; \zeta(s), \qquad \operatorname{Re} s > 1,$$
so that at $s = 2$ the product of the damped failure masses across all characteristics is
$\pi^2/6$.

I want to be careful about what this is and isn't. It is not a claim that the distribution of the
primes governs neural networks. It is an *identity*: the reliability ramp's failure spectrum has
exactly the shape of a local Euler factor, and the aggregate over characteristics is $\zeta$. But
it does carry a real moral. Euler factors exist because geometric series are infinite. A cliff —
failure vanishing exactly beyond some width — would make the failure mass a *finite* sum, and the
Euler factor would collapse. The zeta identity holds precisely because the ramp never terminates.
The absence of a threshold is what makes the arithmetic work.

---

## What to actually do

The theory converts into a design rule with two matching halves. To reach reliability $1 - \epsilon$
it *suffices* to take width
$$k \ge \log_p\!\left(\frac{m}{\epsilon}\right),$$
and it is *necessary* to take
$$k \ge \log_p\!\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right).$$
The two differ by an additive $\log_p m$, never by a multiplicative factor. So there *is* a
well-located knee — you can say where the curve turns — even though there is no sharp threshold. The
knee's *position* is pinned; its *sharpness* does not exist.

For the system that started this: reserve **at least four exclusive dimensions** for a boundary
token. Two is nearly robust; one is a coin flip dressed up as a design. And the deeper methodological
point, which generalises far beyond periods and neural networks: when a single run of an experiment
reports a "critical width", ask whether you are looking at a threshold or at one draw from a ramp.
Ramps masquerade as thresholds all the time — at any width where the failure probability is $0.2$,
five out of six samples look like a clean success. The way to tell them apart is not more careful
measurement at one width; it is measurement at several widths, plus a model that says what the
alternatives could look like.

That is what the mathematics above provides: a family of curves in which monotonicity, the
insufficiency of the first dimension, diminishing returns, geometric approach to certainty, and the
strict impossibility of ever attaining it are all consequences of a single elementary counting
argument — and, at the far end, a bridge to one of the oldest identities in number theory. The
reliability curve of a full stop turns out to be an Euler factor. I did not see that coming either.

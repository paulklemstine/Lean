# Counting the Uncountable: How Many *Shapes* Do Cubic Patterns Come In?

Imagine you are handed a machine that can stamp out patterns. Each pattern is built
from ten switches, and the rule is simple: you pick three distinct switches, and you may
either "wire them together" or not. A pattern is just a choice — for every possible trio
of switches — of whether that trio is wired or left alone.

How many patterns are there? There are $\binom{10}{3} = 120$ possible trios, and each
one is an independent yes/no decision. So the total number of patterns is

$$2^{120} = 1\,329\,227\,995\,784\,915\,872\,903\,807\,060\,280\,344\,576,$$

a number with thirty-seven digits. That is already more patterns than there are atoms in
a billion Earths. But here is the twist that turns a counting exercise into deep
mathematics: **most of these patterns are secretly the same.**

## When two patterns are "the same"

The ten switches don't have fixed identities. We are allowed to *relabel* and *mix*
them — not just by permuting them, but by forming any reversible linear combination over
the two-element number system (where $1 + 1 = 0$). This collection of allowed mixings is
a famous group called the **general linear group** $\mathrm{GL}(10,2)$: the group of all
invertible $10 \times 10$ matrices whose entries are $0$ or $1$, with arithmetic done
modulo $2$.

Two patterns are considered *equivalent* if one can be turned into the other by such a
mixing of the switches. Think of it like this: a chemical molecule looks different
depending on how you rotate it, but it is still the same molecule. In the same way, two
of our patterns may look different bit-for-bit, yet describe the same underlying
structure once we account for relabeling.

The natural question — the one that classification is really about — is:

> **How many genuinely different patterns are there, once equivalent ones are counted as one?**

In the language of group actions, we are asking for the number of **orbits** of
$\mathrm{GL}(10,2)$ acting on the space of these patterns. Each orbit is one "shape,"
one true equivalence class. And it has been proposed, based on painstaking
enumeration, that the number of *nonzero* shapes (excluding the trivial all-off pattern)
is exactly

$$3\,691\,560.$$

That is an astonishing collapse: from $2^{120}$ raw patterns down to fewer than four
million essential shapes. But how do we know a number like $3\,691\,560$ is even in the
right ballpark? Can we *prove* something rigorous about it, from first principles,
without carrying out the full enumeration?

The answer is yes — and the tool is one of the oldest and most reliable ideas in
mathematics: the pigeonhole principle.

## The pigeonhole principle, at cosmic scale

Suppose you have to distribute $2^{120}$ letters into mailboxes, where each mailbox is an
orbit. How big can a single mailbox be? An orbit is generated by applying every element
of the group to a single pattern, so an orbit can contain **at most as many patterns as
there are group elements.** The size of the group is known exactly. Using the classical
formula for the order of a general linear group over a finite field,

$$|\mathrm{GL}(10,2)| = \prod_{i=0}^{9}\bigl(2^{10} - 2^{i}\bigr)
= 366\,440\,137\,299\,948\,128\,422\,802\,227\,200,$$

a thirty-digit number. If every mailbox holds at most $|\mathrm{GL}(10,2)|$ letters, then
the number of mailboxes must be at least the number of letters divided by that maximum
capacity. This is the pigeonhole principle: you cannot fit more letters into fewer, fuller
boxes than the arithmetic allows.

There is one refinement. The all-off pattern — the "zero" — sits completely alone: every
mixing of the switches leaves it unchanged, so it forms an orbit of size one. If we set it
aside and count only the nonzero patterns, we get the sharper estimate

$$\text{number of nonzero shapes} \;\ge\;
\left\lceil \frac{2^{120} - 1}{|\mathrm{GL}(10,2)|} \right\rceil.$$

Carry out this single division of two enormous integers, round up, and something clean
emerges:

$$\text{number of nonzero shapes} \;\ge\; 3\,627\,409.$$

This is a **proven** floor. No matter how the enumeration turns out, there cannot be
fewer than $3\,627\,409$ essential shapes of cubic pattern on ten switches. It is not a
heuristic, not a simulation, not an approximation — it is a hard mathematical guarantee
extracted from two exact integers.

## Meeting the proposed answer

Now place the proven floor next to the proposed exact count:

$$3\,627\,409 \;\le\; 3\,691\,560.$$

The proposed value clears the floor — and it clears it by only

$$3\,691\,560 - 3\,627\,409 = 64\,151.$$

In relative terms, the proposed count sits a mere **$1.77\%$** above the rigorous lower
bound. Out of a search space of $2^{120}$, first-principles reasoning alone pins the true
answer down to within one part in fifty-seven. That is a remarkably tight cage for a
number that no formula is known to produce in closed form.

## What the gap of 64,151 is telling us

Why isn't the pigeonhole bound *exactly* right? Because not every mailbox is full. The
bound assumes each nonzero pattern lives in an orbit of the maximum possible size —
equivalently, that no nonzero pattern is left unchanged by any nontrivial mixing of the
switches. Patterns with that property are called **regular** (they have "trivial
stabilizer"), and their orbits are as large as the group.

But some patterns *are* symmetric: there exist nontrivial mixings that map them to
themselves. Such patterns sit in **shorter** orbits, so they need *more* mailboxes than
the pigeonhole count predicts. Every unit of shortfall from the maximum orbit size adds
one extra shape beyond the floor. The gap of $64\,151$ is precisely the accumulated
excess contributed by these symmetric, non-regular patterns.

In other words, the number $64\,151$ is not noise — it is a **measurement of hidden
symmetry.** It quantifies exactly how much the population of self-symmetric cubic patterns
inflates the shape count above what pure counting would give. The smallness of this gap
relative to the whole ($1.77\%$) tells us that, in ten variables, the overwhelming
majority of cubic patterns are rigid: they have no symmetry at all, and their orbits are
as large as they can possibly be.

## A window, and a moving target

What we have, then, is a rigorously proven **two-sided window**. On one side, the trivial
upper limit: there cannot be more nonzero shapes than there are nonzero patterns,
$2^{120} - 1$. On the other side, the pigeonhole floor $3\,627\,409$. The true answer —
and the proposed value $3\,691\,560$ — must live inside

$$\bigl[\,3\,627\,409,\; 2^{120}-1\,\bigr],$$

and in fact hugs the lower end of that window astonishingly closely.

This picture also hints at a beautiful large-scale story. As the number of switches $n$
grows, the group $\mathrm{GL}(n,2)$ swells far faster than the "defect" caused by
symmetric patterns. The prediction is that the pigeonhole floor and the true count
converge: in the limit, *almost every* cubic pattern becomes rigid, and the fraction of
symmetric ones vanishes. The tight $1.77\%$ gap at ten variables is the first clear
glimpse of this convergence — the moment where the window narrows enough to see the
pattern behind the pattern.

## Why this matters

Classifying algebraic forms up to linear change of variables is one of the oldest engines
of mathematics — it is how we understand conics and quadrics, how coding theorists sort
error-correcting codes, and how physicists organize states with the same symmetry type.
Cubic forms over the two-element field sit at a frontier where the objects are simple to
describe but ferociously numerous, and exact classification quickly outruns human
enumeration.

The lesson here is that even when an exact count is out of reach of any known formula, a
single, elementary principle — count the letters, bound the mailboxes, divide — can fence
the answer into a razor-thin interval. The pigeonhole principle, deployed against a
thirty-seven-digit search space and a thirty-digit group, delivers a guarantee that no
amount of raw computation can overturn: there are at least $3\,627\,409$ essential shapes
of cubic pattern on ten switches, and the proposed count of $3\,691\,560$ fits inside the
proven window with room to spare.

Sometimes the most powerful thing you can prove about an uncomputable number is exactly
where it has to live.

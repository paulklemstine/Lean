# When Equality Becomes a Shape: A Self-Contained Tour of Homotopy Type Theory

## The most innocent question in mathematics

Ask a child what it means for two things to be equal and you will get a confident
answer: they are the same. Ask a mathematician and, for two thousand years, you
would have gotten the same answer dressed in heavier clothing. Equality, we were
taught, is a *yes-or-no* affair. Either two objects are equal or they are not,
and once you know which, there is nothing left to say. The statement "2 + 2 = 4"
carries no extra baggage; it is simply true, full stop.

Homotopy Type Theory — HoTT for short — begins by quietly refusing this dogma.
Its founding intuition, due in its modern form to the late Fields medalist
Vladimir Voevodsky, is startling: **a proof that two things are equal is itself an
object worth studying, and there can be genuinely different ways for two things to
be the same.**

If that sounds like a contradiction, here is the picture that dissolves it.
Imagine two points on a sheet of paper. "Equal" is reinterpreted as "there is a
path connecting them." On a blank sheet, any two points are joined by essentially
one path, and HoTT recovers ordinary equality. But now punch a hole in the paper.
Suddenly a path that goes *left* of the hole and a path that goes *right* of the
hole cannot be slid into one another — they are different ways of connecting the
same two points. Equality has acquired structure. It has become geometry.

This article is about a compact, fully self-contained piece of mathematics that
builds this geometric notion of equality from scratch and proves four of its
deepest load-bearing theorems — with no hidden assumptions, no borrowed
machinery, and nothing swept under the rug. We will state every result plainly
and explain why each one matters.

## The trap that had to be avoided

There is a subtle obstacle to even *talking* about HoTT inside a modern proof
language. Most foundational systems come with a built-in notion of equality that
is, by design, *proof-irrelevant*: any two proofs that `a = b` are themselves
automatically considered equal. This principle is called Uniqueness of Identity
Proofs (UIP), or Axiom K. It is perfectly reasonable if you believe equality is a
yes-or-no affair — and it is fatal to HoTT, because it flattens every "path" down
to a single point. With UIP in force, the holed sheet of paper magically heals
itself; left and right become the same; geometry vanishes.

So the development described here makes a deliberate and crucial choice. Instead
of using the ambient equality, it introduces a *synthetic identity type*, written
`Path a b`, defined as the smallest structure generated by a single constructor:
for every point `a`, there is the trivial "stay put" path `refl a` from `a` to
itself. Crucially, `Path` is declared to live in the universe of *data*, not in
the universe of *propositions*. This one decision is the whole game. Because
`Path` is a genuine data type, the system does **not** hand us UIP for free. Every
law we want about paths must be earned by honest reasoning, and — most
importantly — the possibility of genuinely distinct paths is preserved.

The only tool we are allowed for reasoning about paths is *path induction*: to
prove something about an arbitrary path, it suffices to prove it for the trivial
path `refl`. This is the formal echo of the topological fact that any path can be
continuously retracted back to its starting point. From this single principle,
the entire edifice is built.

## Paths form a world that composes

The first thing one proves is that paths behave like the arrows of a richly
structured network — what mathematicians call a *groupoid*, and what HoTT reveals
to be an *∞-groupoid*. Every path `p : Path a b` can be reversed to a path
`p⁻¹ : Path b a` (symmetry). Two compatible paths `p : Path a b` and
`q : Path b c` can be concatenated into `p ⬝ q : Path a c` (transitivity). Doing
nothing — the path `refl` — is a unit for this composition. And these operations
obey the expected algebra: composition is associative, and a path followed by its
own reverse is "the same as" doing nothing.

But here is the subtlety that makes HoTT what it is: "the same as" in the previous
sentence is *itself a path* — a path between paths, a higher-dimensional cell. The
law `p ⬝ p⁻¹ = refl` is not a bald equation; it is a witnessed deformation, a
two-dimensional sheet showing how to shrink the round trip down to a standstill.
Each of these coherence laws — left and right units, associativity, inverse
laws — is proved by collapsing the arbitrary path to `refl` and observing that
everything snaps into place. This is the homotopical groupoid laid bare:

- **Reversal:** every `p : Path a b` yields `p⁻¹ : Path b a`.
- **Composition:** `p : Path a b` and `q : Path b c` yield `p ⬝ q : Path a c`.
- **Unit laws:** `refl ⬝ p = p` (which holds on the nose) and `p ⬝ refl = p`
  (witnessed by a higher path).
- **Inverse laws:** `p ⬝ p⁻¹` and `p⁻¹ ⬝ p` are both connected to `refl`.
- **Associativity:** `(p ⬝ q) ⬝ r` is connected to `p ⬝ (q ⬝ r)`.

Functions respect this structure too. Any function `f` sends a path `p : Path a b`
to a path `ap f p : Path (f a) (f b)` — "action on paths" — and this operation is
compatible with composition of functions and sends the identity function to
nothing at all. Type families can be *transported* along paths: if `P` is a
property varying over a space and you have a path from `a` to `b`, then any
instance of `P a` can be carried to an instance of `P b`. Equality, reborn as
geometry, lets you *move data around*.

## What does it really mean for two spaces to be "the same"?

Here the story sharpens. In ordinary mathematics, two structures are "the same" if
there is a function back and forth that undoes itself — an isomorphism, or in the
homotopical setting, an *equivalence*. But there turn out to be two competing
definitions of equivalence, and reconciling them is the first deep theorem.

The **naive** definition says: a map `f` is an equivalence if it has a
*quasi-inverse* — a map `g` going the other way, together with proofs that `g`
undoes `f` and `f` undoes `g`. This is the definition every undergraduate knows.
It has a hidden defect: a single map can have its quasi-inverse data filled in
many genuinely different ways, so "being an equivalence" carries unwanted extra
structure.

The **good** definition says: a map `f` is an equivalence if all of its *fibers*
are contractible. The fiber over a point `b` is the collection of all inputs that
`f` sends to `b`, bundled with the evidence of how. "Contractible" means this
collection has exactly one point, up to a path — it is a space with no holes and
no spare room, a single point in disguise. This definition has the elegant
property that being an equivalence is a mere *yes-or-no* fact again, with no extra
baggage.

The first headline theorem reconciles them:

> **Theorem (Coincidence of equivalences).** A map has a quasi-inverse if and only
> if all of its fibers are contractible.

In words: the lazy, familiar notion and the clean, structural notion pick out
*exactly the same maps*. The forward direction is delicate — given an arbitrary
quasi-inverse, you cannot directly conclude the fibers are contractible, because
the quasi-inverse data may be incoherent. The fix, a classical maneuver called
*adjointification*, surgically adjusts one of the two homotopies so that they
satisfy a triangle compatibility law, after which contractibility falls out. This
required building a small calculus of paths-between-paths — naturality squares,
cancellation laws, associativity at the second level — purely to push the
coherence through.

## The theorem that manufactures geometry

The second headline result is the engine room of the whole subject. It is called
the **Fundamental Theorem of Identity Types**, and it answers the question: how do
you ever get your hands on what the paths in a space actually *look like*?

Suppose you have a guess. You think the paths out of a fixed point `a` in some
space are described by a family `C` — for each point `x`, a description `C x` of
"the ways `a` equals `x`." You package your guess as a fibrewise map
`f : for every x, Path a x → C x`. The Fundamental Theorem says:

> **Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Identity Types).** The family of maps
> `f x : Path a x → C x` is a fibrewise equivalence **if and only if** the total
> space `Σ x, C x` is contractible.

The "if and only if" — the full biconditional — is what makes this a *tool*
rather than a description. The backward direction is the magic: if you can exhibit
*a single contractibility witness* for the total space of your guess, the theorem
*manufactures* the equivalence `Path a x ≃ C x` for you, for free. You do not have
to build the equivalence by hand; you only have to design a good family `C` and
prove one contractibility fact.

This is the formal heart of the **encode–decode method**, the standard technique
for computing identity types of concrete spaces. Want to know what equality looks
like in the two-element type, or in the natural numbers, or in a coproduct? Cook
up the right family `C`, check the total space is contractible, and the theorem
hands you the answer. The creative work collapses to a single well-chosen guess.

## Univalence and the right to reason by equivalence

The third result reaches for the philosophical summit of HoTT: the **univalence
axiom**, Voevodsky's revolutionary principle that *equivalent types are equal*. If
two structures behave identically — if there is an equivalence between them — then
univalence declares them genuinely the same object, and any true statement about
one is automatically a true statement about the other. It is the rigorous
crystallization of the everyday mathematical habit of "working up to isomorphism,"
finally promoted from a convention to a law.

The development here takes univalence as a hypothesis and extracts its most useful
consequence, **equivalence induction**:

> **Theorem (Equivalence induction).** To prove that a property `P` holds of
> *every* equivalence out of a fixed type `A`, it suffices to prove that `P` holds
> of the identity equivalence on `A`.

This is a license of extraordinary power. Any time you want to establish something
about all equivalences, you may pretend, without loss of generality, that the
equivalence is the trivial "do nothing" one. The hard general case dissolves into
the easy base case. This is the precise tool that powers the *Structure Identity
Principle* — the statement that isomorphic algebraic structures (groups, monoids,
rings) are equal and therefore share all properties — and it is the same tool that
lets one derive, remarkably, that *function extensionality itself is a consequence
of univalence* rather than an independent assumption.

## Building a new shape by hand: propositional truncation

The fourth result demonstrates that this framework is not merely descriptive but
*constructive*: it can build genuinely new spaces with prescribed properties.
These are called **higher inductive types**, and the simplest interesting one is
*propositional truncation*.

The idea answers a real need. Sometimes you have an object of type `A` — say, a
specific solution to an equation — but you only want to remember the *bare fact
that a solution exists*, deliberately forgetting which one. Propositional
truncation, written `‖A‖`, is the space `A` with all its internal structure
crushed flat: it remembers whether `A` was inhabited and nothing else. Any two of
its points are connected by a path, so it carries at most one bit of information.

Here it is realized concretely as a quotient — `A` with *every* pair of points
forcibly identified — and equipped with its defining **recursion principle**:

> **Theorem (Truncation recursion).** To define a map out of `‖A‖` into a type `P`
> in which all points are already connected (a "proposition"), it suffices to give
> a map out of `A` into `P`. Moreover, this extension is **unique**.

The uniqueness is the part that certifies `‖A‖` is the *correct* truncation and
not some accidental quotient: it pins down `‖A‖` by a universal property. This is a
genuine higher inductive type, built and verified from the ground up.

## Why a self-contained foundation matters

Each of these four theorems — the coincidence of equivalences, the Fundamental
Theorem of Identity Types, equivalence induction, and propositional truncation —
is a pillar of homotopy type theory. What is unusual here is that they are
assembled into a single, airtight, *library-free* fragment. Nothing is imported;
every groupoid law, every coherence cell, every contractibility argument is built
from the lone primitive of path induction. The result is a miniature foundation
you can hold in your hand and inspect in its entirety.

There is a recurring melody in the proofs, a method the authors call
*contractibility juggling*: nearly every difficult theorem is reduced to carrying
the property "this space is a single point in disguise" along an equivalence or a
retraction, seeded by one workhorse fact — that the space of points equal to a
fixed point is itself contractible. Geometry, it turns out, is mostly the art of
recognizing when something complicated is secretly a point.

## The bigger picture

Why should anyone outside the foundations of mathematics care that equality can be
a shape? Because the idea reorganizes how we think about *sameness* everywhere it
appears. In software, two data structures may be "the same" in many inequivalent
ways, and tracking those ways correctly is the difference between a correct program
and a subtle bug. In physics, the symmetries that relate two descriptions of a
system are exactly "paths between points" in a space of theories. In pure
mathematics, the dream of doing everything "up to isomorphism" without ever lying
becomes, under univalence, a theorem rather than a hope.

Homotopy Type Theory took the most innocent question in mathematics — what does it
mean for two things to be equal? — and answered it not with a definition but with a
geometry. The compact development surveyed here shows that this geometry can be
erected on a single primitive, that its deepest theorems can be proved with
nothing borrowed, and that the whole structure stands on its own. Equality, it
turns out, was never a yes-or-no question. It was a space all along, and we are
only now learning to walk around in it.

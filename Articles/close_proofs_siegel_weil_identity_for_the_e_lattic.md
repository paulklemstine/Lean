# Counting in Eight Dimensions: The Hidden Order of the $E_8$ Lattice

## A perfect packing, and a perfect count

Imagine trying to stack cannonballs — but in eight dimensions. In our ordinary
three-dimensional world, the densest way to pack spheres is the arrangement a
greengrocer uses for oranges. Push up to eight dimensions and something almost
miraculous happens: there is a single, breathtakingly symmetric arrangement of
points, called the **$E_8$ lattice**, that packs spheres more tightly than
anything else. It is so rigid and so beautiful that it shows up all over
mathematics and physics — in the classification of Lie groups, in string theory,
and (as the mathematician Maryna Viazovska proved in 2016) as the provably
optimal sphere packing in dimension eight.

This article is about a different kind of perfection hidden inside $E_8$: not how
tightly it packs, but how you *count* its points. It turns out that the number of
lattice points at each distance from the origin is governed by an exquisitely
simple formula — one that connects the geometry of eight-dimensional space to the
humble arithmetic of divisors, and even to a secret test for prime numbers.

## What is a lattice, and what are we counting?

A **lattice** is a perfectly regular, infinite grid of points. The simplest
example is the set of all points with whole-number coordinates. The $E_8$ lattice
lives in eight-dimensional space and is much richer: it is *even*, meaning the
squared length of every vector is an even integer, and *unimodular*, meaning it is
as dense as it can possibly be while staying "self-dual" — a kind of perfect
balance.

For each positive integer $n$, we ask a simple question:

> How many vectors in the $E_8$ lattice have squared length exactly $2n$?

Call this count $r_{E_8}(n)$. The vectors of squared length $2$ (that is, $n=1$)
are the famous **240 roots** of $E_8$ — the eight-dimensional analog of the
directions to the vertices of a beautiful symmetric figure. So $r_{E_8}(1) = 240$.
For $n = 2$ there are $2160$ vectors, for $n = 3$ there are $6720$, and so on.
Where do these numbers come from?

## The sum of cubes of divisors

Here is the surprise. Define, for a positive integer $n$, the arithmetic quantity

$$\sigma_3(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} d^3,$$

the sum of the cubes of all the divisors of $n$. For example,
$\sigma_3(6) = 1^3 + 2^3 + 3^3 + 6^3 = 1 + 8 + 27 + 216 = 252$. This function knows
nothing about eight-dimensional geometry — it is pure number theory, a question
about how integers factor.

And yet, the central theorem says:

> **The $E_8$ counting formula.** For every positive integer $n$,
> $$r_{E_8}(n) = 240\,\sigma_3(n).$$

Let us test it. For $n=1$, the only divisor of $1$ is $1$, so $\sigma_3(1) = 1$
and $240 \cdot 1 = 240$ — exactly the number of roots. For $n=2$, the divisors are
$1$ and $2$, giving $\sigma_3(2) = 1 + 8 = 9$, and $240 \cdot 9 = 2160$. For
$n=3$, $\sigma_3(3) = 1 + 27 = 28$ and $240 \cdot 28 = 6720$. Every value matches.
A purely geometric count in eight dimensions is *identical* to a purely
arithmetic sum over divisors.

This is one instance of a deep principle called the **Siegel–Weil identity**,
which says that averaging over all lattices of a given type reproduces a special,
highly symmetric function. In dimension eight the "average" collapses to a single
lattice — $E_8$ is the *only* even unimodular lattice of its kind — so the average
becomes an exact equality. The special function on the other side is the
**weight-4 Eisenstein series** $E_4$, one of the most fundamental objects in the
theory of modular forms. In short: *the theta series of $E_8$ is $E_4$.* The
numbers $240\,\sigma_3(n)$ are literally its Fourier coefficients.

## The arithmetic has a life of its own

Once you know the counts are governed by $\sigma_3$, you can mine that function
for structure — and it is astonishingly rich.

**A geometric formula on prime powers.** When $n$ is a power of a prime, say
$n = p^a$, the divisors are just $1, p, p^2, \ldots, p^a$, so their cubes form a
geometric progression:

$$\sigma_3(p^a) = 1 + p^3 + p^6 + \cdots + p^{3a} = \frac{p^{3(a+1)} - 1}{p^3 - 1}.$$

**A three-term recurrence.** The counts are not independent from one prime power
to the next. They satisfy a **Hecke recurrence**: for any prime $p$ and any
$r \ge 0$,

$$\sigma_3(p^{r+2}) + p^3\,\sigma_3(p^r) = \sigma_3(p)\,\sigma_3(p^{r+1}).$$

This is the signature of an "eigenform" — it says the whole tower of counts is
generated by simple, self-reproducing arithmetic. Crucially, this recurrence
*requires* $p$ to be prime: try it with the composite number $6$ and it breaks
($\sigma_3(36) + 6^3\,\sigma_3(1) = 55477$, but $\sigma_3(6)^2 = 63504$). The
prime numbers are baked into the very rhythm of the lattice.

**Multiplicativity.** If $m$ and $n$ share no common factor, then
$\sigma_3(mn) = \sigma_3(m)\,\sigma_3(n)$. This lets you compute $\sigma_3$ of any
number from its prime-power pieces.

**A master convolution law.** All of the above are special cases of one grand
identity that fuses two counts into one:

$$\sigma_3(m)\,\sigma_3(n) = \sum_{d \mid \gcd(m,n)} d^3\,\sigma_3\!\left(\frac{mn}{d^2}\right).$$

When $m$ and $n$ are coprime the sum has a single term and we recover
multiplicativity; when $m = n$ it becomes a striking "sum-of-squares" relation,
$\sigma_3(n)^2 = \sum_{d \mid n} d^3\,\sigma_3(n^2/d^2)$.

## A secret hidden inside: how to spot a prime

Now for a genuinely delightful consequence. We just saw that $\sigma_3(n)$ counts
the cubes of divisors. Every number $n \ge 2$ has at least the two divisors $1$
and $n$, contributing $1 + n^3$ to the sum. So we always have the lower bound

$$\sigma_3(n) \ge n^3 + 1.$$

When is this bound *exactly* achieved? Only when $n$ has no other divisors — that
is, only when $n$ is prime! This gives a clean, exact primality test dressed up in
the language of the $E_8$ lattice:

> **Primality from lattice counts.** For $n \ge 2$,
> $$\sigma_3(n) = n^3 + 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n \text{ is prime}.$$

For instance $\sigma_3(17) = 1 + 17^3 = 4914$ (so $17$ is prime), while
$\sigma_3(18) = 6813 \ne 18^3 + 1$ (so $18$ is composite). The Fourier
coefficients of an eight-dimensional geometric object secretly encode which
numbers are prime.

**A hidden congruence.** There is one more piece of quiet order. Comparing the
sum of cubes of divisors, $\sigma_3(n)$, with the ordinary sum of divisors,
$\sigma_1(n) = \sum_{d\mid n} d$, one finds they are always congruent modulo $6$:

$$\sigma_3(n) \equiv \sigma_1(n) \pmod 6.$$

The reason is beautifully elementary: for any integer $d$, the difference
$d^3 - d = (d-1)\,d\,(d+1)$ is a product of three consecutive integers, hence
divisible by both $2$ and $3$, so $d^3 \equiv d \pmod 6$. Summing over the
divisors of $n$ gives the congruence. A fact about three consecutive numbers
propagates all the way up to a relation between two different divisor systems.

## Contrarian warnings: where intuition fails

Part of understanding a formula is knowing exactly how far it can be pushed. Two
tempting generalizations turn out to be *false*, and it is instructive to see why.

First, one might guess that the raw counts $r_{E_8}(n) = 240\,\sigma_3(n)$ are
multiplicative — that $r_{E_8}(mn) = r_{E_8}(m)\,r_{E_8}(n)$ for coprime $m, n$.
They are not. At $m=2, n=3$ we have $r_{E_8}(6) = 60480$ but
$r_{E_8}(2)\,r_{E_8}(3) = 14515200$. The culprit is the normalizing factor $240$:
it is $\sigma_3$ that is multiplicative, not $240\,\sigma_3$. Multiplying two
counts double-counts the constant. The correct statement keeps the factor $240$ in
exactly one place.

Second, as noted above, the Hecke recurrence genuinely needs a prime base; it
collapses the moment you feed it a composite. These "negative" results are not
failures — they sharpen the theorem by drawing its precise boundary.

## The next frontier

The story does not end here. There is a beautiful conjectured identity, verified
numerically, that would elevate the single-lattice statement to a genuine product
law. It reads

$$\sigma_7(n) = \sigma_3(n) + 120 \sum_{m=1}^{n-1} \sigma_3(m)\,\sigma_3(n-m),$$

relating the weight-8 divisor sum $\sigma_7$ to a convolution of two copies of
$\sigma_3$. In the language of the underlying symmetry, this expresses the fact
that "$E_4$ squared equals $E_8$" — the statement that the space of the relevant
symmetric functions of weight eight is exactly one-dimensional, so any two such
functions with the same leading terms must coincide. Establishing it rigorously,
either through the theory of modular forms or through an elementary but intricate
manipulation of divisor sums, is the natural next milestone. And beyond it lies
the full geometric dream: to prove directly that *every* even unimodular lattice
in dimension eight — not just $E_8$ — has exactly $240\,\sigma_3(n)$ vectors at
each length, the genuine Siegel–Weil statement in its most tangible form.

## Why it matters

What makes this circle of ideas so compelling is the bridge it builds. On one
side sits geometry: a rigid crystal of points in eight dimensions, the tightest
sphere packing there is. On the other sits arithmetic: divisors, primes,
congruences, the raw material of number theory. The $E_8$ counting formula welds
them together so completely that a question about *distances* becomes a question
about *factorizations*, and vice versa. You can read primality off a geometric
count; you can read a geometric count off a sum of cubes. That two such distant
worlds share a single heartbeat — the function $\sigma_3$ — is the kind of unity
that makes mathematics feel less invented than discovered.

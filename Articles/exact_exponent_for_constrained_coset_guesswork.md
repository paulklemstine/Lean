# The Price of a Secret: How Coding Reshapes the Guessing Game

Imagine a game with the simplest possible rules. Someone picks a secret string
of bits — a password, a cryptographic key, a garbled message — and your only
move is to guess. You call out one candidate at a time, in whatever order you
like, and after each guess you are told a single word: *wrong* or *right*. There
is no partial credit, no "you're getting warmer." How long until you win?

This is the game of **guesswork**, and it is one of the cleanest models we have
for the effort of a brute-force attacker. It captures the essence of password
cracking, of side-channel attacks that measure how long a system takes to reject
a bad key, and of any situation in which a secret can only be tested by trying it.
The surprising thing is that a game this simple hides a rich and beautiful
mathematics — and that this mathematics tells us, exactly, what a good code buys
you when you hide a secret inside it.

## The order of your guesses is everything

Suppose the secret is drawn at random, and you know the odds. If some strings are
more likely than others, you should obviously guess them first. Guess the most
probable candidate, then the next most probable, and so on down the list. Under
this optimal ordering, the number of guesses you need to find a particular
string $x$ is exactly its **rank**: how many strings are at least as likely as it.
Call this number $G(x)$.

We would love to know the *typical* value of $G$, but averages here are treacherous.
A rare-but-catastrophic secret — one that sits near the bottom of your guessing
list — can dominate the accounting. To take this seriously, we track not just the
average number of guesses but the growth of its **moments**: the expected value of
$G(X)^\rho$ for a tuning parameter $\rho > 0$. Small $\rho$ describes a patient
attacker who cares about typical effort; large $\rho$ describes a cautious analyst
who worries about the worst realistic case. The single parameter $\rho$ sweeps
across the whole spectrum of attacker temperaments.

For a secret that is $n$ bits long, generated by flipping a biased coin — heads
with probability $p$, independently for each bit — the moment $E[G(X^n)^\rho]$ grows
exponentially in $n$. The quantity that matters is the **exponent**, the
per-bit growth rate
$$
E(\rho, p) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\,\log_2 E\big[G(X^n)^\rho\big].
$$
A landmark result of information theory, due to Arıkan and sharpened by Arıkan and
Merhav, computes this exponent exactly. It is not the ordinary Shannon entropy that
governs data compression; it is a subtler cousin.

## Enter the Rényi entropy

The right yardstick for guessing is the **Rényi entropy**. Where Shannon entropy
measures the average surprise of a source, the Rényi entropy of order $\alpha$,
$$
H_\alpha(p) \;=\; \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\,\log_2\!\big(p^{\alpha} + (1-p)^{\alpha}\big),
$$
is a whole family of entropies, one for each $\alpha$, that weigh the tail of the
distribution differently. As $\alpha \to 1$ it smoothly becomes the familiar
Shannon entropy $H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)$; the excluded value
$\alpha = 1$ is a *removable* singularity, a hole that the surrounding values fill
in perfectly.

The Arıkan–Merhav theorem says the guessing exponent is precisely
$$
E(\rho, p) \;=\; \rho\, H_{\frac{1}{1+\rho}}(p).
$$
The attacker's temperament $\rho$ does two things at once: it multiplies the whole
expression, and it *selects which Rényi entropy* to use, namely the one of order
$\alpha = 1/(1+\rho)$. A patient attacker ($\rho \to 0$) sees the Shannon entropy; an
increasingly cautious one ($\rho \to \infty$) slides the order down toward $\alpha = 0$,
where the entropy saturates at its maximum. This is a genuinely different exponent
from the one that governs compression, and it is the correct measure of how hard a
random secret is to guess.

## Now hide the secret inside a code

Here is where the story turns. In real systems, secrets are rarely raw coin flips.
They live inside the structure of an **error-correcting code**. A binary linear code
of rate $R$ takes $Rn$ information bits and pads them out to $n$ transmitted bits,
adding redundancy that lets a receiver detect and repair errors. The set of valid
codewords is a linear subspace of the space of all $2^n$ bit strings, and this
subspace carves the whole space into equal-sized parallel slices called **cosets**.

When a codeword is sent across a noisy channel and arrives corrupted, the pattern of
errors is pinned down — up to the code's ambiguity — by a short fingerprint called
the *syndrome*. The syndrome tells the attacker exactly one thing: **which coset** the
true noise pattern lives in. So the guessing game changes. The attacker no longer
searches the entire space of $2^n$ possible noise vectors; they search inside a single
coset, which contains only a $2^{-(1-R)n}$ fraction of them.

The question is irresistible: how much easier does this make the game? If the
attacker's search space shrinks by a factor of $2^{-(1-R)n}$, does the guessing effort
shrink by the same factor? Or does the structure of the code, the correlations it
introduces, distort the answer in some complicated way?

## The exact answer: a clean subtraction

The central result of this work is that the answer is as clean as one could hope.
The constrained coset guessing exponent is exactly the unconstrained one, shifted
down by a single, simple term:
$$
E_{\mathrm{coset}}(\rho, R, p) \;=\; E(\rho, p) \;-\; \rho\,(1-R)
\;=\; \rho\, H_{\frac{1}{1+\rho}}(p) \;-\; \rho\,(1-R).
$$

Read that formula slowly, because its structure is the whole point. The exponent
splits into two independent pieces that never interfere:

- an **information term** $\rho\, H_{1/(1+\rho)}(p)$, which depends only on the noise —
  how random the secret is; and
- a **coding term** $\rho\,(1-R)$, which depends only on the code — how much
  redundancy it spends.

The redundancy $1 - R$ is precisely the fraction of bits the code devotes to
protection rather than payload. The result says: **every bit of redundancy the code
spends translates, exactly and linearly, into a reduction of the guessing exponent** —
and it does so *regardless of the noise distribution* $p$. The two worlds — the
difficulty of the source and the cost of the code — decouple completely. This
additive separation is a structural fingerprint of linearity itself.

Crucially, this is an **equality, not a bound**. A naive argument — the coset is a
subset of the whole space, so guessing inside it can only be easier — delivers merely
an inequality, $E_{\mathrm{coset}} \le E$. Getting the *exact* shift requires taking the
coset's density seriously: a coset is not just *some* subset, it is a subset of a very
specific size, $2^{-(1-R)n}$ of the whole, and raising the compressed rank to the
$\rho$-th power multiplies the moment by exactly $2^{-\rho(1-R)n}$. That deterministic
multiplicative factor is what turns the vague inequality into a sharp identity.

## Why the shift is exact, and why it is universal

Underneath the headline formula lies a small, sturdy engine. Suppose any positive
sequence $f(n)$ grows with some exponential rate $E$, meaning
$\frac{1}{n}\log_2 f(n) \to E$. Multiply it, term by term, by the deterministic factor
$2^{-sn}$. Then the product's growth rate is *exactly* $E - s$:
$$
\frac{1}{n}\log_2\!\big(2^{-sn} f(n)\big) \;=\; -s + \frac{1}{n}\log_2 f(n) \;\longrightarrow\; -s + E.
$$
The identity in the middle is not an approximation valid only in the limit — it holds
for every $n \ge 1$, because the logarithm turns the multiplication into an addition
and the deterministic factor contributes exactly $-s$ per bit. A deterministic
multiplier adds nothing to the fluctuations of the sequence; it only slides the
exponent.

This is why the coset shift is both exact and universal. The parameter $p$ enters the
story *only* through the unconstrained exponent $E$; the compression factor $s = \rho(1-R)$
knows nothing about the noise. So the shift $-\rho(1-R)$ is uniform in $p$: whether the
channel is nearly silent or maximally noisy, spending redundancy $1-R$ costs the
attacker exactly the same reduction in the exponent. And because the engine only asks
for "a positive sequence with a growth rate," it applies far beyond binary codes:
swap $2^{-(1-R)n}$ for $q^{-(1-R)n}$ and the same argument governs $q$-ary alphabets.

## The corners of the map

A good formula should behave sensibly at its edges, and this one does.

**Full rate, $R = 1$.** A rate-1 code has no redundancy; it has a single coset, which is
the entire space. The constraint is vacuous, and indeed the coding term $\rho(1-R)$
vanishes: $E_{\mathrm{coset}}(\rho, 1, p) = E(\rho, p)$. Guessing inside "the one coset" is just
guessing, unconstrained.

**The nonnegativity guard.** For any valid rate $R \le 1$ and any $\rho \ge 0$, the shift
$\rho(1-R)$ is nonnegative. The constraint can only ever make guessing *easier*, never
harder — exactly as intuition demands. The code never accidentally helps the secret
hide.

**Maximal noise, $p = 1/2$.** When the coin is fair, every noise vector is equally likely
and the source is as hard to guess as possible. Here the Rényi entropy saturates:
$H_\alpha(1/2) = 1$ for *every* order $\alpha \ne 1$. The closed form then collapses to a
strikingly simple value,
$$
E_{\mathrm{coset}}\big(\rho, R, \tfrac12\big) \;=\; \rho\,R.
$$
All trace of the entropy is gone; the exponent is just the code rate, scaled by the
attacker's temperament $\rho$. This is the purest expression of the whole phenomenon:
strip away the source's structure by making it maximally random, and what remains is
pure coding.

**Symmetry.** The Rényi entropy does not care whether we call the biased outcome "heads"
or "tails": $H_\alpha(p) = H_\alpha(1-p)$. The difficulty of guessing depends on how far the
coin is from fair, not on which way it leans.

## A phase boundary you can compute

Because the exponent is *linear* in the rate $R$, it crosses zero at a single, exactly
computable rate:
$$
R^\*(\rho, p) \;=\; 1 - H_{\frac{1}{1+\rho}}(p).
$$
Above this rate the exponent is positive and guessing is exponentially hard; below it
the exponent turns negative and the moment decays — guessing becomes sub-exponentially
easy. This is not an estimate or a heuristic threshold. It is a sharp phase boundary,
handed to us in closed form, separating the "secure" and "insecure" regimes of the
code as a function of the noise and the attacker's temperament. For the fair-coin
source it sits at $R^\* = 0$: any positive rate is already hard.

## Why it matters

Guesswork is not an abstract curiosity. It is the mathematics of brute force. When a
system leaks *which coset* a secret lives in — through a syndrome, a checksum, an error
report, or a timing side channel — this result tells you, to the exponent, how much
that leak helps an attacker. And the answer is refreshingly actionable: the damage is
exactly $\rho(1-R)$ per bit, it depends only on the code's redundancy, and it is
completely independent of the noise. A designer choosing between codes can read off the
security cost of redundancy directly, without ever needing to know the channel.

The deeper lesson is one of *decoupling*. Two forces shape how hard a hidden secret is
to guess: the intrinsic randomness of the secret and the structure of the code that
wraps it. One might expect these to tangle together in some intractable way. Instead,
they add. The source contributes its Rényi entropy; the code subtracts its redundancy;
and the exponent is simply their difference. In a subject where clean formulas are rare
and hard-won, that additive separation — exact, universal, and provable — is a small
piece of order carved out of a genuinely noisy world.

# The Number That Knows Its Own Symmetry

## How $1.7296$ bits reveal the shape of a hidden group

### A question a prime cannot dodge

Pick a prime number — say $11$. Divide it by $56$ and keep the remainder: $11$.
Now ask a strange-sounding question: *how many times must you multiply $11$ by
itself before the result is $\pm 1$ modulo $56$?*

$$11^2 = 121 = 2\cdot 56 + 9 \equiv 9, \qquad 11^3 \equiv 99 \equiv 43 \equiv -13,
\qquad 11^6 \equiv 43^2 \equiv 1 .$$

Six steps. We say $11$ has **type $6$**. Try $13$: $13^2 = 169 = 3\cdot 56 + 1
\equiv 1$, so $13$ has type $2$. Try $29$: $29 \equiv -27$, and $29^2 = 841 =
15\cdot 56 + 1 \equiv 1$, type $2$ again. Try $55 \equiv -1$: type $1$.

This little computation is not a curiosity. The type of a prime is a piece of
deep arithmetic wearing a very cheap disguise. It records exactly how that prime
*factors* inside a particular number system built out of roots of unity — and,
through that, it records the order of a symmetry attached to the prime. The
purpose of this article is to tell the story of one specific number system,
where the game of types turns out to encode, in a single real number, the
complete shape of a symmetry group that has no right to be so easily read off.

That number is $1.7296$ bits.

### Fields built from roots of unity, and their real shadows

Fix an integer $f$ — call it the **conductor** — and let
$\zeta_f = e^{2\pi i / f}$, the basic $f$-th root of unity. Adjoining $\zeta_f$
to the rational numbers gives the **cyclotomic field** $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$: all
numbers you can build from rationals and $\zeta_f$ by addition, subtraction,
multiplication and division. It is a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$ of dimension
$\varphi(f)$, Euler's totient — the count of integers between $1$ and $f$ that
share no factor with $f$.

For $f = 56 = 8\cdot 7$ we get $\varphi(56) = 24$: the field
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})$ is $24$-dimensional over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Cyclotomic fields carry symmetry. For each residue $a$ coprime to $56$ there is a
symmetry $\sigma_a$ of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})$ sending $\zeta_{56} \mapsto
\zeta_{56}^a$, and these are *all* the symmetries. So the symmetry group — the
Galois group — is exactly the group $(\mathbb{Z}/56)^\times$ of reduced residues
under multiplication, a group of order $24$.

Among these symmetries one is special: $\sigma_{-1}$, which sends $\zeta$ to
$\zeta^{-1} = \bar\zeta$. It is complex conjugation, and it has order $2$. The
numbers it leaves alone form a smaller field, the **real cyclotomic field**

$$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})^{+} = \mathbb{Q}\!\left(\zeta_{56} + \zeta_{56}^{-1}\right)
= \mathbb{Q}\!\left(2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{56}\right),$$

which sits inside the real numbers. Because we quotiented by a symmetry of order
$2$, its dimension is halved:

> **Theorem (Degree twelve).** $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})^{+}$ has dimension exactly
> $12$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and its symmetry group is
> $G^{+} = (\mathbb{Z}/56)^\times / \{\pm 1\}$, of order $12$.

### The first group with a choice

Here is where conductor $56$ earns its place. Up to this point in the story —
and there is a long story, a ladder of fields of degree $2, 3, 4, \dots$ — every
symmetry group encountered was **cyclic**: generated by a single element, like
the hours on a clock. Cyclic groups of a given order are unique, so knowing the
order tells you the group, and there is nothing to detect.

At order $12$ that stops being true. There are exactly two abelian groups of
order $12$:

- the cyclic group $C_{12}$ (one generator, one orbit of length $12$), and
- the product $C_6 \times C_2$ (two independent generators, of orders $6$ and $2$).

They have the same size. They are genuinely different: $C_{12}$ contains an
element of order $12$; in $C_6\times C_2$ every element $g$ satisfies $6g = 0$,
so no element has order more than $6$. Order $12$ is the first composite order
where the ladder faces a *choice* — and conductor $56$ is where it takes the
non-cyclic branch.

Why is $G^{+}$ non-cyclic here? Because $56 = 8 \cdot 7$ splits the unit group as

$$(\mathbb{Z}/56)^\times \;\cong\; (\mathbb{Z}/8)^\times \times (\mathbb{Z}/7)^\times
\;\cong\; (C_2\times C_2)\times C_6 .$$

Concretely, every reduced residue mod $56$ can be written in exactly one way as

$$a \;=\; 3^{\,i}\cdot 13^{\,j}\cdot (-1)^{k}, \qquad
0\le i<6,\; 0\le j<2,\; 0\le k<2 .$$

Here $3$ has order $6$ ($3^6 = 729 = 13\cdot 56 + 1$), $13$ has order $2$, and
$-1$ has order $2$; the three of them are independent, and $6\cdot 2\cdot 2 = 24$
accounts for every unit. Killing the $\pm 1$ factor — which is precisely what
passing to the real subfield does — removes the last coordinate and leaves

$$G^{+} \;\cong\; C_6\times C_2 .$$

Its exponent is $6$, strictly less than its order $12$, so no single element
generates it:

> **Theorem (Non-cyclicity).** The symmetry group of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})^{+}$
> is an abelian group of order $12$ that is not cyclic; it is isomorphic to
> $C_6\times C_2$.

This is the first rung of the ladder where that happens.

### Types, Frobenius, and how primes break apart

Now return to the game we started with. For a prime $p$ other than the two
ramified primes $2$ and $7$, define its **type**

$$T(p) \;=\; \min\{\,k\ge 1 \;:\; p^k \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{56}\,\}.$$

Two facts turn this into geometry.

First, $T(p)$ depends only on $p \bmod 56$. It is a function of the residue, not
of the prime — remainders are all the information it consumes.

Second — and this is the arithmetic content — $T(p)$ is the order of the
**Frobenius symmetry** of $p$ inside $G^{+}$. Under the identification
$G^{+}\cong C_6\times C_2$ described above, the residue $3^i 13^j$ corresponds to
the pair $(i,j)$, and one checks case by case that

$$T\!\left(3^i 13^j\right) \;=\; \text{order of } (i,j) \text{ in } C_6\times C_2 .$$

The order of the Frobenius symmetry controls how the prime $p$ decomposes in
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})^{+}$: the prime splits into $g$ distinct prime factors,
each of "degree" $f = T(p)$, and

$$f\cdot g \;=\; 12 .$$

There is a clean picture behind this. Frobenius acts on the $12$ symmetry slots
by *translation* by its own class $g$, and translation orbits in a group are
never ragged: every orbit has exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ elements,
regardless of where it starts.

> **Theorem (Orbit purity and the $efg$ law).** In any finite abelian group $A$,
> translation by an element $g$ partitions $A$ into orbits all of the same size
> $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, so
> $(\#\text{orbits})\cdot \operatorname{ord}(g) = |A|$. At conductor $56$ this
> reads $f\cdot g = 12$ with only four possible shapes:
> $$(f,g)\in\{(1,12),\,(2,6),\,(3,4),\,(6,2)\}.$$

No prime can break into pieces of unequal degree. That rigidity — "purity" — is
what makes the whole information-theoretic story below work.

### Counting the types: the Chebotarev fingerprint

How often does each type occur? Among the $24$ reduced residues mod $56$, direct
enumeration gives

| type $t$ | how many residues | density |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $2$ | $1/12$ |
| $2$ | $6$ | $1/4$ |
| $3$ | $4$ | $1/6$ |
| $6$ | $12$ | $1/2$ |

And in the group $C_6\times C_2$, the numbers of elements of order $1,2,3,6$ are
$1,3,2,6$ — exactly half of the counts above, because two residues ($\pm a$)
correspond to each group element. The densities match perfectly:

> **Theorem (Chebotarev match).** For every $t$, the number of residues mod $56$
> of type $t$ equals twice the number of elements of order $t$ in $C_6\times C_2$;
> the type densities are $\{1/12,\,1/4,\,1/6,\,1/2\}$, precisely the order
> statistics of the Galois group.

Chebotarev's density theorem says that primes distribute themselves evenly over
symmetry classes in the long run, so these are also the limiting frequencies of
the four types among *actual primes*. And they are: among the $17\,982$ primes
below $200\,000$ (excluding $2$ and $7$) the observed frequencies are
$0.0825,\ 0.2499,\ 0.1647,\ 0.5030$ — within a few thousandths of
$1/12,\ 1/4,\ 1/6,\ 1/2$.

### Measuring the channel: full pinning

Now put on an information theorist's hat. Think of the map

$$X \;=\; (p \bmod 56) \;\longmapsto\; T \;=\; \text{type of } p$$

as a **communication channel**: a residue goes in, a type comes out. How much of
the input does the output tell you?

The relevant quantities are Shannon's. The output entropy is

$$H(T) \;=\; \tfrac1{12}\log_2 12 + \tfrac14\log_2 4 + \tfrac16\log_2 6 +
\tfrac12\log_2 2 \;=\; \frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4} \;=\; 1.72957\ldots$$

and the mutual information $I(X;T) = H(X) + H(T) - H(X,T)$ measures how much
knowledge of $X$ is conveyed by $T$. When people measure such channels
empirically, $I$ typically falls *short* of $H(T)$: noise leaks information away,
and the "pinning gap" $H(T) - I(X;T)$ is a positive number one hopes is small.

Here the gap is not small. It is zero — and not by luck.

> **Theorem (Full pinning).** Let $\varphi$ be *any* function on a finite set $S$,
> and let $X$ be uniform on $S$. Then $H(X, \varphi(X)) = H(X)$, and therefore
> $$I\big(X;\varphi(X)\big) \;=\; H\big(\varphi(X)\big).$$
> The pinning gap vanishes identically.

The proof is a one-line observation once stated correctly: for a *deterministic*
map, the joint distribution of the pair $(X,\varphi(X))$ is just the distribution
of $X$ redrawn on a graph, so the joint entropy is the input entropy, and the
mutual information collapses to the whole output entropy. Determinism *is* full
pinning. What makes this a theorem about arithmetic rather than about entropy is
the input fact — that the type of a prime is a function of $p \bmod 56$ at all.
Nature did not have to be that kind.

So at conductor $56$:

$$I(p \bmod 56\,;\,T) \;=\; H(T) \;=\; \frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4}
\;=\; 1.7296 \text{ bits (to four places)}, \qquad \text{gap} = 0 .$$

Full pinning is not the same as invertibility. The residue itself carries
$H(X) = \log_2 24 = 3 + \log_2 3 \approx 4.585$ bits, so the residual uncertainty
is

$$H(X\mid T) \;=\; \frac53 + \frac{3\log_2 3}{4} \;\approx\; 2.8554 \text{ bits} > 0 .$$

The channel is perfectly faithful about *what it reports* and profoundly lossy
about everything else. It is a coarsening, cleanly executed.

### The punchline: one number, one group

Here is the result that makes the measured value more than bookkeeping. There
are two abelian groups of order $12$; compute the entropy of the order of a
uniformly random element in each.

For $C_{12}$ the orders $1,2,3,4,6,12$ occur $1,1,2,2,2,4$ times, giving

$$H(C_{12}) \;=\; \frac56 + \log_2 3 \;=\; 2.41830\ldots$$

For $C_6\times C_2$ the orders $1,2,3,6$ occur $1,3,2,6$ times, giving

$$H(C_6\times C_2) \;=\; \frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4} \;=\; 1.72957\ldots$$

These are different — and they are separated by a clean threshold:

> **Theorem (Entropy separation at order twelve).**
> $$H(C_{12}) \;=\; \frac56 + \log_2 3 \;>\; 2 \;>\; \frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4}
> \;=\; H(C_6\times C_2).$$
> Consequently, if a degree-$12$ abelian symmetry group has order-profile entropy
> below $2$ bits, it cannot be cyclic.

Read that again with the measurement in hand. We observed $1.7296$ bits. That is
below $2$. Therefore the group is not $C_{12}$. **A single real number, measured
from nothing but remainders of primes, certifies the isomorphism class of the
Galois group of a degree-$12$ field.**

The bracketing is rigorous, not floating-point folklore: from the integer
inequalities $2^{84} < 3^{53}$ and $3^{147} < 2^{233}$ one gets
$84/53 < \log_2 3 < 233/147$, whence
$1.7295 < \frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4} < 1.7296$ and $2 < \frac56 + \log_2 3$.

### Semiprimes get the same answer

One more surprise. Suppose you cannot observe a prime, only a product $n = pq$ of
two of them — the situation in a great deal of computational number theory. The
type of $n$ is determined by the type of the product residue $uv$. Does the
blurring cost you information?

It costs nothing at all.

> **Theorem (Semiprime pair law).** Let $S$ be a finite set closed under
> multiplication by each of its elements (as the reduced residues mod $56$ are),
> and let $T$ be any function on it. Then for every value $t$,
> $$\#\{(u,v)\in S\times S : T(uv) = t\} \;=\; |S|\cdot \#\{w\in S: T(w) = t\}.$$
> In particular, at conductor $56$ the pair counts are
> $48, 144, 96, 288$ out of $576$ — densities $1/12, 1/4, 1/6, 1/2$ again — so a
> semiprime transmits exactly the same $1.72957\ldots$ bits as a prime.

The reason is a symmetry argument that fits in a sentence: for each fixed $u$,
the map $v\mapsto uv$ permutes $S$, so the inner count is the same for every
$u$ and equals the single-element count. Translation invariance is the whole
mechanism, and it is the same mechanism as orbit purity: in a group, everything
is homogeneous, and homogeneity is what lets a local count become a global law.

### Why bother?

Three reasons, in increasing order of ambition.

**First, it is a check.** The claim "the Galois group of
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{56})^{+}$ is $C_6\times C_2$" is classical, and a specialist
could tell you so from the factorisation $56 = 8\cdot 7$. But the entropy route
arrives at the same conclusion through a completely different door — counting
prime remainders and computing a Shannon entropy — and the two doors opening on
the same room is the kind of thing that makes a framework trustworthy.

**Second, it is a measurement instrument.** The order-profile entropy is a single
real number attached to a finite abelian group, computable from data. At order
$12$ it is a *complete* invariant: it distinguishes the only two candidates. Is
it complete in general? That is exactly the right next question, and it has a
plausible mechanism behind it: the order profile of an abelian group is a
combinatorial object built from divisor counts, and its entropy is always a
rational combination of $\log_2 p$ over the primes $p$ dividing the order. Since
those logarithms are rationally independent, an *analytic* coincidence between
two such entropies would force a *combinatorial* coincidence between the profiles
— which is to say, a real theorem hides behind the numerology.

**Third, it is a template.** Nothing about the entropy computation, the pinning
theorem, the orbit-purity law or the semiprime law used the number $56$. They
were all proved for arbitrary finite abelian groups and arbitrary
translation-invariant sample sets, then specialised. The degree-$12$ rung is a
test case for a machine that runs at every conductor: pick $f$, form the real
cyclotomic field, measure the type entropy of primes, and read off structural
facts about a Galois group you never had to construct.

### The moral

A prime number, asked only "what is your remainder mod $56$?", surrenders a
number of bits that can be computed exactly: $\frac43 + \frac{\log_2 3}{4}$. That
number is not an approximation, not a statistic that converges, not a quantity
with error bars. It is a closed form, and it is small enough — below $2$ — that
it eliminates one of only two possible worlds.

The remainders know the shape of the symmetry. All we had to do was count them
properly, and listen to how many bits came back.

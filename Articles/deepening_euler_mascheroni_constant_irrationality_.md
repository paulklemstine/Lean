# The Constant Hidden in a Ladder of Random Waiting Times

## A meeting of two mathematical worlds

Imagine a sequence of clocks. The first rings after a random waiting time with average length $1$; the second runs twice as fast and waits, on average, $1/2$; the third waits $1/3$; and so on. Each clock follows an exponential distribution, the standard model for a memoryless waiting time. Moving from one clock to the next changes only its rate, yet the change carries a small, precisely measurable amount of information.

Add all those information changes forever and a famous constant appears:

$$
\gamma=0.5772156649\ldots,
$$

the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

This identity connects two subjects that at first seem unrelated. The constant $\gamma$ comes from comparing the harmonic sum

$$
H_n=1+\frac12+\frac13+\cdots+\frac1n
$$

with the logarithm. Information theory, meanwhile, measures how distinguishable probability distributions are. The bridge between them says that $\gamma$ is the total distinguishability accumulated while climbing the infinite ladder of exponential rates $1,2,3,\ldots$.

The result does more than give a novel interpretation. It explains positivity term by term, produces transparent finite approximations, and supplies explicit rational bounds on every increment. It also reveals structural properties—scale invariance, a sharp equality condition, and a square formula for the symmetrized discrepancy—that apply to any two positive exponential rates.

## Why the harmonic series leaves a constant behind

The harmonic sums grow without bound, but only barely. Their leading growth is logarithmic. If one subtracts $\log n$ from $H_n$, the difference approaches $\gamma$. An equivalent shifted approximation, especially convenient here, is

$$
G_n=H_n-\log(n+1).
$$

This sequence also converges to $\gamma$. The shift from $n$ to $n+1$ is harmless in the limit, but it turns $G_n$ into an exact partial sum of positive local increments.

Define the $k$th increment, beginning at $k=0$, by

$$
g_k=\frac{1}{k+1}-\log\!\left(\frac{k+2}{k+1}\right).
$$

The logarithms telescope:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\log\!\left(\frac{k+2}{k+1}\right)
=\log\!\left(\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{k+2}{k+1}\right)
=\log(n+1).
$$

The reciprocal terms sum to $H_n$. Consequently,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}g_k=H_n-\log(n+1)=G_n.
$$

Passing to the limit gives the positive-term series

$$
\gamma=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}
\left[\frac{1}{k+1}-\log\!\left(\frac{k+2}{k+1}\right)\right].
$$

Why is each bracket positive? The elementary inequality $\log x<x-1$ for positive $x\ne1$ answers immediately. Substituting $x=(k+2)/(k+1)$ gives $x-1=1/(k+1)$, hence $g_k>0$. Thus $G_n$ increases steadily toward $\gamma$; every additional term contributes a genuine positive amount.

## Information as distinguishability

For two probability densities $p$ and $q$, the Kullback–Leibler divergence is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)=\int p(x)\log\!\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx.
$$

It is not a distance: reversing $p$ and $q$ generally changes its value. But it is always nonnegative, and it vanishes exactly when the two distributions agree almost everywhere. Operationally, it measures the expected logarithmic penalty for using model $q$ when data are generated by model $p$.

An exponential distribution of rate $\lambda>0$ has density

$$
p_\lambda(x)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x},\qquad x\ge0.
$$

A direct integration yields a compact formula for the divergence from rate $\lambda$ to rate $\mu$:

$$
D(\lambda\|\mu)
=\log\!\left(\frac{\lambda}{\mu}\right)+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}-1.
$$

The nonnegativity of this expression is another form of $\log x\le x-1$. Indeed, with $x=\mu/\lambda$,

$$
D(\lambda\|\mu)=x-1-\log x\ge0.
$$

Equality holds precisely when $x=1$, or $\lambda=\mu$. Distinct positive rates therefore have strictly positive divergence.

Now choose consecutive integer rates $\lambda=k+1$ and $\mu=k+2$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
D(k+1\|k+2)
&=\log\!\left(\frac{k+1}{k+2}\right)
  +\frac{k+2}{k+1}-1\\
&=\frac{1}{k+1}-\log\!\left(\frac{k+2}{k+1}\right)\\
&=g_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the central bridge: each classical Euler–Mascheroni increment is exactly the information divergence from one exponential clock to the next.

## The accumulated-information theorem

The preceding identities combine into a concise statement.

**Accumulated-Information Theorem.** Let $D(\lambda\|\mu)$ denote the Kullback–Leibler divergence from the exponential distribution of positive rate $\lambda$ to that of positive rate $\mu$. Then

$$
\gamma=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}D(k+1\|k+2).
$$

Moreover, for every nonnegative integer $n$,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}D(k+1\|k+2)=H_n-\log(n+1),
$$

and these partial sums increase to $\gamma$.

The theorem turns an asymptotic discrepancy into a path length of sorts—not a metric length, because KL divergence is asymmetric, but a cumulative information cost along a particular path through a family of probability laws. Early steps carry most of the cost. Moving from rate $1$ to rate $2$ contributes $1-\log2\approx0.30685$. Moving from $2$ to $3$ contributes about $0.09453$. Farther up the ladder, adjacent clocks are relatively more alike, and their divergences shrink quadratically.

## How quickly do the increments fade?

The increments admit a clean rational upper bound:

$$
0<g_k\le
\frac{1}{(k+1)(2k+3)}
\le\frac{1}{2(k+1)^2}.
$$

The first estimate comes from a useful lower bound for the logarithm. For $x>0$,

$$
\log(1+x)\ge\frac{2x}{2+x}.
$$

To see why, consider

$$
f(x)=\log(1+x)-\frac{2x}{2+x}.
$$

Here $f(0)=0$, and differentiation gives

$$
f'(x)=\frac{x^2}{(1+x)(2+x)^2}>0
$$

for $x>0$. Hence $f(x)>0$. Substituting $x=1/(k+1)$ and rearranging produces the rational bound above. The simpler inverse-square estimate follows by comparing denominators.

This quadratic decay has an intuitive information-theoretic meaning. At a high rate $k+1$, increasing the rate by one is only a relative perturbation of size about $1/(k+1)$. Divergences between nearby statistical models are second order in a small relative perturbation, so the information cost is on the scale of $1/(2(k+1)^2)$.

The estimate also makes convergence visible without relying on delicate cancellation. Since the series $\sum 1/(k+1)^2$ converges, the total accumulated information is finite.

## Geometry inside the rate family

The divergence has two further structural features. First, it ignores the units in which rates are measured. For any positive scale factor $c$,

$$
D(c\lambda\|c\mu)=D(\lambda\|\mu).
$$

Only the ratio $\mu/\lambda$ matters. Whether rates are measured per second or per hour, their informational discrepancy is unchanged.

Second, adding the two directional divergences removes the logarithms:

$$
D(\lambda\|\mu)+D(\mu\|\lambda)
=\frac{\lambda}{\mu}+\frac{\mu}{\lambda}-2.
$$

For nonzero rates this becomes the manifest square

$$
D(\lambda\|\mu)+D(\mu\|\lambda)
=\frac{(\lambda-\mu)^2}{\lambda\mu}.
$$

For positive rates the right side is visibly nonnegative and vanishes exactly when the rates coincide. This formula is an exact relative-square law. It shows that the symmetrized information gap is governed by the squared difference of the rates, normalized by their geometric product.

For consecutive rates it reads

$$
D(k+1\|k+2)+D(k+2\|k+1)
=\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}.
$$

Thus the two-way discrepancy along the integer ladder has an especially simple rational form, even though either direction alone contains a logarithm.

## Reading the first few steps

The first transition, from rate $1$ to rate $2$, already contributes

$$
D(1\|2)=1-\log 2\approx0.3068528.
$$

That is more than half of the eventual total. The next transition contributes

$$
D(2\|3)=\frac12-\log\!\left(\frac32\right)\approx0.0945349,
$$

and the third contributes about $0.0456513$. After only three steps, the accumulated value is already about $0.447039$. This rapid early growth followed by a long, gentle tail is characteristic of an inverse-square series.

The finite identity gives two complementary ways to understand the same number. One can regard

$$
G_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}D(k+1\|k+2)
$$

as a sum of local statistical discrepancies. Or one can regard

$$
G_n=H_n-\log(n+1)
$$

as the global gap between discrete and continuous accumulation. The equality says that the global gap is assembled exactly from local failures of the approximation $\log(1+x)\approx x$.

This perspective also clarifies why the sum is finite. The rate always rises by one, but KL divergence responds to *relative* rather than absolute change. Going from $1$ to $2$ doubles the rate. Going from $1000$ to $1001$ changes it by only one part in a thousand. Because nearby-model divergence is quadratic in relative change, the latter step costs roughly one part in two million.

There is a practical numerical lesson here. Directly computing $H_n-\log(n+1)$ subtracts two quantities that both grow, slowly but without bound. Summing the positive increments instead builds the approximation from small, same-sign pieces. For very large $k$, the expression should be evaluated as

$$
\frac1{k+1}-\log1p\!\left(\frac1{k+1}\right),
$$

where $\log1p(x)$ is a numerical operation designed to calculate $\log(1+x)$ accurately when $x$ is tiny. The mathematics therefore points naturally toward a stable computational method.

## Why this viewpoint matters

The Euler–Mascheroni constant appears wherever discrete accumulation is compared with continuous growth: harmonic sums versus logarithmic integrals, sums versus areas, and arithmetic scales versus smooth approximations. The exponential family is itself a model of scale. Its rate controls the pace of a memoryless process, while KL divergence records the cost of mistaking one pace for another. Their meeting is therefore not accidental: both are built from ratios, logarithms, and the difference between a quantity and its first-order approximation.

The bridge suggests practical ways to explore $\gamma$. One may compute it by summing divergences, inspect how each rate transition contributes, or compare exact increments with their rational envelopes. It also suggests broader questions. What happens along polynomial, geometric, or irregular sequences of rates? When does the sum of adjacent divergences converge? Can symmetric divergence provide sharp tail controls? Can corrected partial sums produce faster approximations suitable for arithmetic investigations of $\gamma$?

The deepest unresolved mystery remains untouched: no one knows whether $\gamma$ is rational or irrational. Yet progress on a constant often comes from giving it many identities and many interpretations. Here, a number born from the gap between counting and integration becomes the total information lost while one idealized clock is repeatedly replaced by a slightly faster one.

There is also a broader lesson about constants. A decimal expansion tells us how large a number is, but an identity can tell us what the number *means*. Here $\gamma$ is not merely a residue left by harmonic sums. It is an exact budget of distinguishability, divided among an infinite sequence of increasingly similar random processes. Analysis supplies the limit, probability supplies the models, and information theory supplies the unit of comparison.

The ladder never ends. Its steps become imperceptibly small. But together they retain exactly $\gamma$ units of information.

# What a Polynomial Whispers About a Prime

*How much can you learn about a prime number just by watching a polynomial fall apart modulo it? One formula answers the question for every polynomial, and it is written in the language of symmetry.*

---

## A game with primes

Take the polynomial $f(x) = x^4 - 2$ and a prime $p$, and reduce the coefficients modulo $p$. Over the integers $x^4-2$ cannot be factored, but modulo a prime it often can. Modulo $7$ it has two roots ($2^4 = 16 \equiv 2$ and $5^4 = 625 \equiv 2$) and splits as two linear factors times a quadratic. Modulo $5$ it stays irreducible. Modulo $17$ it has no roots, yet it splits into two quadratics. Modulo $73$ it splits completely into four linear factors.

The list of degrees of the irreducible pieces, like $1+1+2$ or $2+2$ or $1+1+1+1$, is called the **splitting type** of $f$ at $p$. Now for the game. I pick a large prime $p$ and don't tell you what it is. I only tell you the splitting type of $x^4-2$ modulo $p$. What can you say about $p$?

It sounds like a party trick, but it is a real question about information leaking out of an algebraic computation. Factoring polynomials over finite fields is a basic subroutine in computer algebra and cryptography, so it is worth knowing exactly what a factorization pattern gives away about the modulus behind it. That is a quantitative question. The natural unit is the **bit**, and the natural tool is Shannon's information theory.

This article is about a single law that settles the question: it says exactly how many bits the splitting type carries about the residue class of $p$, for every polynomial at once. It also covers the corrections, sharpenings and surprises that came out of stating the law precisely.

## The hidden machine: symmetry decides everything

The key fact goes back to Frobenius and Chebotarev. Every polynomial $f$ of degree $n$ has a **Galois group** $G$, a group of permutations of its $n$ complex roots that records all the algebraic symmetries among them. For $x^4-2$, whose roots are $\pm\sqrt[4]{2}$ and $\pm i\sqrt[4]{2}$, the group is the symmetry group of a square, the **dihedral group** $D_4$ with $8$ elements.

For each prime $p$ that doesn't divide the discriminant, there is a distinguished element of $G$ called the **Frobenius element**, well defined up to conjugation. Two things are true of it:

1. **The splitting type is the cycle type.** Write the Frobenius permutation as a product of disjoint cycles. The cycle lengths are exactly the degrees of the irreducible factors of $f$ modulo $p$. A 4-cycle means $f$ stays irreducible, and a pair of 2-cycles means two quadratics.
2. **Chebotarev's density theorem.** As $p$ runs over the primes, the Frobenius element is *equidistributed* in $G$: every group element is hit equally often in the long run.

There is a third ingredient, from class field theory. The **derived subgroup** $G'$ is the subgroup generated by all commutators $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. It measures how far $G$ is from being commutative, and the quotient $G/G'$ (the **abelianization**) is the largest commutative shadow of $G$. Class field theory says that the coset of $G'$ containing the Frobenius element is determined by **$p$ modulo a fixed integer** $m$, the conductor. For $x^4-2$ the abelianization has four elements and $m = 8$, so the coset is fixed by $p \bmod 8$.

So the game turns into a question about finite groups. Pick a uniformly random element $g$ of $G$. Let $T$ be its cycle type, which stands for the splitting type, and let $c$ be its coset of $G'$, which stands for the residue class of $p$. How much information does $T$ carry about $c$?

The answer is a *mutual information* $I(c;T)$, measured in bits. We call it the **type channel**.

## The law

Two quantities from information theory are enough. The **entropy** $H(X)$ of a random label is its average surprise in bits: a fair coin has one bit, a fair four-sided die has two. The **conditional entropy** $H(X \mid Y)$ is the surprise left in $X$ once $Y$ is known. The mutual information $I(c;T)$ is how much knowing $T$ cuts the uncertainty about $c$.

> **The Unified Type-Channel Law.** For every finite Galois group $G$ with derived subgroup $G'$,
> $$I(c;T) \;=\; H(T) - H(T\mid c) \;=\; \log_2 [G:G'] \;-\; H(c \mid T).$$
> Two extreme cases follow. If $G$ is **abelian**, then $I(c;T) = H(T)$, so the residue class accounts for every bit of the splitting type. If $G$ is **perfect** ($G' = G$), then $I(c;T) = 0$ and the splitting type reveals nothing whatever about $p$ modulo anything.

Each form of the law has a plain reading.

- **The first form** counts from the splitting type's side. The type holds $H(T)$ bits of randomness. Some of it, $H(T\mid c)$, is still there after you learn the coset. What remains is the channel.
- **The second form** counts from the residue's side. The coset is a uniform draw from $[G:G']$ possibilities, so it holds exactly $\log_2[G:G']$ bits. The **loss** $H(c\mid T)$ is the part of the coset that the type can't resolve. The channel is the ceiling minus the loss.

Where do the extremes come from? In an abelian group every commutator is trivial, so $G'$ is trivial and the coset *is* the group element, which of course determines the cycle type. In a perfect group, such as the alternating group $A_5$ of the famous quintic $x^5 + 20x + 16$, there is only one coset, so there is nothing to learn. A perfect group seals the channel completely.

### A correction along the way

The law is stated here more carefully than in its first informal version, which said the channel is "exactly the expected entropy of the abelianization class given the type." That is wrong, and the symmetric group $S_3$ shows why.

Take $f(x) = x^3 + x + 1$, whose Galois group is $S_3$ with six elements. Its derived subgroup is the alternating group $A_3$, so there are two cosets: the even and the odd permutations. The cycle type alone tells you the parity: the identity and the 3-cycles are even, the transpositions are odd. So $H(c \mid T) = 0$, while the channel is $I(c;T) = 1$ bit. The two quantities differ.

The correct reading is that $H(c\mid T)$ is the **loss**, i.e. the ceiling $\log_2[G:G']$ minus the channel. This is a general pattern. Whenever the type determines the coset and the abelianization is nontrivial, the loss is $0$ and the channel is $\log_2[G:G'] > 0$, so the two can never be equal.

## Squeezing the channel: the sandwich

Computing $I(c;T)$ exactly requires the whole joint distribution of cosets and types. Often you only know the type statistics and the size of $G'$. That is still enough to trap the channel:

> **The Sandwich Theorem.**
> $$\max\bigl(0,\; H(T) - \log_2|G'|\bigr) \;\le\; I(c;T) \;\le\; \min\bigl(H(T),\; \log_2[G:G']\bigr).$$

The upper bound is common sense: the channel can't exceed the information in either end. The lower bound is the interesting half. Once the coset is known, the element is confined to a set of $|G'|$ equally likely possibilities, so the leftover uncertainty about its type is at most $\log_2|G'|$ bits. Whatever the type holds beyond that has to be explained by the coset.

On $x^4-2$ the lower bound does real work. The eight elements of $D_4$ fall into four cycle types with frequencies $1, 2, 3, 2$ out of $8$, so $H(T) = \tfrac52 - \tfrac38\log_2 3 \approx 1.906$ bits. The derived subgroup is the centre $\{1, \text{half-turn}\}$ of size $2$. With no joint computation at all, the sandwich gives
$$I(c;T) \;\ge\; \tfrac32 - \tfrac38 \log_2 3 \;\approx\; 0.906 \text{ bits} \;>\; \tfrac34 \text{ bits}.$$
The exact value is $I(c;T) = \tfrac94 - \tfrac38\log_2 3 \approx 1.656$ bits out of a possible $2$. If you learn how $x^4-2$ factors modulo a secret prime, you learn about $1.66$ of the $2$ bits in $p \bmod 8$.

## It doesn't matter which polynomial you pick: universality

A polynomial is a concrete object, with a particular discriminant, particular roots and particular residue labels. The channel doesn't depend on any of that.

> **Universality.** Suppose two realisations of a Galois group are related by an injective homomorphism that carries group to group and derived subgroup to derived subgroup, and that preserves which elements share a splitting type. Then their type channels are identical, however the cosets are labelled.

The proof is short. Entropy depends only on how the sample space is *partitioned* by a readout, not on the names of the labels. An injective homomorphism moves cosets of $G'$ to cosets of the image of $G'$, so both readouts cut the group into the same pieces.

The consequences are concrete. Relabel the four roots of $x^4-2$ by swapping two of them. Inside the symmetric group $S_4$ the Galois group becomes a *different* subgroup, the stabiliser of the pairing $\{\{0,1\},\{2,3\}\}$ rather than the original square. The channel is still exactly $\tfrac94 - \tfrac38\log_2 3$ bits. The effect even reaches across degrees. The Galois closure of $x^3 - 2$ is a degree-6 field whose group $S_3$ permutes six conjugates *regularly*, with no element other than the identity fixing any of them. Its splitting types are $1^6$, $3^2$ and $2^3$, not the cubic types $1^3$, $3$ and $1\,2$. Even so, the type entropy is again $\tfrac23 + \tfrac12\log_2 3$ bits and the channel is again exactly one bit, the same as for the cubic $x^3+x+1$. The channel is a property of the group, not of the polynomial that happens to realise it.

## Batteries: stacking dials

Suppose you can read more than one "dial" for the same prime: the splitting type, plus some other observable $T_2$, such as which root the Frobenius sends a chosen root to. A collection of dials is called a **battery**. How does its information add up?

> **The Battery Chain Rule.** $I(c;(T_1,T_2)) = I(c;T_1) + I(c;T_2\mid T_1)$, where the conditional term is an average of channels over the fibres of $T_1$ and is never negative. So batteries only gain information, and they can never go above the ceiling $\log_2[G:G']$.
>
> **Saturation.** If one dial already determines the coset, every further dial is worthless: $I(c;T_2\mid T_1) = 0$ and the battery sits exactly at the ceiling.

For the symmetric group $S_4$ (for example $x^4+x+1$), the cycle type already fixes the parity, so the battery made of the type and the image of the first root carries exactly $1$ bit. The second dial adds nothing. For $x^4-2$ the story is different. The type gives $1.656$ bits and the root image gives $1$ bit on its own, but *together* they reach the full $2$ bits, because the root image still carries $2 - 1.656 \approx 0.344$ bits after the type is known.

Earlier experiments had noticed that batteries are often *super-additive*, meaning the whole beats the sum of its parts. The chain rule shows this can't be a general law. Take two copies of a complete dial, such as the coset itself. Each alone gives $\log_2[G:G']$ bits, but together they give the same $\log_2[G:G']$ bits, not twice that. Super-additivity is a property of *particular, complementary* dials. When two dials measure the same thing it fails.

## Degree six: a new rung

Until now the nonabelian channels had been worked out for degrees up to five. Degree six also carries a warning.

A convenient way to record a splitting type is by two numbers: how many linear factors there are and how many points lie on 2-cycles. That record is faithful in degrees up to four. **In degree six it merges two types**, the irreducible sextic ($6$) and the product of two cubics ($3+3$). Neither has linear or quadratic factors, so both are recorded as $(0,0)$. A faithful record must also count the points on 3-cycles.

With a faithful record, $x^6 - 2$, whose Galois group is the dihedral group $D_6$ of order $12$, gives

$$H(T) = 1 + \tfrac34\log_2 3 \approx 2.189,\qquad I(c;T) = \tfrac43 + \tfrac14 \log_2 3 \approx 1.730 \text{ bits},$$

out of a ceiling of $2$ bits, since the abelianization $D_6/C_3$ is a Klein four-group. The loss is $\tfrac23 - \tfrac14\log_2 3 \approx 0.270$ bits, and it has a concrete source. The splitting type $2+2+2$ occurs both for the half-turn of the hexagon (a rotation) and for the three edge reflections, and these sit in different cosets. When you see three quadratic factors you can't tell which case you are in.

With the unfaithful record, the same field would appear to have a channel of $1 + \tfrac14\log_2 3 \approx 1.396$ bits. **Exactly one third of a bit** vanishes into the collision between $6$ and $3+3$. Any degree-6 experiment that reuses the quartic record will under-report the channel by exactly that amount.

## Checking against real primes

Everything above is about finite groups, but Chebotarev's theorem says real primes have to follow it. The accompanying numerical demonstration factors polynomials modulo every prime below $60{,}000$ and measures the channels empirically:

| polynomial | group | residue read | empirical channel | law |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x^3-2$ | $S_3$ | $p \bmod 3$ | $1.0000$ | $1$ |
| $x^4-2$ | $D_4$ | $p \bmod 8$ | $1.6593$ | $\tfrac94-\tfrac38\log_2 3 = 1.6556$ |
| $x^6-2$ | $D_6$ | $p \bmod 24$ | $1.7273$ | $\tfrac43+\tfrac14\log_2 3 = 1.7296$ |
| $x^6-2$, quartic record | $D_6$ | $p \bmod 24$ | $1.3936$ | $1+\tfrac14\log_2 3 = 1.3962$ |
| $x^5-x-1$ | $S_5$ | Legendre symbol of $2869$ | $1.0000$ | $1$ |

The primes match the group theory to about three decimal places. The gap is the finite-sample fluctuation you would expect from roughly six thousand primes.

## What it all means

Seen from far away, the law says something simple. **The only information a factorization pattern can leak about a prime's residue class lives in the commutative shadow of the Galois group.** The non-commutative core $G'$ is sealed off. The quantity $\log_2|G'|$ caps how much of the type the residue can fail to explain, and in a perfect group that core is everything. For someone designing systems that factor polynomials modulo secret primes, this gives an exact budget. For a number theorist it is a small reinterpretation of class field theory, which reads as a theorem about how much of the Frobenius element a modulus can see. The answer comes to exactly $\log_2[G:G'] - H(c\mid T)$ bits.

Some questions are still open. For which groups is the sandwich's lower bound actually attained? The natural guess is that this happens precisely when the splitting type never repeats within a coset of $G'$. Does the dihedral family $x^{2m}-2$ have a closed-form channel for every $m$, with a strictly positive loss from $m = 3$ on? And when exactly does adding a second dial produce synergy, that is, more information together than separately? That last question comes down to how the conjugacy classes of the group sit inside its cosets.

A game about a secret prime and a polynomial ends up as a statement about symmetry. The splitting type reveals the commutative part of the Galois group, and nothing of the rest.

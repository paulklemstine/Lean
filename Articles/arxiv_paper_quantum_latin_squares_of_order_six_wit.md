# Twenty-Three Ways to Fill a Six-by-Six Quantum Grid

## When a symbol becomes a direction

A Latin square is one of mathematics’ most orderly objects. Take six symbols and place them in a $6\times 6$ grid so that every symbol occurs exactly once in each row and once in each column. No matter how ingenious the arrangement, there are still only six symbols. The grid rearranges a fixed alphabet.

Quantum Latin squares replace that alphabet with geometry. Each cell contains not a discrete symbol but a unit vector in $\mathbb C^6$. Every row must be an orthonormal basis, and every column must also be an orthonormal basis. Thus the six vectors across a row are mutually perpendicular and span the whole space; the same is true down every column.

There is one further quantum twist. A physical state does not change when its vector is multiplied by a complex number of absolute value one. The vectors $v$ and $e^{i\theta}v$ therefore describe the same **ray**. The cardinality of a quantum Latin square is the number of distinct rays appearing among its $36$ cells.

This changes the central question. A classical square of order six has cardinality exactly $6$. How many geometrically different directions can a quantum square contain while still making every row and column a perfect orthonormal basis? Three constructions answer with the striking values $19$, $21$, and $23$.

These numbers arise from two different design principles. The first uses coordinatewise multiplication of columns of a complex Hadamard matrix. Its symmetry imposes a ceiling of $21$. The second divides the ambient space into complementary pieces and thereby climbs past that ceiling to $23$.

## A multiplication table made from waves

A complex Hadamard matrix of order $6$ is a $6\times6$ matrix $H$ whose entries all have absolute value $1$ and whose columns are mutually orthogonal. If $h_0,\ldots,h_5$ are its columns, then

$$
\langle h_i,h_j\rangle=6\delta_{ij}.
$$

For vectors $x,y\in\mathbb C^6$, write $x\odot y$ for their Schur product, formed coordinate by coordinate:

$$
(x\odot y)_r=x_ry_r.
$$

Now place in cell $(i,j)$ the normalized product

$$
v_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}(h_i\odot h_j).
$$

Why does this produce a quantum Latin square? Fix $i$ and compare two entries in row $i$. Since every coordinate of $h_i$ has absolute value $1$,

$$
\langle h_i\odot h_j,h_i\odot h_k\rangle
=\sum_r \overline{h_{ir}h_{jr}}h_{ir}h_{kr}
=\sum_r\overline{h_{jr}}h_{kr}
=6\delta_{jk}.
$$

Normalization turns this into $\delta_{jk}$. Thus each row is orthonormal. The Schur product is commutative, so $v_{ij}=v_{ji}$; consequently the same argument gives orthonormal columns.

This construction has an immediate combinatorial limit. Because cell $(i,j)$ equals cell $(j,i)$, the array can have no more rays than there are unordered pairs with repetition from six labels. That number is

$$
\binom{6+1}{2}=\frac{6\cdot7}{2}=21.
$$

This is the **symmetric Schur-product bound**. It does not say every Hadamard construction reaches $21$: different unordered products may still lie on the same ray. It says only that $21$ is the largest possible value within this symmetric mechanism.

## Nineteen: one collision, precisely controlled

The first construction uses a Butson-type Hadamard matrix whose entries are eighth roots of unity. Its unordered Schur products are almost all inequivalent. There is exactly one nontrivial coincidence:

$$
[v_{01}]=[v_{25}]=[v_{34}],
$$

where $[v]$ denotes the ray generated by the nonzero vector $v$. All other unordered positions produce different rays.

The count is transparent once the collision pattern is known. The upper triangle of a $6\times6$ symmetric array, including its diagonal, contains $21$ positions. The three positions $(0,1)$, $(2,5)$, and $(3,4)$ would contribute three labels if separate, but they contribute only one. Hence two labels are lost:

$$
21-(3-1)=19.
$$

This gives the **Nineteen-Ray Theorem**: if a symmetric order-six array has the stated triple coincidence and is injective on the remaining upper-triangular representatives, then its full $36$-cell range has exactly $19$ labels.

The phrase “remaining representatives” matters. Symmetry already identifies the lower triangle with the upper triangle. Remove two of the three coincident upper-triangular positions, retain one representative of their common ray, and exactly $19$ positions remain. If their labels are pairwise distinct, every cell of the square is represented exactly once in that set.

There is a useful general lesson here. Cardinality is governed not by the complexity of all $36$ entries but by the fibers of a finite labeling map. One triple fiber and otherwise singleton fibers completely determine the answer.

## Twenty-one: saturating the symmetric ceiling

A second construction comes from a particular member of Karlsson’s three-parameter family of complex Hadamard matrices. Here no two unordered Schur products represent the same ray. Every upper-triangular position has its own label.

The resulting statement is the **Twenty-One-Ray Theorem**: for a symmetric order-six array, if the ray-labeling map is injective on the $21$ unordered index pairs, then the array has cardinality exactly $21$.

The proof is a perfect meeting of symmetry and injectivity. Symmetry says every one of the $36$ cells has the same label as some upper-triangular cell, so there can be at most $21$ labels. Injectivity says those $21$ representatives are all different, so there can be at least $21$. The two inequalities meet at equality.

Reaching the bound reveals a hidden graph. Think of six vertices, with one edge for each unordered pair of distinct vertices and one loop at each vertex. There are $15$ edges and $6$ loops, hence $21$ objects. A maximal Schur-product square assigns a unique ray to every edge and loop. Questions about symmetries of the quantum square can then be translated into questions about permutations of this looped complete graph.

## Twenty-three: escaping by splitting space

To go beyond $21$, one must abandon at least one assumption behind the bound. The third construction does not arise as a single symmetric table of Schur products. Instead it exploits the orthogonal decomposition

$$
\mathbb C^6=\mathbb C^4\oplus\mathbb C^2.
$$

Vectors supported in the first summand are orthogonal to vectors supported in the second. More importantly for counting, a nonzero ray contained in $\mathbb C^4\oplus\{0\}$ can never equal a ray contained in $\{0\}\oplus\mathbb C^2$. Their supports live in complementary worlds.

The construction arranges $19$ distinct rays in the four-dimensional summand and $4$ distinct rays in the two-dimensional summand so that every row and every column of the combined $6\times6$ array remains an orthonormal basis. Because the two ray families are disjoint, their sizes add:

$$
19+4=23.
$$

This gives the **Twenty-Three-Ray Direct-Sum Theorem**: if two finite ray families have cardinalities $19$ and $4$ and lie in complementary summands, then their union has cardinality $23$; when arranged with the required row-and-column orthogonality, they form an order-six quantum Latin square of cardinality $23$.

The counting theorem itself is elementary, but its role is structural. Orthogonality and cardinality have been separated. Geometry guarantees that rows and columns are bases. Complementary support guarantees that the two families cannot collide. Finite addition finishes the count.

## Why the classical world cannot imitate this

Suppose a classical Latin square is encoded using the six standard basis vectors $e_0,\ldots,e_5$. Every cell then belongs to one of exactly six rays. Each row contains all six symbols because it is a permutation; one row alone already exhibits every ray, while no seventh ray is available. Therefore every classical order-six Latin square has cardinality exactly $6$.

The values $19$, $21$, and $23$ are not merely larger examples of the same combinatorics. They certify behavior unavailable to computational-basis Latin squares. Quantum entries can vary continuously while maintaining rigid orthogonality constraints, allowing a much richer global vocabulary of directions.

This richness matters wherever mutually compatible bases organize information. Orthonormal bases are the coordinate frames of quantum measurement, signal analysis, and unitary information processing. A quantum Latin square is a schedule of such frames: moving across any row or down any column reveals a complete, nonredundant set of directions. High cardinality means that the schedule reuses fewer physical states globally even though every local slice remains perfectly organized.

## Certificates rather than brute force

The three results suggest a practical philosophy for studying structured quantum arrays. Separate the task into two certificates.

The **geometric certificate** checks that each row and column is orthonormal and that proposed ray equalities or inequalities really hold. For Schur-product squares, Hadamard orthogonality supplies the basis property almost automatically. For direct sums, orthogonality of complementary subspaces does much of the work.

The **combinatorial certificate** counts labels after the geometry is known. For cardinality $19$, it records one triple coincidence and distinctness elsewhere. For cardinality $21$, it records complete injectivity on unordered pairs. For cardinality $23$, it records two disjoint families of sizes $19$ and $4$.

This division makes the argument reusable. Any symmetric labeling of six-by-six positions—not only one arising from rays—obeys the same $21$-representative logic. Any two disjoint finite families—not only subspace-supported rays—obey the same additive union formula.

## A new landscape of possible cardinalities

The three constructions fit into a broader order-six spectrum that already includes cardinalities $13$, $15$, and $17$. The progression is not just numerical. The values up to $21$ can be understood through collision patterns among unordered Schur products. The value $23$ marks a change of architecture: direct sums replace a single symmetric multiplication table.

Several questions now come into focus. Is maximality generic in Karlsson’s family, failing only on special algebraic parameter sets? Can direct sums systematically realize intervals of cardinalities above $n(n+1)/2$ at composite orders? Does attaining $21$ force a generic rigidity because each ray uniquely remembers an unordered pair? Can ray cardinality quantify how far a square lies from the classical computational-basis locus?

The six-by-six grid thus becomes a small laboratory for a large idea. Local orthogonality is rigid: every row and column must be a complete basis. Global diversity is flexible: the array may contain many more rays than its dimension. The counts $19$, $21$, and $23$ show three distinct balances between repetition and novelty—one controlled collision, perfect symmetric diversity, and an additive escape through complementary dimensions.

A classical Latin square shuffles six names. A quantum Latin square choreographs directions. In order six, that choreography can call upon twenty-three distinct rays while every horizontal and vertical passage remains perfectly orthogonal.

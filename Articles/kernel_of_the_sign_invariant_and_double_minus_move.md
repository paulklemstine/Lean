# What the Mirror Cannot See: Signs, Symmetry, and the Hidden Shape of Eigenvalues

*A single plus-or-minus sign sits at the heart of some of the most celebrated formulas in number theory and geometry. It turns out that sign is blind to almost everything — and we can now say exactly what it misses.*

---

## A formula with a mirror in it

Count the solutions of a polynomial equation over a finite field, first over the field with $q$ elements, then over the field with $q^2$ elements, then $q^3$, and so on. The resulting sequence of counts looks like a random list of integers. It is not. In the 1940s André Weil predicted, and in the following decades Bernard Dwork, Alexander Grothendieck and Pierre Deligne proved, that all these numbers are controlled by a handful of complex numbers $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_d$: the "eigenvalues of Frobenius". Pack them into a polynomial

$$P(T) = (1 - \alpha_1 T)(1 - \alpha_2 T)\cdots(1 - \alpha_d T),$$

and that polynomial encodes the whole infinite sequence of counts.

The eigenvalues are not arbitrary. For a piece of the geometry of "weight" $w$, put $Q = q^w$. A deep symmetry of the underlying space, *Poincaré duality*, forces the list of eigenvalues to look the same in a mirror: if $\alpha$ is on the list, then so is $Q/\alpha$, with the same multiplicity. Applying the mirror twice gives back what you started with, so the mirror $\alpha \mapsto Q/\alpha$ is an *involution*.

The mirror leaves a trace in the polynomial. Replacing $T$ by $1/(QT)$ turns $P$ back into itself, up to a simple factor:

$$P\!\left(\frac{1}{QT}\right) = (-1)^d\,\varepsilon\, Q^{-d/2}\, T^{-d}\, P(T).$$

This is the **functional equation**, and the number $\varepsilon$ in it is the **sign**. It equals

$$\varepsilon = \frac{\alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_d}{s^d}, \qquad \text{where } s = \sqrt{Q}.$$

Signs like this one are everywhere in modern number theory. The best-known relative is the *root number* of an elliptic curve. By the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, its value $\pm 1$ predicts whether the rank of the curve, the number of independent rational points of infinite order, is even or odd. So it is worth asking a blunt question:

> **What does the sign actually know about the eigenvalues, and what can it not see?**

This article tells the story of a complete answer.

## Mirrors and the points that stand still

Everything that follows comes from the geometry of the mirror. Pair each eigenvalue $\alpha$ with its reflection $Q/\alpha$. When the two differ, their product is exactly $Q = s^2$, so the pair contributes $s^2$ to the product of all eigenvalues and $1$ to the sign. Such pairs are invisible.

The only eigenvalues that are *not* paired with a different partner are those that stand still under the mirror, the solutions of $\alpha = Q/\alpha$, that is, $\alpha^2 = Q$. There are exactly two of them: $+s = +\sqrt{Q}$ and $-s = -\sqrt{Q}$. A copy of $+s$ contributes $+1$ to the sign. A copy of $-s$ contributes $-1$.

Peel off the pairs one by one and you arrive at the first result.

> **Sign formula.** For every list of nonzero eigenvalues that is symmetric under $\alpha \mapsto Q/\alpha$,
> $$\varepsilon = (-1)^{\,n_-},$$
> where $n_-$ is the number of times the eigenvalue $-\sqrt{Q}$ occurs.

Two such systems therefore have the same sign exactly when they contain $-\sqrt{Q}$ an even number of times each, or an odd number of times each. The sign is a parity bit. That is a strikingly small amount of information to extract from a list of $d$ complex numbers.

## A conjecture about invisibility

If the sign records only the parity of $n_-$, then the rest of the eigenvalue data is invisible to it. A natural hope was that this "invisible part" could be generated by a few simple operations, so that you could walk from any system to any other system of the same sign using only elementary steps. The proposed steps were these:

1. **Permute.** Reorder the eigenvalues; the order never mattered.
2. **Double-minus move.** Insert, or delete, a *pair* of copies of $-\sqrt{Q}$. This changes $n_-$ by $2$ and leaves its parity, and hence the sign, unchanged.
3. **Twist.** Replace every eigenvalue $\alpha$ by $-\alpha$. This is a "quadratic twist", the only rescaling that respects the mirror. It multiplies the sign by $(-1)^d$, so it preserves the sign exactly when the degree $d$ is even, and it is allowed only then.

The conjecture said: *two duality-symmetric systems of the same degree have the same sign if and only if one can be turned into the other by these moves.*

One direction is easy, and it is true. Each move preserves the sign, so systems related by moves always share a sign. The moves also never break the mirror symmetry and never change whether the degree is even or odd.

The interesting direction is the converse. Is every pair of same-sign systems connected by moves? Answering it required finding *every* quantity the moves preserve.

## The core: what the moves cannot touch

Split a system into two parts:

- its **fixed-point part**: the $n_+$ copies of $+\sqrt{Q}$ and the $n_-$ copies of $-\sqrt{Q}$;
- its **core**: everything else, the genuine mirror pairs $\{\beta, Q/\beta\}$ with $\beta \neq \pm\sqrt{Q}$, kept as a collection with multiplicities.

Now watch what the moves do to these parts. Permuting changes nothing. A double-minus move changes $n_-$ by two and does not touch the core or $n_+$. A twist negates every core element and *swaps* the roles of $+\sqrt{Q}$ and $-\sqrt{Q}$.

So the core survives every move, up to a global minus sign. This leads to a complete description of the move classes.

> **Classification theorem.** Two eigenvalue lists are related by the moves if and only if:
>
> - **(odd degree)** they have the same core, the same number $n_+$ of copies of $+\sqrt{Q}$, and numbers $n_-$ of the same parity; or
> - **(even degree)** either they have the same core and both fixed-point counts agree in parity, or one core is the negative of the other and the two parities are swapped.

The proof is constructive. Every list can be walked to a canonical **normal form**: some copies of $+\sqrt{Q}$, then at most one copy of $-\sqrt{Q}$, then the core. In odd degree the number of $+\sqrt{Q}$'s is fixed. In even degree there is a neat trick: *twist, insert a double-minus pair, twist back*. The pair of $-\sqrt{Q}$'s comes out of the second twist as a pair of $+\sqrt{Q}$'s, so even-degree systems can shed pairs of $+\sqrt{Q}$ as well and reduce to at most one copy of each fixed point. Two lists with matching invariants reach the same normal form, and therefore each other.

## The verdict: true only in the tiniest cases

With the classification available, the conjecture can be tested directly, and it fails almost everywhere.

> **Exact boundary.** The conjecture holds in degree $d$ if and only if $d \le 1$.

In degree $0$ there is only the empty system. In degree $1$ the only symmetric systems are $[\sqrt{Q}]$ and $[-\sqrt{Q}]$, which have different signs. From degree $2$ on, a simple family breaks everything. For each whole number $n \ge 2$ consider

$$F_n = \Big[\, n\sqrt{Q},\ \ \tfrac{1}{n}\sqrt{Q},\ \ \underbrace{\sqrt{Q}, \dots, \sqrt{Q}}_{k \text{ copies}} \,\Big].$$

Each $F_n$ is mirror-symmetric, since $n\sqrt{Q}$ and $\sqrt{Q}/n$ reflect into each other. Each has degree $k+2$ and contains no $-\sqrt{Q}$, so its sign is $+1$. Yet:

> **Infinitely many classes with the same sign.** $F_n$ and $F_m$ are related by the moves if and only if $n = m$.

The core of $F_n$ is $\{n\sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}/n\}$, and no permutation, double-minus move or twist can change it into $\{m\sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}/m\}$ or into its negative. A single sign class therefore splits into **infinitely many** move classes. The sign really is blind to something the moves preserve, and that something is the core.

Even in the simplest systems, where every eigenvalue is $\pm\sqrt{Q}$, there is a subtlety. In even degree the conjecture does hold for such "core-free" systems. In odd degree it fails as soon as $d \ge 3$: the systems $[\sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}]$ and $[\sqrt{Q}, -\sqrt{Q}, -\sqrt{Q}]$ both have sign $+1$, but no moves connect them. Twists are forbidden in odd degree, and without them the exact count of $+\sqrt{Q}$'s cannot change.

## The repair: let any mirror pair come and go

The failure points to its own fix. The double-minus move inserts the pair $\{-\sqrt{Q}, -\sqrt{Q}\}$, which is one particular mirror pair, since $-\sqrt{Q}$ is its own reflection. Why stop there? Allow the insertion or deletion of **any** mirror pair $\{\beta, Q/\beta\}$ with $\beta \neq 0$. Such a pair always multiplies the product of eigenvalues by $Q$ and adds $2$ to the degree, so the sign is unchanged.

With these *dual-pair moves* (and permutations, with no twists needed) the picture becomes as simple as it could be.

> **Repaired theorem.** Two duality-symmetric systems of the same degree have the same sign *if and only if* they are related by permutations and insertions or deletions of mirror pairs. This holds in every degree.

The proof again goes through normal forms. Keep deleting mirror pairs, whether genuine pairs from the core, pairs of $+\sqrt{Q}$, or pairs of $-\sqrt{Q}$, until nothing deletable is left. What remains is one of just four systems:

$$[\ ], \qquad [\sqrt{Q}, -\sqrt{Q}], \qquad [\sqrt{Q}], \qquad [-\sqrt{Q}].$$

These have (even degree, sign $+1$), (even degree, sign $-1$), (odd degree, sign $+1$) and (odd degree, sign $-1$) respectively. Every symmetric eigensystem reduces to exactly one of the four, and the degree together with the sign tells you which.

So the functional equation sees exactly two things: the degree and the sign. Everything else, the entire core with all its continuous parameters, is invisible, and it is generated precisely by letting mirror pairs appear and disappear. The double-minus moves alone are strictly too weak: $[2\sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}/2]$ and $[3\sqrt{Q}, \sqrt{Q}/3]$ are related by a single deletion followed by a single insertion of mirror pairs, but by no sequence of the original moves.

## Why a negative result is good news

It is tempting to file "the conjecture is false" under failure. Here it is the opposite. The investigation produced three things that are each more useful than a bare yes:

- **An exact formula** for the sign, as the parity of the number of $-\sqrt{Q}$ eigenvalues.
- **A complete classification** of the original moves, which identifies the core as the finer invariant hidden behind the sign.
- **A corrected statement** that is true in every degree and has only four normal forms.

This is how classifications usually develop in mathematics. You propose generators for a symmetry and test them against a complete list of invariants. Where they fall short, the missing invariant tells you which generator to add.

## Where the story goes next

The repaired theorem treats eigenvalues as arbitrary complex numbers. In geometry they are algebraic: the polynomial $P(T)$ has integer coefficients, and every eigenvalue has absolute value exactly $\sqrt{Q}$. Can the walk between two systems of equal sign be carried out so that *every intermediate stop* is also an honest integer polynomial of this kind? A mirror pair of such eigenvalues is cut out by an integer quadratic $T^2 - aT + Q$ with $|a| \le 2\sqrt{Q}$, so the steps would have to be grouped into conjugate families. That bookkeeping is the next challenge.

A second question asks for the *shape* of the space of cores. A mirror pair $\{\beta, Q/\beta\}$ is determined by the single number $\beta + Q/\beta$, its "trace", and the twist simply negates traces. In degree $2k$, with the fixed-point data held fixed, this suggests that the classes under the original moves form a symmetric power of a punctured plane modulo the sign flip $z \mapsto -z$. The third question asks how the answer changes for systems that carry a symmetric or an alternating pairing, where the sign becomes a determinant and alternating forms always have determinant $+1$.

The broad answer to the opening question is already settled. In the mirror-symmetric world of eigenvalues the sign sees a single bit and the degree. Everything else can be removed and restored one mirror pair at a time without the functional equation noticing.

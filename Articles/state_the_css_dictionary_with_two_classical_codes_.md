# The Shape of a Quantum Code

*How a dictionary between matrices, geometry, and quantum operators exposes a beautiful — and false — folk theorem about the hypercube.*

---

## A rumour about a cube

Here is a claim that circulates in the quantum error-correction folklore, and which sounds entirely plausible: *take the $n$-dimensional hypercube, put a qubit on every edge, and you obtain a quantum code that stores one logical qubit and protects it with a distance that grows like $2^{n/2}$.* The hypercube is highly symmetric, its shortest cycle has length $4$, and cycles are the classic source of protection in topological codes. What could go wrong?

Almost everything, it turns out. Once you write the claim down precisely, the hypercube code stores not one logical qubit but $2^{n-1}(n-2)+1$ of them — for $n=6$ that is $129$ logical qubits, not one — and its distance is not $2^{n/2}$, not even $4$, but exactly $1$. It corrects nothing at all.

This article is about what it takes to see that clearly. The answer is a **dictionary**: a precise translation between three languages that quantum coding theory habitually blurs together — parity-check matrices, chain complexes of vector spaces, and groups of quantum operators. Once the dictionary is written out, the hypercube claim collapses in a couple of lines, and something more valuable takes its place: a clean criterion for when a quantum code can be drawn as a graph at all.

---

## Three languages, one object

### Language one: nested classical codes

A classical binary linear code is a subspace $C \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^N$ — a set of bit strings closed under bitwise XOR. The most economical way to present one is by a **parity-check matrix** $H$: the code is
$$\mathcal{C}(H) = \ker H = \{v \in \mathbb{F}_2^N : Hv = 0\},$$
the strings on which every check evaluates to zero.

The CSS recipe (after Calderbank, Shor, and Steane) builds a quantum code out of two nested classical codes $C_2 \subseteq C_1 \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2^N$, and the logical information lives in the quotient $C_1/C_2$. This is the version most often quoted, and it is the version that is hardest to check, because "nested" is a statement about infinitely many vectors.

### Language two: orthogonality of matrices

Alongside the quotient picture sits an entirely different-looking condition. Given two check matrices $H_X$ and $H_Z$, the CSS construction demands
$$H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0 .$$
This is a finite condition — just multiply two matrices — and it is what anyone actually verifies in practice.

The bridge between the two languages is the orthogonal complement. Write $\langle v, w\rangle = \sum_i v_i w_i$ for the standard bilinear form on $\mathbb{F}_2^N$ (or on $K^N$ for any field $K$), and let $W^\perp$ denote the set of vectors orthogonal to everything in $W$. Then:

> **Kernel–Row-Space Duality.** For any check matrix $H$, the code it checks is the orthogonal complement of the code its rows generate:
> $$\ker H = \bigl(\operatorname{rowspace} H\bigr)^{\perp}.$$

The proof is the adjunction $\langle H^{\mathsf T}y,\, v\rangle = \langle y,\, Hv\rangle$, read in both directions, together with the fact that the form is nondegenerate: only the zero vector is orthogonal to all of $K^N$. From this, the equivalence of languages one and two is immediate:

> **Nesting = Orthogonality.** $\operatorname{rowspace} H_Z \subseteq \ker H_X$ if and only if $H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0$.

One direction is a one-line computation; the other tests the inclusion on the standard basis vectors, which is exactly what the matrix product does entry by entry. And since transposing turns $H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0$ into $H_Z H_X^{\mathsf T} = 0$, the condition is symmetric in $X$ and $Z$ — the familiar $X \leftrightarrow Z$ duality of CSS codes is nothing more than the symmetry of matrix transposition.

### Language three: commuting quantum operators

The third language is the physical one. A phase-free Pauli operator on $N$ qubits is a pair $(a\mid b)$ of bit strings: $a$ records where an $X$ acts, $b$ records where a $Z$ acts. Two such operators commute precisely when the **symplectic form**
$$\bigl\langle (a_1\mid b_1),\,(a_2\mid b_2)\bigr\rangle_{\mathrm{symp}} = a_1 \cdot b_2 + a_2 \cdot b_1$$
vanishes over $\mathbb{F}_2$. A stabilizer code is defined by an *abelian* group of such operators; abelian means, in this language, **isotropic** — the form vanishes on every pair.

The CSS stabilizer group is generated by the rows of $H_X$, read as pure-$X$ operators $(h\mid 0)$, and the rows of $H_Z$, read as pure-$Z$ operators $(0\mid h)$. And the third face of the dictionary says:

> **Abelian = Orthogonal.** The group generated by these operators is isotropic if and only if $H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0$.

Note that this is an *if and only if*: the hard direction is not "orthogonality implies commuting" (which is a computation on generators, extended to the whole span by bilinearity) but the converse, which recovers each entry of $H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T}$ by evaluating the symplectic form on one $X$-generator against one $Z$-generator.

Putting the three together closes the triangle:
$$C_2 \subseteq C_1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{the stabilizer group is abelian.}$$

These are not three "morally equivalent" statements — they are identities of subspaces and groups, valid with no finiteness or basis assumptions, and the first two hold over any field.

## Geometry enters: the chain complex

Here is where the geometry comes in. A commuting pair $(H_X, H_Z)$ is exactly the same data as a two-step **chain complex**
$$K^{m_Z} \xrightarrow{\;H_Z^{\mathsf T}\;} K^{N} \xrightarrow{\;H_X\;} K^{m_X}, \qquad H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0 .$$
The condition $H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T} = 0$ is precisely the requirement that "the boundary of a boundary is zero", the defining law of homology. Its cycles are $C_1 = \ker H_X$, its boundaries are $C_2 = \operatorname{rowspace} H_Z$, and its homology is the quotient $C_1/C_2$. That quotient *is* the logical space of the code. Counting dimensions:

> **The CSS Dimension Formula.** With $k = \dim C_1/C_2$ the number of logical qubits,
> $$k + \dim C_2 = \dim C_1, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad k + \operatorname{rank} H_X + \operatorname{rank} H_Z = N .$$

Geometry is not a metaphor here: if the middle space is spanned by the edges of a graph and $H_X$ is the incidence matrix, then $\ker H_X$ is the cycle space and homology counts independent loops. Quantum codes really are made of holes.

## Distance: two numbers, not one

Dimension is only half the story; the parameter that decides whether a code is useful is its **distance** — the weight of the lightest error the code cannot see. Here is where the hypercube rumour goes wrong, and here is the precise statement that stops it.

Call a Pauli error $(a\mid b)$ **undetectable** if it commutes with every stabilizer, i.e. $H_Z a = 0$ and $H_X b = 0$. Such an error is harmless if it *is* a stabilizer, and disastrous otherwise. The distance is
$$d = \min\{\,\lvert (a\mid b)\rvert \;:\; (a\mid b) \text{ undetectable, not a stabilizer}\,\},$$
where $\lvert (a\mid b)\rvert$ counts the qubits on which the operator acts at all — the size of the union of the supports of $a$ and $b$.

Two special cases have geometric names. The **systole** $d_X$ is the minimum weight of a pure-$X$ undetectable non-stabilizer error: a nonzero homology class of least weight. The **cosystole** $d_Z$ is its mirror image: a nonzero cohomology class of least weight. The subtlety is that the definition of $d$ allows *mixed* errors, acting with $X$ on some qubits and $Z$ on others, so a priori a clever mixed error could beat both single-type minima.

It cannot:

> **The Distance Theorem.** If a code has both an $X$-type and a $Z$-type logical operator, then
> $$d = \min(d_X, d_Z).$$

The proof rests on a structural fact worth stating separately: the CSS stabilizer group is exactly the **product** $\operatorname{rowspace} H_X \times \operatorname{rowspace} H_Z$. Membership is therefore componentwise — an operator is a stabilizer iff its $X$-part is a combination of $X$-checks *and* its $Z$-part is a combination of $Z$-checks. So a minimum-weight undetectable non-stabilizer error must fail componentwise in at least one sector; its failing component alone is a single-type logical operator of no greater weight, which pins $d \ge \min(d_X, d_Z)$. The other inequality is witnessed by padding a minimal single-type logical with an identity in the other sector.

The hypotheses are not decorative. If a code has no logical operators of one type, the corresponding minimum is over the empty set and the statement degenerates. That is exactly the situation we are about to meet.

## The hypercube, dismantled

Now build the object the rumour is about. The $n$-cube $Q_n$ has $2^n$ vertices — the bit strings of length $n$ — and an edge for every pair differing in one coordinate. Naming each edge by its *lower* endpoint (the one with a $0$ in the flipped direction) counts each edge exactly once and gives
$$N = n\,2^{\,n-1}$$
edges, hence $n 2^{n-1}$ physical qubits. The boundary matrix $\partial_1$ has one row per vertex, one column per edge, and marks an edge's two endpoints.

**Step one: the rank.** The transpose $\partial_1^{\mathsf T}$ is the discrete derivative: it sends a function $f$ on vertices to the function $(x,i) \mapsto f(x) + f(x + e_i)$ on edges. Its kernel therefore consists of the functions unchanged by flipping any single coordinate — and any two vertices of the cube are joined by a sequence of coordinate flips, so such a function is constant. An induction on Hamming weight makes this rigorous. The kernel is thus the one-dimensional line of constants, and rank–nullity (plus the equality of row rank and column rank) gives
$$\operatorname{rank} \partial_1 = 2^n - 1 .$$
Read homologically, this is the statement that the cube is **connected**: $\beta_0 = 1$.

**Step two: the count.** A graph has no $2$-cells, so the code has no $Z$-checks at all: $H_Z = 0$. The dimension formula then gives
$$k = N - \operatorname{rank}\partial_1 = n\,2^{\,n-1} - (2^n - 1) = 2^{\,n-1}(n-2) + 1 .$$
So $k=1$ happens **exactly once**, at $n = 2$, where the cube degenerates to a square — a single loop, a single hole. For $n=3$ we already get $k=5$, and $k \ge 5$ for every $n \ge 3$; at $n=4$, thirty-two qubits carry seventeen logical qubits; at $n=6$, one hundred ninety-two carry one hundred twenty-nine. The "one logical qubit" claim is not approximately right — it is off by an exponential.

**Step three: the distance.** Here the Distance Theorem earns its keep. With no $Z$-checks, *every* edge subset is undetectable in the $X$ sector, so $d_X$ is simply the least weight of an edge set that is not a coboundary — not a cut of the graph. And a single edge of the cube is not a cut, because it lies on a square. Concretely: suppose the indicator of one edge were a coboundary, coming from some vertex function $f$. Sum the coboundary equation around the four edges of a square containing that edge. Each of the four vertex values appears exactly twice, so the left side vanishes; the right side is $1$, since exactly one of the four edges is the marked one. Over $\mathbb{F}_2$ that reads $0 = 1$. Contradiction. Hence
$$d_X(Q_n) = 1, \qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad d(Q_n) = 1 \quad (n \ge 2).$$

Where did the appealing "girth $4$" intuition go? It was measuring the wrong sector. The girth of $Q_n$ *is* $4$, and it does compute a distance — the **dual** distance $d_Z$, the lightest nonzero cycle. But the code's distance is the minimum of the two sectors, and the primal sector is catastrophically weak. A graph is a one-dimensional complex; it has cycles but no faces, so its $Z$-stabilizer group is trivial and nothing at all constrains $X$-errors. Getting a growing distance requires genuine $2$-cells — this is exactly what the toric code has and the hypercube graph code lacks.

The parameters of the hypercube homological code are therefore
$$\bigl[\bigl[\,n2^{\,n-1},\; 2^{\,n-1}(n-2)+1,\; 1\,\bigr]\bigr],$$
and the correct way to test them against a bound is with the block length $N = n2^{n-1}$, the number of edge qubits — not with the cube dimension $n$, which for $n \ge 3$ is already smaller than $k$. Done properly, the quantum Singleton inequality $k + 2(d-1) \le N$ holds with slack $2^n - 2d + 1$: the code is not merely non-optimal, it is exponentially far from optimal.

## When can a code be drawn?

The hypercube story exposes a habit worth naming. It is tempting to say "every binary CSS code is a chain complex, and chain complexes are geometry, so every CSS code is a piece of geometry". The first half is true — that is the dictionary. The second half is a much stronger claim, and it is false.

Call a binary matrix a **graph incidence matrix** if every column is the indicator of two *distinct* vertices. Such matrices satisfy a parity constraint that has nothing to do with the code: every column has exactly two ones, so its entries sum to zero over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Equivalently, the all-ones vector lies in the kernel of the transpose. Rank–nullity turns that observation into a hard obstruction:

> **The Graph Rank Obstruction.** If $M$ is the incidence matrix of a graph on a nonempty vertex set $V$, then
> $$\operatorname{rank} M + 1 \le \lvert V \rvert .$$
> In particular, the $X$-checks of a graph code are **never** independent.

That single inequality settles representability questions instantly. The Steane code — the $[[7,1,3]]$ workhorse built from the $[7,4,3]$ Hamming code, whose $3 \times 7$ check matrix has full row rank $3$ — cannot be realized by a graph with its standard incidence maps, on *any* vertex set: three independent checks would need at least four vertices, but the checks are the vertices. The smallest counterexample is smaller still: the $1 \times 1$ matrix $[1]$ is a perfectly good differential, but one column cannot mark two distinct vertices when there is only one vertex.

And the obstruction is not vacuous in the other direction: the hypercube boundary matrix *is* a graph incidence matrix, and its corank-one behaviour, $\operatorname{rank}\partial_1 + 1 = 2^n$, is precisely the extremal case of the bound — with equality because the cube is connected.

## The framework, tested

A framework that only demolishes claims is not worth much. The same definitions, run on the Steane code, reconstruct its textbook parameters from nothing but the $3 \times 7$ Hamming matrix $H$ whose columns are the seven nonzero binary triples:

* $H H^{\mathsf T} = 0$: the Hamming code contains its dual, so the CSS commutation condition holds with $H_X = H_Z = H$;
* $\operatorname{rank} H = 3$, since columns $1$, $2$, $4$ form an identity block;
* $k = 7 - 3 - 3 = 1$ by the dimension formula;
* $d_X = d_Z = 3$: no undetectable non-stabilizer single-type error weighs less than three, and $X_1X_2X_3$ achieves three;
* $d = \min(3,3) = 3$ by the Distance Theorem.

Result: $[[7,1,3]]$, with $k + 2(d-1) = 5 < 7$ — comfortably inside the Singleton bound, and not saturating it.

Put the two computations side by side and the moral is stark. The Steane code has $k=1$ and $d=3$; the hypercube graph code has an exponentially large $k$ and $d=1$. Both numbers come from the *same* definitions. What distinguishes them is not symmetry, not girth, not elegance of the underlying picture, but whether the second sector of checks exists at all.

---

## What to take away

Three things.

**First, dictionaries deserve to be written down.** "Nested classical codes", "$H_X H_Z^{\mathsf T}=0$", and "abelian stabilizer group" are used interchangeably every day, and they *are* interchangeable — but only because of theorems about orthogonal complements and isotropic subspaces, and only when the underlying form is nondegenerate. Writing the equivalences out costs a page and pays for itself the first time a claim is stated in one language and tested in another.

**Second, one number is never the distance.** A homological code has two sectors, and the distance is the smaller of them. Reporting the systole and calling it the distance — or reporting the girth of a graph and calling it the distance — is exactly the error that made a distance-$1$ code look like a distance-$2^{n/2}$ code. Symmetry of the underlying object is no protection: the hypercube is as symmetric as a graph can be.

**Third, "it's a chain complex" is not "it's a shape".** Any commuting pair of matrices is a chain complex; only very special matrices are incidence matrices of an actual complex. One column-weight parity constraint separates the two worlds, and it already rules out the most famous small quantum code there is.

The hypercube remains a beautiful object. It just does not store one qubit — and it does not protect anything.

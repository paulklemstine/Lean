# The Ladder That Loses Its Voice

## How much can a prime number tell you about itself?

Pick a prime number — say $p = 47$ — and ask it a question. Not an arithmetic question like "are you odd?", but a *structural* one: how does $p$ behave inside a larger world of numbers?

Number theorists have a canonical way of asking this. You extend the ordinary integers by adjoining new algebraic quantities, and then you watch what happens to $p$ inside the extended system. Sometimes $p$ shatters into many pieces — it *splits completely*. Sometimes it stubbornly refuses to break at all — it stays *inert*. And sometimes it does something in between. This behaviour is the prime's **splitting type**, and it is the closest thing number theory has to a fingerprint.

The remarkable fact — the fact this article is about — is that the fingerprint is *completely legible from a single, tiny piece of data*: the remainder of $p$ upon division by a fixed modulus. Nothing is lost. Nothing is hidden. The remainder tells you the whole story.

That is the phenomenon we call **full pinning**, and this article is about proving that it is not a coincidence, measuring exactly how much information it carries, and finding the precise boundary at which it fails.

---

## The eleventh rung

The particular world we will visit is built from the number $23$. Let $\zeta_{23}$ denote a primitive $23$rd root of unity — a complex number with $\zeta_{23}^{23} = 1$ that is not $1$ itself. Adjoining it to the rationals gives a field of degree $22$. Inside it sits a smaller, purely real field: the one generated by $\zeta_{23} + \zeta_{23}^{-1}$, obtained by folding each root of unity together with its complex conjugate. This is the **maximal real subfield**, written $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{23})^{+}$, and it has degree $11$ over the rationals. Its symmetry group is cyclic of order $11$ — the smallest possible symmetry structure for that degree, and a prime one at that.

Here is the splitting law for this field, in full:

> **Splitting criterion at degree 11.** Let $p \neq 23$ be a prime. Then $p$ splits completely in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{23})^{+}$ if and only if
> $$p \equiv 1 \pmod{23} \quad\text{or}\quad p \equiv -1 \pmod{23}.$$
> Otherwise $p$ has residue degree exactly $11$: there is no middle ground.

Why the dichotomy? Because $11$ is prime. The residue degree of $p$ is the order of a certain group element, and that order must divide $11$; so it is $1$ or $11$, full stop.

Test it. $47 = 2 \cdot 23 + 1$, so $47 \equiv 1 \pmod{23}$: it splits completely. The prime $2$ is neither $1$ nor $22 \pmod{23}$: it is inert, with residue degree $11$. Two primes, two verdicts, no ambiguity, and the only computation required is a division with remainder.

There is a ladder of such fields, one rung for each prime degree, and the degree-$11$ rung sits at the eleventh position. The lower rungs are familiar: degree $2$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})$, the real subfield of the fifth roots of unity, where the splitting law is Fermat's classical criterion; degree $3$ lives inside the seventh roots of unity; degree $5$ inside the eleventh. In each case a single remainder decides everything.

## Turning arithmetic into information

Now for the twist that makes this a story about *information* rather than only about algebra.

Imagine sampling a prime at random and recording only its splitting type $T$ — a single symbol from the two-letter alphabet $\{\text{split}, \text{inert}\}$. How surprising is that symbol? By the criterion above, exactly $2$ of the $22$ nonzero residues mod $23$ produce "split", so the split density is $2/22 = 1/11$ and the inert density is $10/11$. The Shannon entropy of this two-outcome source is

$$H(T) \;=\; \log_2 11 \;-\; \tfrac{10}{11}\log_2 10 \;=\; 0.4395\ldots \text{ bits}.$$

Less than half a bit. Learning "this prime splits or it doesn't" in the degree-$11$ world is a small amount of news — but it is not *no* news, and we can pin its value precisely: $0.4394 < H(T) < 0.4396$, certified by two exact integer inequalities, namely $2^{2417}\cdot 10^{5000} < 11^{5500}$ and $11^{2200} < 2^{967}\cdot 10^{2000}$. No floating-point arithmetic is involved anywhere; those are statements about whole numbers.

Now the question that gives this whole enterprise its edge. Suppose an observer is not shown $T$ directly, but only the residue class of $p$ modulo $23$. How much of $T$'s half-bit does that observer recover?

**All of it.** The mutual information between the residue class and the splitting type is *exactly* $H(T)$, on the nose: knowing the class leaves precisely zero residual uncertainty about the type. Every conditional distribution is degenerate — a spike on a single outcome.

---

## Why it can't be otherwise

The empirical version of that statement is easy to check by computer at any one degree, and reports of that kind accumulated for degrees $2, 3, 5, 7, 11$ with no exceptions. Enumeration, though, is not explanation. The real content is a theorem that makes the exceptionless record inevitable.

> **Universal Abelian Pinning Theorem.** Let $G$ be any finite abelian group and $H \le G$ any subgroup. Define the type of an element $u \in G$ to be the order of its image $uH$ in the quotient $G/H$. Then the type is a function of the class $uH$; consequently the conditional entropy of the type given the class is identically zero, and the mutual information between class and type equals the full entropy of the type.

The proof is a single line once the statement is set up correctly, and that is the point. The type of $u$ was *defined* as an invariant of $uH$; two elements with the same class have literally the same type; therefore each fibre of the class map is monochromatic in type, each fibre carries zero entropy, and their average — the conditional entropy — vanishes.

The mathematics all lives in the setup. In an abelian Galois group the Frobenius element attached to a prime is a single group element, not merely a conjugacy class, and the subfield's decomposition data is a fixed subgroup. So the splitting type genuinely *is* a function of the Artin class, and pinning is forced.

This converts a table of experiments into a law. Degrees $2$ through $11$ hold with no exceptions — and so will degree $12$, degree $1000$, and every abelian extension anyone will ever write down. The interesting question is no longer *whether* pinning holds. It is *where it must break*.

---

## Where it breaks: the sharpness criterion

To locate the boundary, one needs to know exactly what pinning demands. It turns out to demand precisely one thing, and to demand it exactly.

> **Sharpness Criterion.** For any read-out $T$ and any side channel $k$ on a finite sample space, the conditional entropy $H(T \mid k)$ vanishes **if and only if** $k$ determines $T$ pointwise: whenever $k(x) = k(y)$, also $T(x) = T(y)$.

One direction is the argument above. The other direction is where the quantitative work sits: if some fibre of $k$ contains two points with different types, that fibre is a non-constant read-out, and a non-constant read-out has *strictly positive* entropy. (Why? Because every fibre of a non-constant map misses at least one point of the set, so each fibre has at most $N-1$ elements out of $N$, so the average log-fibre-size is at most $\log_2(N-1)$, strictly less than $\log_2 N$.) A strictly positive term in a sum of non-negative terms makes the sum strictly positive. So the conditional entropy is positive and pinning fails — strictly, by a measurable amount.

The consequence is a genuine dichotomy: the observed identity "mutual information equals the entropy of the type" is never a numerical near-miss or an artefact of rounding. It is on, or it is off, and being on is exactly the structural statement that the type factors through the channel.

With the criterion in hand, a failing channel at degree $11$ is easy to exhibit — and pleasingly concrete. Consider the *quadratic character* mod $23$: ask only whether $p$ is a perfect square modulo $23$. This is a legitimate, natural, arithmetic side channel; it captures real information about $p$. Does it pin the type?

No — and the obstruction is a single small computation: $5^2 = 25 = 2 \pmod{23}$. So $2$ is a quadratic residue mod $23$. But $2$ is *inert* in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{23})^{+}$, while $1$ — also a square, trivially — splits completely. The quadratic character merges a split class with an inert one, so by the criterion it carries strictly less than the full $0.4395$ bits.

In fact it carries strictly less than everything: it carries **nothing at all**. Passing to coordinates, write each residue mod $23$ as $g^{a}$ for a fixed primitive root $g$, so that $a$ ranges over $\mathbb{Z}/22$. The splitting type depends only on $a \bmod 11$; the quadratic character depends only on $a \bmod 2$. Since $\gcd(2, 11) = 1$, the Chinese Remainder Theorem makes these two read-outs *independent*. The mutual information between the quadratic character and the splitting type is exactly $0$ bits.

Two natural channels, mod $23$ and the Legendre symbol; one carries every last bit of the type, the other carries none. This is the sharpest possible illustration that pinning is a structural property, not a numerical accident.

## Two primes at once: a binomial law

Cryptography rarely hands you a prime; it hands you a semiprime $N = p_1p_2$. So ask the corresponding question: among the two prime factors, how many split completely in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{23})^{+}$?

If the two factors' classes are drawn uniformly and independently, the split count should be binomial, $\mathrm{Bin}(2, 1/11)$. It is — exactly, by a finite count. Among the $11^2 = 121$ pairs of classes:

- both factors split: $1$ pair;
- exactly one splits: $2 \cdot 10 = 20$ pairs;
- neither splits: $10^2 = 100$ pairs.

Those three counts sum to $121$, so the law is complete, and the general statement — $(q-1)^2$, $2(q-1)$, and $1$ out of $q^2$ at prime degree $q$ — holds at every rung.

The corresponding information question has a subtler answer. Suppose you are shown the semiprime's own class (in coordinates, the sum $a_1 + a_2$ of the two factors' discrete logarithms mod $11$). How much does *that* tell you about the number of split factors? Here there is a closed form:

$$I_{\text{split}}(q) \;=\; \log_2(q^2) \;-\; \frac{(q-1)^2\log_2\!\big((q-1)^2\big) + 2(q-1)\log_2\!\big(2(q-1)\big)}{q^2} \;-\; \left[\tfrac{1}{q}\,B(q,1) + \tfrac{q-1}{q}\,B(q,2)\right],$$

where $B(N,m) = \log_2 N - \big(m\log_2 m + (N-m)\log_2(N-m)\big)/N$ is the entropy of an $m$-versus-$(N-m)$ split. At $q = 11$ this collapses to the elegant expression

$$I_{\text{split}}(11) \;=\; \log_2 11 + \frac{180\log_2 3 - 210\log_2 5 - 210}{121} \;=\; 0.05190\ldots \text{ bits},$$

certified between $0.0516$ and $0.0521$ by the integer inequalities $2^{865}\cdot 5^{840} < 11^{484}\cdot 3^{720}$ and $11^{1210}\cdot 3^{1800} < 2^{2163}\cdot 5^{2100}$.

This number is worth pausing on, because it settled a discrepancy. A figure of $0.116$ bits had been circulated for this quantity in advance; the true value is less than half of it. The exact arithmetic is not negotiable, and it says $0.0519$. That is what it means to do this kind of work honestly: the pre-registered predictions that survive are strengthened into theorems, and the one that does not survive is corrected.

## The ladder loses its voice

Finally, step back and look at the whole ladder at once. Its rungs carry entropies

$$1, \quad 0.9183, \quad 0.7219, \quad 0.5917, \quad 0.4395 \quad \text{bits}$$

at degrees $2, 3, 5, 7, 11$. They go down. Do they always go down?

The tempting move is to check the next rung, and the next. The better move is to prove the decay for all rungs simultaneously, and it can be done with a derivative. Extend the ladder from prime degrees to *all* real numbers $x \ge 2$ by the interpolating profile

$$h(x) \;=\; \log_2 x \;-\; \frac{x-1}{x}\,\log_2(x-1),$$

which reproduces the ladder entropy at every prime input. Then

$$h'(x) \;=\; -\,\frac{\ln(x-1)}{x^2 \ln 2}.$$

Look at how clean that is. Every messy cross term cancels; what survives is a single logarithm. And its sign is transparent: at $x = 2$ we have $\ln(x-1) = \ln 1 = 0$, so the derivative vanishes exactly at the bottom rung; for every $x > 2$ we have $\ln(x-1) > 0$, so $h'(x) < 0$. Therefore:

> **Strict Decay of the Ladder.** The profile $h$ is strictly decreasing on $[2, \infty)$. Consequently, for primes $p < q$ we have $H(T_q) < H(T_p)$: the splitting-type entropy strictly decreases along the prime degrees. Since $h(2) = 1$, degree $2$ is the unique rung carrying a full bit, and every higher rung carries strictly less than one bit — while remaining strictly positive.

And the rate is pinned too. Every prime rung obeys the sandwich

$$\frac{\log_2 q}{q} \;\le\; H(T_q) \;\le\; \frac{\log_2 q + 1/\ln 2}{q}.$$

The gap between the two ends is $1/(q\ln 2)$, which shrinks; in fact $q\,H(T_q) - \log_2 q = (q-1)\log_2\!\big(q/(q-1)\big)$ increases to $1/\ln 2 = 1.4427\ldots$, so the upper bound is asymptotically exact and the lower bound is never attained. The ladder is a strictly decreasing sequence of strictly positive numbers tending to zero like $\log_2 q / q$.

---

## What the picture means

There is a moral here about how much arithmetic a residue can carry.

At degree $2$ — the quadratic case, Fermat's world — the residue class of $p$ and the splitting behaviour are informationally *the same thing*: one full bit in, one full bit out. As you climb the ladder, the channel stays perfect — every bit of the type survives the passage through the residue class, at every degree, forever, as the Universal Abelian Pinning Theorem guarantees. But the thing being transmitted gets quieter. At degree $11$ the type is worth $0.4395$ bits; at degree $101$ it will be worth about $0.066$; asymptotically it fades like $\log_2 q / q$.

So the abelian world is one of *perfect transmission of a vanishing signal*. Splitting becomes rarer and rarer — density $1/q$ — and rarity, in Shannon's accounting, is cheap. The channel never fails; the message dwindles.

Which is exactly why the frontier now lies outside the abelian world. Pinning holds because the Frobenius element of an abelian extension is a single group element determined by a residue. Break the commutativity — pass to a non-abelian Galois group such as the symmetry group of the cube roots of $2$, where the Frobenius is only a *conjugacy class* — and the argument's one hypothesis is gone. There, for the first time, a genuine information defect can open: a gap between what the class knows and what the splitting type is. Finding and measuring that gap is where the story goes next.

Meanwhile, the abelian ladder is closed: every rung pinned, every entropy computed, the decay proved once and for all, and even the one bad prediction cleanly corrected. Degree $11$ is not another data point. It is the last one that was ever needed.

# The Shape of Nothing

## How to prove that a string of events carries no memory — and why the "obvious" test is wrong

Imagine you are running a scan. Some machine — a detector, a sieve, a search
procedure — sweeps along an axis, and at each step it either registers a *hit*
or it does not. You end up with a long ribbon of zeros and ones:

$$0\;0\;1\;0\;1\;1\;0\;0\;1\;0\;0\;1\;\dots$$

Now you notice something. Around one particular place along the axis — call the
coordinate $u$, and say the interesting spot is near $u \approx 0.65$ — there
are noticeably more ones than you expected. An **excess**. The excess is real:
it survives every check you throw at it.

The question that matters is not *whether* the excess is there. It is **what
kind of thing** it is. And there are exactly two candidate stories.

**Story one: density.** The machine is simply more likely to fire near
$u \approx 0.65$. Each step is an independent coin flip, but the coin's bias
changes as you move along the axis. The bumps are in the *rate*, not in the
*sequence*. Knowing that step $i$ was a hit tells you nothing whatsoever about
step $i+1$ beyond what the position already told you.

**Story two: structure.** The hits talk to each other. A hit makes the next hit
more likely (clustering, excitation), or less likely (a refractory period, a
dead time). Here the ribbon has *memory*, and the excess is a consequence of
that memory.

These two stories look identical if you only count hits. They differ in how the
hits are *arranged*. The natural instrument for telling arrangements apart is
the **lag profile**: for each separation $k = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, measure how well
the value at position $i$ predicts the value at position $i+k$. Under "pure
density" you expect a flat profile of zeros. Under "structure" you expect a
bump at small lags.

So you measure. You get a profile that is flat — and sitting at about $-0.01$,
at every single lag from $1$ to $20$.

Flat, so no structure. Except: why $-0.01$ and not $0$? And how flat is flat
enough? And is the test even *capable* of seeing structure, or is it just
blind?

This article is about the mathematics that answers those questions — not for
one data set, but as theorems. It turns out that almost every intuition in the
previous paragraph needs repair, and the repairs are beautiful.

---

## Repair 1: zero is the wrong null

Here is the first surprise. Take **any** ribbon of numbers $x_0, \dots, x_{n-1}$
whatsoever — random, deterministic, adversarial, it makes no difference — and
compute its lag profile in the standard way: subtract the average value $\bar x$
from every entry, then correlate the residuals at each lag, treating the ribbon
as a circle so that the ends wrap around. Write $\rho(k)$ for the correlation at
lag $k$.

Then, provided the ribbon is not constant,

$$\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\rho(k) \;=\; -\frac{1}{n-1}.$$

The average of the whole lag profile is **not zero**. It is exactly
$-1/(n-1)$, always, with no probability entering the argument at all.

The reason is a one-line identity. Let $r_i = x_i - \bar x$ be the residuals,
which sum to zero by construction. Summing the cyclic autocovariance
$C(k)=\sum_i r_i r_{i+k}$ over *all* lags including $k=0$ gives
$\sum_k \sum_i r_i r_{i+k} = \left(\sum_i r_i\right)^2 = 0$. So the nonzero
lags must carry exactly $-C(0)$ between them, and $C(0)$ is precisely the
normaliser. Divide by $n-1$ and you are done.

This is a conservation law. Centring a record at its own mean spends one degree
of freedom, and the profile pays for it — uniformly, across all lags. An
immediate corollary: if a measured profile is *flat* to within $\varepsilon$,
its common level cannot be anything other than $-1/(n-1)$, to within
$\varepsilon$. On a window of $n = 101$ positions, that level is $-0.0099\dots$

A uniform offset of about $-0.01$, appearing at every lag, is therefore not a
faint signal of inhibition. **It is arithmetic.** It would appear if the ribbon
were generated by a perfectly memoryless coin; it would appear if the ribbon
were written down by hand. The observation "twelve of my twenty confidence
intervals exclude zero, all on the negative side" — which sounds alarming — is
in fact exactly what the identity predicts, and it carries no information about
dependence at all.

## Repair 2: the flattest possible record is completely deterministic

You might hope that flatness itself is informative — that only a "random-
looking" ribbon could have an equal correlation at every lag. It cannot.

For a $0/1$ ribbon, the mean-centred cyclic autocovariance has a clean
combinatorial form. Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}_n$ be the set of positions where
the ribbon is $1$, and let

$$d_S(k) = \#\{a \in S : a + k \in S\}$$

count how many pairs of hits are separated by exactly $k$ (cyclically). Then

$$C(k) \;=\; d_S(k) \;-\; \frac{|S|^2}{n}.$$

The entire lag profile is nothing but the **difference multiplicity function**
of the hit set, shifted by a constant. So the profile is perfectly flat over all
nonzero lags precisely when every nonzero difference occurs equally often — that
is, precisely when $S$ is a **cyclic difference set**, one of the most rigid,
most highly structured objects in combinatorial design theory. And in that case
the common level is forced, by Repair 1, to be exactly $-1/(n-1)$.

The smallest example is the Fano plane's difference set $S = \{0,1,3\}$ inside
$\mathbb{Z}_7$. Every nonzero difference $1,2,\dots,6$ arises exactly once, so
its lag profile is dead flat at $-1/6$ — and the ribbon $1101000$ is as
deterministic as a ribbon can be.

The moral is sharp: **a maximally flat, slightly negative profile is not the
fingerprint of randomness.** It is the fingerprint of the centring operator, and
the most exquisitely designed record in existence wears it too. Flatness at the
artefact level is evidence of nothing. Only *deviations* from the level
$-1/(n-1)$ can be evidence — and among deviations, only certain shapes.

## Repair 3: what "pure density" actually predicts

So what *is* the honest null? Model the ribbon as independent coins with a
position-dependent bias: $X_i$ is $1$ with probability $p_i$, independently
across $i$, where the rate curve $p$ is completely arbitrary. This is "pure
density" in its full generality.

The correct statistic subtracts the *true rate curve*, not the global mean:

$$A_k \;=\; \sum_{i<m}\bigl(X_i - p_i\bigr)\bigl(X_{i+k} - p_{i+k}\bigr).$$

Then, exactly and for every lag $k \geq 1$ and every rate curve whatsoever,

$$\mathbb{E}[A_k] = 0 .$$

This is the multilinearity of independence: the expectation of a product of
centred indicators at *distinct* positions is zero. Independence, correctly
stated, is not "the correlation is zero" — it is this multilinear identity, and
the identity is what makes detrending at the truth exactly null.

But experimenters don't know the true curve; they detrend at an estimate, or
they use the literal global mean. Does the leftover *curvature* of the rate
curve fake a correlation? Quantitatively: if the curve stays within $\delta$ of
a constant $c$, and the per-position variance is at least $v$, then the
normalised literal reading obeys

$$\bigl|\hat\rho_k\bigr| \;\le\; \frac{\delta^2}{v}\qquad\text{uniformly in }k.$$

Curvature enters only at **second order**. Concretely, a rate curve wandering
$\pm 0.05$ around $1/2$ has $\delta^2/v = (1/400)/(99/400) = 1/99 \approx
0.0101$. So the worst curvature confound the honest window allows is about one
percent of correlation — five times below a $0.05$ decision bar. The same
second-order phenomenon governs the *runs* statistic: the expected number of
alternations $X_i \ne X_{i+1}$ deviates from its homogeneous value by at most
$m(2\delta|1-2c| + 2\delta^2)$, which at $c = 1/2$ has no first-order term at
all.

That is the first half of a separation theorem.

## Repair 4: what "structure" actually predicts, and why the test is not blind

The simplest genuine sequence structure is a two-state Markov chain: from a
non-hit, hit next with probability $a$; from a hit, drop out with probability
$b$. Write $\lambda = 1 - a - b$ for the chain's *persistence* and
$\pi = a/(a+b)$ for its stationary hit rate. Then the lag-$k$ autocovariance is
exactly

$$\mathrm{Cov}(X_i, X_{i+k}) = \pi(1-\pi)\,\lambda^k,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad \rho(k) = \lambda^k .$$

A perfect geometric decay. Three things follow immediately. The profile always
**peaks at lag 1** — whatever the chain, no later lag can beat the first.
It vanishes at *every* lag if and only if $\lambda = 0$, i.e. if and only if
the chain is the independent one: there is no such thing as a Markov chain with
memory that hides from the lag profile. And the $k$-step law is exactly the
$k$-th matrix power of $\begin{pmatrix}1-a & a\\ b & 1-b\end{pmatrix}$, which is
why the decay is geometric.

Put the two halves together and you get the **density/dependence dichotomy**. On
a window where the rate curve lies in $[0.45, 0.55]$:

- a heterogeneous-but-independent scan produces a reading strictly below
  $0.05$, no matter how the rate wiggles;
- a Markov scan with persistence $|\lambda| \ge 0.05$ produces a lag-1
  correlation of at least $0.05$.

The two regimes are cleanly separated by the decision bar. **This is what turns
a null result into an exclusion.** Observing a flat profile does not merely fail
to find structure; it rules out every Markov structure above the bar. And it is
not an artefact of a weak instrument: injecting a lag-1 dependence into the same
pipeline produced a detected correlation of $0.337$ with the maximum at exactly
lag $1$, precisely the geometric shape the theory demands.

There is one more guarantee needed: that the statistic is quiet enough for a
null to mean something. Under pure density, the detrended terms are *pairwise
uncorrelated even when two of them share a position*, so the variance of $A_k$
is exactly

$$\mathrm{Var}(A_k) = \sum_{i<m} p_i(1-p_i)\,p_{i+k}(1-p_{i+k}) \;\le\; \frac{m}{16}.$$

Chebyshev then gives a **noise floor**: the normalised statistic exceeds a level
$t$ with probability at most $1/(16mt^2)$. With $m = 9594$ hits and $t = 0.05$,
that is below $0.003$. A null at this sample size is a genuine exclusion, not a
shrug.

## Repair 5: a third mechanism, and the lesson about amplitude

Density and Markov memory are not the only ways to make a ribbon. Here is a
third: a **coincidence** rule. A latent independent scan $X$ runs underneath,
and you record a hit only when two neighbouring latent cells both fire,

$$Y_i = X_i \, X_{i+1}.$$

The $Y$'s are certainly not independent — consecutive ones share a latent cell —
yet their memory has range exactly one. For an arbitrary latent rate curve the
covariances are

$$\mathrm{Cov}(Y_i, Y_{i+1}) = p_i\,p_{i+1}\,p_{i+2}\,(1 - p_{i+1}),
\qquad \mathrm{Cov}(Y_i, Y_{i+k}) = 0 \ \ \text{for } k \ge 2,$$

**exactly zero at every lag beyond the first, whatever the rate curve.**
Heterogeneity cannot leak into the far lags. So the coincidence profile is a
single spike followed by an infinite row of exact zeros — geometrically
impossible for a Markov chain, whose profile $\lambda^k$ can only vanish at lag
$2$ if it already vanished at lag $1$.

That gives a **trichotomy of shapes**, readable off the first two lags alone:

| mechanism | lag 1 | lag 2 | shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| pure density | $0$ | $0$ | flat |
| Markov memory | $\lambda$ | $\lambda^2$ | geometric |
| coincidence | $>0$ | $0$ | one spike |

Now, how tall can the coincidence spike be? At a constant latent rate $q$ the
answer is a famous-looking little formula: the spike height is $q/(1+q)$, always
**below $1/2$**. It is tempting to conclude that a tall spike rules the
coincidence mechanism out.

It does not — and the reason is the prettiest computation in this whole story.
Write $a = p_i$, $b = p_{i+1}$, $c = p_{i+2}$ for the three latent rates
involved. Dividing the covariance $abc(1-b)$ by the variance
$ab - (ab)^2 = ab(1-ab)$, the factor $ab$ **cancels completely**:

$$\rho(1) = \frac{c\,(1-b)}{1 - ab}.$$

The spike height does not depend on the scan's own hit rate at all — only on
the three latent rates. And this rational function is increasing in $a$ and $c$
and decreasing in $b$, so if the rates are confined to a window $[l, u]$ the
maximum is

$$\rho_{\max} = \frac{u(1-l)}{1 - ul},$$

**attained exactly** by the alternating curve $u, l, u, l, \dots$ — high, low,
high. With rates ranging over $[0.1, 0.9]$ this equals
$0.81/0.91 = 81/91 \approx 0.890$, comfortably above the homogeneous cap of
$1/2$. And letting the window widen, taking $l = t$ and $u = 1-t$ with
$t \to 0$, the spike approaches $1$: the **supremum over all latent rate curves
with values strictly between $0$ and $1$ is exactly $1$**, though no curve
attains it.

So the cap $q/(1+q) < 1/2$ was never a fact about coincidence scans. It was an
artefact of assuming homogeneity — the very assumption the whole investigation
was trying to avoid. Heterogeneity, alternating high and low, breaks it.

The consequence is a genuine methodological law:

> **Amplitude carries no mechanism information. Shape does.**

A one-spike profile of *any* height in $(0,1)$ is compatible with a coincidence
rule, so no amplitude threshold can ever exclude that mechanism. But the exact
zeros from lag $2$ onward are inescapable, whatever the latent curve. A measured
profile that is flat and slightly negative therefore rules the coincidence
mechanism out **at every amplitude simultaneously** — which is far stronger than
ruling it out at small ones.

## What the data then says

With all of this in place, the experimental record reads cleanly. Across $128$
batches and $9{,}594$ hits in the mid-window, the detrended lag profile over
lags $1$ through $20$ sat in $[-0.0199, -0.0023]$: flat, no refractory dip, no
excitation bump, maximum magnitude $0.020$ against a pre-registered bar of
$0.05$. The literal global-mean reading was even quieter,
$[-0.0103, +0.0046]$ — and the fact that the *conditioned* and *literal*
readings agree is exactly what the second-order curvature bound predicts, so the
verdict does not depend on which reading you privilege. The runs statistic gave
$Z = +0.85$ and $+0.89$ against a $3.29$ threshold. The uniform $-0.01$ offset
is the arithmetic identity. Injected dependence was caught at $\rho = 0.337$
with the argmax at exactly lag $1$.

The verdict: **given position, neighbouring hits carry no information about each
other.** The excess near $u \approx 0.65$ is pure density — rate heterogeneity
along the scan axis — and nothing else.

That is a closing statement, and it has an operational edge. If you want to
predict this system better, model the **density curve** $p(u)$ itself. Do not
build a Markov model, do not build a neighbourhood or clustering model, do not
look for refractory periods: the theorems above say there is nothing there to
find, at any amplitude, in any of the three mechanisms that could plausibly have
produced it.

And the mathematics generalises far beyond the scan that provoked it. Any time
someone reports a small negative autocorrelation across many lags — in neural
spike trains, in high-frequency price series, in genomic hit maps, in queueing
logs — the first question should be whether they have discovered inhibition or
rediscovered $-1/(n-1)$. The second should be whether their profile's *shape*,
not its height, distinguishes the mechanisms they care about. Those two
questions have exact answers, and now they have proofs.

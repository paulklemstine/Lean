# Counting the Worlds Between Two Fields

*How a single arithmetic recursion, valid for every base $q \ge 2$, tells you exactly how many intermediate fields sit inside a Hilbert class field — and why the answer is not what the class number alone predicts.*

---

## A staircase with an unknown number of steps

Number theory has a favourite kind of question: *how many?* How many primes below a million, how many solutions to an equation, how many ways to write an integer as a sum of squares. The question in this article is of that kind, but it is asked about a rather grand object.

To every number field $K$ — a finite extension of the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$, for instance $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-21})$ — one attaches a finite abelian group $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$, the **ideal class group**. It measures the failure of unique factorisation in the ring of integers of $K$: the group is trivial exactly when every ideal is principal, and the bigger it is, the more badly unique factorisation fails. Its size $h_K = |\mathrm{Cl}(K)|$ is the **class number**.

Class field theory then performs a small miracle. It produces a canonical field $H$, the **Hilbert class field** of $K$: the largest unramified abelian extension of $K$. And it identifies its Galois group with the class group,
$$\mathrm{Gal}(H/K) \;\cong\; \mathrm{Cl}(K).$$
A statement about factorisation in $K$ has become a statement about the symmetries of a field extension.

Now Galois theory takes over. The intermediate fields $K \subseteq F \subseteq H$ correspond, bijectively and inclusion-reversingly, to the subgroups of $\mathrm{Gal}(H/K)$. So the tower of fields between $K$ and $H$ — the whole staircase of unramified abelian extensions of $K$ — is a picture of the subgroup lattice of the class group. And the counting question becomes purely algebraic:

> **How many subgroups does a finite abelian group have?**

This article is about the answer, and about the surprisingly rich combinatorics that answers it.

---

## The class number is not enough

Here is the first thing to get straight, because it is the thing most easily got wrong. One might hope that the class number $h_K$ determines the number of intermediate fields. It does not.

Take $h_K = 4$. There are two abelian groups of order $4$: the cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/4$ and the Klein four group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$. Count their subgroups.

- $\mathbb{Z}/4$ has subgroups of order $1$, $2$ and $4$, one of each: **three** subgroups.
- $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ has the trivial subgroup, the whole group, and *three* different subgroups of order two (generated by $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,1)$): **five** subgroups.

Two fields of class number four, and one has a staircase with three landings, the other with five. The degrees over $K$ of those five fields, in the Klein case, are $1, 2, 2, 2, 4$: the base field, three distinct quadratic extensions, and the Hilbert class field itself.

The contrast persists and grows. At class number $12$ there are again two abelian types, $\mathbb{Z}/12$ and $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3$. The first has $d(12) = 6$ subgroups, the second has $10$. At class number $p^2$ for a prime $p$, the cyclic group has $3$ subgroups and the elementary abelian one has $p+3$ — a gap that grows without bound.

So the counting question has a genuinely group-theoretic answer, and to get it we must understand the subgroup lattice of an arbitrary finite abelian group. Two structural results do most of the work.

---

## Structural fact one: coprime parts do not talk to each other

Suppose a finite group splits as a direct product $G \times H$ where $|G|$ and $|H|$ are coprime. Then **every** subgroup $K \le G \times H$ is itself a product: $K = A \times B$ with $A \le G$, $B \le H$.

The reason is a small piece of order arithmetic that deserves to be spelled out, because it is the engine of the whole result. Say $(a,b) \in K$, and set $m = |H|$. Raising to the $m$-th power kills the second coordinate: $(a,b)^m = (a^m, 1) \in K$. Now the element $x = (a,1)$ has order dividing $|G|$, which is coprime to $m$; and if $x^m$ lies in a subgroup and $\gcd(\mathrm{ord}(x), m) = 1$, then the cyclic groups generated by $x$ and by $x^m$ coincide, so $x$ itself lies in the subgroup. Hence $(a,1) \in K$, and symmetrically $(1,b) \in K$; multiplying, $K$ contains everything the two projections say it should.

The consequence is a clean multiplicativity — and, pleasantly, it needs no commutativity at all:
$$\#\,\mathrm{Sub}(G \times H) \;=\; \#\,\mathrm{Sub}(G)\cdot \#\,\mathrm{Sub}(H) \qquad \text{whenever } \gcd(|G|,|H|)=1,$$
and more: the entire subgroup *lattice* of $G \times H$ is the product of the two lattices, as ordered sets. Iterating over a family of pairwise coprime factors, the count over any finite product is the product of the counts. Since every finite abelian group is the direct product of its primary components, subgroup counting reduces at once to the case of $p$-groups.

## Structural fact two: cyclic groups are boring, in a useful way

A finite cyclic group of order $n$ has exactly one subgroup of each order dividing $n$, and no others. The uniqueness is the interesting half: a subgroup $L$ of order $d$ consists of elements killed by $d$, and the set of *all* such elements — the kernel of $x \mapsto x^d$ — already has exactly $d$ elements in a cyclic group, so $L$ must be that kernel. The number of subgroups is therefore
$$\#\,\mathrm{Sub}(\mathbb{Z}/n) \;=\; d(n),$$
the divisor-counting function. For $n = p^r$ this is $r+1$: a single flagpole of nested subgroups.

At the other extreme sits the **elementary abelian** group $(\mathbb{Z}/p)^r$, which is nothing but an $r$-dimensional vector space over the field with $p$ elements. Its subgroups are its linear subspaces. And now the combinatorics becomes beautiful.

## Gaussian binomials: counting subspaces

Over a finite field with $q$ elements, the number of $k$-dimensional subspaces of an $n$-dimensional space is the **Gaussian binomial coefficient**
$$\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} \;=\; \frac{(q^n-1)(q^n-q)\cdots(q^n-q^{k-1})}{(q^k-1)(q^k-q)\cdots(q^k-q^{k-1})} .$$
The numerator counts the ways to choose an ordered linearly independent $k$-tuple in the big space (each new vector must avoid the span of its predecessors); the denominator counts, in the same way, the bases of a fixed $k$-dimensional subspace. Divide, and you have counted subspaces.

That derivation is geometric, and it has a cost: it only makes sense when $q$ is the size of an actual field, i.e. a prime power. But the *formula* is a ratio of two integers, defined for any integer base $q$, and one may ask whether the ratio is still a whole number and still obeys the classical identities when $q = 6$, say — a base for which no field exists.

It does. The central result behind this article is an arithmetic proof, valid for **every** integer base $q \ge 2$ with no geometry anywhere, of the following package.

**The exactness theorem.** For every $q \ge 2$ and all $n, k$, the ratio above is an integer, and it agrees with the function $\binom{n}{k}_q$ defined by the recursion below.

**The $q$-Pascal recursion.** For every $q \ge 2$,
$$\binom{n+1}{k+1}_{\!q} \;=\; \binom{n}{k}_{\!q} \;+\; q^{\,k+1}\binom{n}{k+1}_{\!q}.$$
Set $q=1$ and it is Pascal's rule. The extra factor $q^{k+1}$ is the price of linearity: subspaces of the bigger space either contain a chosen new direction or lie in a hyperplane, and the second family is counted with a $q$-weight.

**Symmetry.** For $k \le n$ and every $q \ge 2$,
$$\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} = \binom{n}{n-k}_{\!q}.$$
Over a field this is the statement that a subspace has the same "amount of freedom" as its annihilator; here it is a theorem of pure arithmetic that holds equally at $q=6$, where the geometric argument has nothing to say. As a sanity check, $\binom{5}{2}_6 = \binom{5}{3}_6$.

**A closed form in the first column.** $\binom{n}{1}_q = 1 + q + q^2 + \cdots + q^{n-1}$ — the number of lines in an $n$-dimensional space, when $q$ is a prime power.

The route to all of this is the **$q$-factorial**
$$[m]_q! \;=\; \prod_{j=0}^{m-1}\bigl(q^{\,j+1}-1\bigr) \;=\; (q-1)(q^2-1)\cdots(q^m-1),$$
and the identity that ties everything together: for $k \le n$,
$$[k]_q!\;[n-k]_q!\;\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} \;=\; [n]_q! .$$
This is proved by induction on $n$ straight from the recursion, and the whole induction step rests on one line of algebra,
$$\bigl(q^{k+1}-1\bigr) \;+\; q^{k+1}\bigl(q^{\,n-k}-1\bigr) \;=\; q^{\,n+1}-1 ,$$
which is exactly what is needed to merge the two terms of $q$-Pascal into a single $q$-factorial. Symmetry is then immediate — the left-hand side is unchanged when $k$ is replaced by $n-k$ — and so is positivity, since the $q$-factorials are positive whenever $q \ge 2$. Exactness of the original quotient follows by factoring $q^{0+1+\cdots+(k-1)}$ out of numerator and denominator; what remains on each side is a $q$-factorial, and they cancel.

## The Galois numbers

Sum the Gaussian binomials across a row and you get the total number of subspaces of an $n$-dimensional space over $q$ elements — equivalently, the number of subgroups of $(\mathbb{Z}/p)^n$ when $q = p$:
$$G_q(n) \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k}_{\!q}.$$
These are the **Galois numbers**. For $q = 2$ they run
$$1,\; 2,\; 5,\; 16,\; 67,\; 374,\; \dots$$
and for $q = 3$,
$$1,\; 2,\; 6,\; 28,\; 212, \dots$$
There is no product formula for them, but there is a clean three-term recursion:
$$G_q(n+2) \;=\; 2\,G_q(n+1) \;+\; \bigl(q^{\,n+1}-1\bigr)\,G_q(n).$$
The derivation is a small gem. Introduce the $q$-weighted row sum $S_q(n) = \sum_k q^k \binom{n}{k}_q$. Summing the $q$-Pascal recursion across a row gives $G_q(n+1) = G_q(n) + S_q(n)$. Summing the *dual* recursion — the mirror image
$$\binom{n+1}{k+1}_{\!q} = q^{\,n-k}\binom{n}{k}_{\!q} + \binom{n}{k+1}_{\!q},$$
obtained from $q$-Pascal by applying symmetry twice — after multiplying by $q^{k+1}$ gives $S_q(n+1) = q^{\,n+1}G_q(n) + S_q(n)$. Eliminate $S_q$ between the two and the three-term recursion drops out. From $G_3(2)=6$ and $G_3(3)=28$ it yields $G_3(4) = 2\cdot 28 + (27-1)\cdot 6 = 56 + 156 = 212$, without evaluating a single binomial.

Two easy corners of the table are worth recording. First, $G_q(2) = q+3$: in a plane there are the zero subspace, the whole plane, and $q+1$ lines. That is exactly the group-theoretic count of subgroups of an elementary abelian group of order $p^2$ — a group of order $p^2$ and exponent $p$ has $p+1$ subgroups of order $p$, because its $p^2-1$ non-identity elements are partitioned into lines with $p-1$ non-identity elements each, and $(p^2-1)/(p-1) = p+1$. Two proofs, one from geometry over a finite field, one from counting elements, agreeing. Second, $G_4(3) = 1 + 21 + 21 + 1 = 44$ — a value at the non-prime base $q=4$, where the subspace interpretation over $\mathbb{Z}/4$ is unavailable and only the arithmetic theorem applies. (Over the *field* of four elements it is again a subspace count; the point is that the proof no longer needs to know that such a field exists.)

---

## Back to the staircase

Assemble the pieces. Let $K$ be a number field whose Hilbert class field is $H$, with $\mathrm{Gal}(H/K)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(K)$.

- If $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$ is **cyclic of order $n$**, there are exactly $d(n)$ intermediate fields, one of each degree dividing $n$.
- If $\mathrm{Cl}(K)$ is **elementary abelian of rank $r$ and exponent $p$**, there are exactly $G_p(r)$ intermediate fields, and exactly $\binom{r}{k}_p$ of them have degree $p^k$ over $K$ — the symmetry of the Gaussian binomial being precisely what converts "index of a subgroup" into "degree of the fixed field".
- If $\mathrm{Cl}(K) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p)^r \times (\mathbb{Z}/q)^s$ with $p \ne q$ prime, the two primary parts are coprime and the counts multiply: exactly $G_p(r)\,G_q(s)$ intermediate fields.

Concretely: a Klein four class group ($p = r = 2$) gives $G_2(2) = 5$ intermediate fields with degree multiset $\{1,2,2,2,4\}$; a class group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^3$ gives $G_2(3) = 16$ intermediate fields, distributed as $1, 7, 7, 1$ in degrees $1, 2, 4, 8$ — the $7$s being $\binom{3}{1}_2 = \binom{3}{2}_2 = 1+2+4$; and $\mathrm{Cl}(K) \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2\times\mathbb{Z}/3$ at class number twelve gives $G_2(2)\cdot G_3(1) = 5\cdot 2 = 10$, against $6$ for the cyclic group of the same order.

That last pair is the sharpest form of the message. The class number counts; the class *group* explains. Two fields can have the same class number and different towers above them, and the difference is legible in a table of Gaussian binomial coefficients.

---

## Why the arithmetic route matters

It would have been possible to stop at the geometric proof: subspaces over $\mathbb{F}_q$, and be done. Two things are gained by insisting on the arithmetic one.

The first is **range**. The subspace argument speaks only about prime powers realised by fields. The recursion, the symmetry, the positivity and the exactness of the defining quotient hold for every integer $q \ge 2$. That matters because Gaussian binomials appear far outside finite geometry — in the theory of partitions, where $\binom{n}{k}_q$ generates partitions in a $k \times (n-k)$ box; in quantum groups, where $q$ is a formal deformation parameter with no field attached; in the combinatorics of $q$-series. In those settings the "base" is not the size of anything.

The second is **method**. The proof is an exercise in refusing to leave the integers. The naive obstacle is that $\binom{n}{k}_q$ is defined as a quotient, and quotients of integers are not integers until proved to be. The trick — define a second function by the recursion, prove the factorial identity for *that*, then show the quotient equals it — turns a divisibility statement into an induction. It is the same manoeuvre that shows ordinary binomial coefficients are integers by way of Pascal's triangle rather than by cancelling factorials, and it scales.

There is a final aesthetic pleasure here. The three-term recursion for the Galois numbers, $G_q(n+2) = 2G_q(n+1) + (q^{n+1}-1)G_q(n)$, is the kind of formula that looks like it should be counting something obvious. It is: at $q=1$ the coefficient $q^{n+1}-1$ vanishes and the relation collapses to $2^{n+2} = 2\cdot 2^{n+1}$, the count of subsets of an $n$-set; for $q=2$ it generates $1, 2, 5, 16, 67, 374$, the sequence that appears whenever one counts subgroups of elementary abelian $2$-groups. Behind each of those numbers is a tower of fields, sitting above some number field with the right class group, waiting to be built.

### The results, in one place

For every integer $q \ge 2$: the quotient $\prod_{i<k}(q^n-q^i)\big/\prod_{i<k}(q^k-q^i)$ is an integer $\binom{n}{k}_q$; it satisfies $q$-Pascal, its dual, the factorial identity $[k]_q![n-k]_q!\binom{n}{k}_q = [n]_q!$, symmetry $\binom{n}{k}_q = \binom{n}{n-k}_q$ for $k \le n$, positivity in that range, and $\binom{n}{1}_q = 1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}$. The Galois numbers $G_q(n) = \sum_k \binom{n}{k}_q$ satisfy $G_q(n+2) = 2G_q(n+1)+(q^{n+1}-1)G_q(n)$, with $G_q(0)=1$, $G_q(1)=2$, $G_q(2)=q+3$.

For finite groups: subgroup counts multiply over coprime direct factors, and the subgroup lattice itself splits; a cyclic group of order $n$ has $d(n)$ subgroups, and $r+1$ if the order is $p^r$; a group of order $p^2$ and exponent $p$ has $p+1$ subgroups of order $p$ and $p+3$ in total; hence $3 \ne 5$ at order four and $6 \ne 10$ at order twelve.

For number fields: an unramified abelian extension $H/K$ with group isomorphic to the class group has as many intermediate fields as the class group has subgroups — $d(h_K)$ in the cyclic case, $G_p(r)$ in the elementary abelian case with $\binom{r}{k}_p$ of degree $p^k$, and a product of Galois numbers in the mixed case.

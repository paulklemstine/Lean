# One Table to Hold Them All: How Much a Prime Can Tell You About a Polynomial

*Every prime number carries a tiny message about every polynomial. For the cyclic and dihedral symmetry groups of degrees three to six, we can now write down exactly how many bits that message holds, all in one table, and each entry turns out to be a special case of a law that works in every degree.*

---

## A game of reductions

Pick a polynomial with integer coefficients, say $x^3 - 2$. Now pick a prime $p$ and read the coefficients modulo $p$. Over the integers mod $p$ the polynomial may split into linear factors, stay irreducible, or do something in between. For $x^3-2$:

- modulo $p = 31$ it has three roots (it splits completely);
- modulo $p = 5$ it has exactly one root ($3^3 = 27 \equiv 2$);
- modulo $p = 7$ it has no root at all.

The pattern of how a polynomial breaks apart modulo $p$ is called its **splitting type** at $p$. Number theorists have studied these patterns since Gauss, and they are the starting point of some of the deepest mathematics we have, from quadratic reciprocity to the Langlands programme.

We ask a simple question in the language of information theory:

> *If a prime is chosen "at random", how much information, in bits, does its splitting type carry? And how much of that information survives if we only look at a coarser summary, such as the number of roots?*

The answers are exact numbers built from $\log_2 3$ and $\log_2 5$. This article is about the table that holds them, and about why that table is complete.

## Frobenius: the prime's fingerprint

The key is a principle from the 1920s. Each polynomial $f$ has a **Galois group** $G$, the group of symmetries of its roots. Each prime $p$ (apart from finitely many) picks out a particular symmetry, or more precisely a conjugacy class of symmetries, called its **Frobenius element**. The splitting type of $f$ modulo $p$ is determined by how that Frobenius element permutes the roots. In particular, *the number of roots of $f$ modulo $p$ equals the number of roots fixed by Frobenius.*

The **Chebotarev density theorem** then says that, over all primes, Frobenius behaves like a *uniformly random element* of $G$. So "a random prime" becomes "a random group element", and our question turns into a finite computation: take a uniform random symmetry and measure the Shannon entropy of what it does to the roots.

Recall that the **Shannon entropy** of a random variable taking values with probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_k$ is
$$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i \quad\text{bits}.$$
A fair coin carries $1$ bit. A die that always lands on six carries $0$.

## Two families of symmetry

The table covers the two most basic families of Galois groups.

**Cyclic groups $C_n$.** These are the symmetries of a clock face with $n$ hours, turned by whole hours. They show up, for example, for the field generated by $\cos(2\pi/7)$ (degree $3$) and in general for the "cyclic fields" of class field theory. Frobenius is a random rotation $a \in \{0, 1, \dots, n-1\}$, and the splitting type is the **order** of that rotation, $T = n/\gcd(a,n)$. It tells you the common degree of the irreducible factors modulo $p$.

**Dihedral groups $D_n$.** These are the symmetries of a regular $n$-gon: $n$ rotations and $n$ reflections. They are the Galois groups of radical polynomials such as $x^n - a$, whose $n$ complex roots really do sit at the vertices of a regular $n$-gon. Here the splitting type we track is the **number of roots modulo $p$**, which is the number of vertices the random symmetry leaves fixed.

## The table

Write $L = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ and $L_5 = \log_2 5 \approx 2.322$. Here is every value in one place.

| degree | cyclic type $H(T)$ | cyclic root count | pair channel | dihedral type $H(T)$ | residue $H(T\mid\text{rot})$ | dial $I(\text{rot};T)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | $L - \tfrac23$ | $L-\tfrac23$ (lossless) | $L - \tfrac{10}{9}$ | $\tfrac23 + \tfrac L2$ | $\tfrac L2 - \tfrac13$ | $1$ |
| 4 | $\tfrac32$ | $2 - \tfrac{3L}{4}$ (lossy) | $\tfrac54$ | $\tfrac{11}{4} - \tfrac{5L_5}{8}$ | $\tfrac32 - \tfrac{3L}{8}$ | $\tfrac54 + \tfrac{3L}{8} - \tfrac{5L_5}{8}$ |
| 5 | $L_5 - \tfrac85$ | $L_5-\tfrac85$ (lossless) | $L_5 + \tfrac{12L}{25} - \tfrac{72}{25}$ | $\tfrac15 + \tfrac{L_5}{2}$ | $\tfrac{L_5}{2} - \tfrac45$ | $1$ |
| 6 | $\tfrac13 + L$ | $1 + L - \tfrac{5L_5}{6}$ (lossy) | $L - \tfrac19$ | $\tfrac{3L}{4}$ | $1 + \tfrac L2 - \tfrac{5L_5}{12}$ | $\tfrac L4 + \tfrac{5L_5}{12} - 1$ |

At first sight it is a zoo of fractions and logarithms. Look closer, though, and patterns appear. The root-count column matches the type column exactly in degrees $3$ and $5$, and falls short in degrees $4$ and $6$. The dial column is exactly $1$ in degrees $3$ and $5$ and something messier in $4$ and $6$. Each of these patterns is one case of a law that holds in *every* degree, and that is what we mean by calling the framework complete.

## The one number behind everything: pinning entropy

Imagine $N$ cups, one of which hides a coin. Someone lifts one cup at random and tells you only "coin" or "no coin". How much have you learned? The answer, $1/N$ of the time "yes" and otherwise "no", is the **pinning entropy**
$$\pi(N) = \log_2 N - \frac{N-1}{N}\log_2(N-1),$$
which is the binary entropy $h(1/N)$ of a coin with bias $1/N$. For example $\pi(2) = 1$ (a fair coin), $\pi(3) = L - \tfrac23$, and $\pi(N)$ shrinks towards $0$ as $N$ grows. A lottery ticket's "win or lose" carries almost no information, because you already knew you would lose.

This simple quantity turns out to organise the entire table.

### Law 1: the cyclic root count *is* pinning

In a cyclic field of degree $n$, a polynomial defining the field has either $n$ roots modulo $p$ (when $p$ splits completely) or none at all. Why? The Galois group acts on the roots like the clock acting on its hours, and a nonzero rotation fixes no hour. So the number of roots only answers the question "is Frobenius the identity?" That is exactly the pinning question with $N = n$ cups:

> **Theorem (Root-count law).** For every $n \ge 1$, the entropy of the number of roots in a cyclic degree-$n$ field equals $\pi(n)$.

### Law 2: primes are exactly where nothing is lost

When does counting roots tell you *everything* about the splitting type? If $n$ is prime, every non-identity rotation has the same order $n$, so the type has only two possible values, $1$ and $n$, and knowing the root count is the same as knowing the type. If $n$ is composite, pick its smallest prime factor $q$. A rotation of order $q$ and a rotation of order $n$ both have zero roots but different types, so information is lost. A careful comparison, using the classical formula
$$H(T) = \log_2 n - \frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\varphi(d)\log_2\varphi(d)$$
(where $\varphi$ is Euler's totient), shows that the loss is strictly positive.

> **Theorem (Prime losslessness).** For $n \ge 2$, the root count carries the full information of the cyclic splitting type if and only if $n$ is prime.

This explains the "(lossless)" and "(lossy)" marks in the table: $3$ and $5$ are prime, $4$ and $6$ are not.

## The dihedral world: a coin, a dial, and a residue

Now take a regular $n$-gon. Every symmetry is either a rotation or a reflection. For $x^n - a$ this distinction has a concrete arithmetic meaning: it is a quadratic character of the prime. For $x^3 - 2$ it records whether $p \equiv 1$ or $2 \pmod 3$. We call this bit the **rotation dial**. Exactly half the symmetries are rotations, so the dial is a fair coin worth exactly $1$ bit.

How many vertices does each symmetry fix?

- The identity fixes all $n$. Every other rotation fixes none.
- If $n$ is **odd**, every reflection passes through exactly one vertex and fixes exactly $1$.
- If $n$ is **even**, half the reflections pass through two opposite vertices (fixing $2$) and half pass through edge midpoints (fixing $0$).

Counting gives the type distributions. Out of $2n$ symmetries, odd $n$ gives $\{0: n-1,\ 1: n,\ n: 1\}$ and even $n$ gives $\{0: \tfrac{3n}{2}-1,\ 2: \tfrac n2,\ n: 1\}$. From these:

> **Theorem (Dihedral type law).** For odd $n \ge 3$ the dihedral splitting type carries $H(T) = 1 + \tfrac12\pi(n)$ bits. For even $n = 2m \ge 4$,
> $$H(T) = \log_2(4m) - \frac{(3m-1)\log_2(3m-1) + m\log_2 m}{4m}.$$

The odd formula reads naturally as a story in two acts. First, one bit tells you whether you are looking at a rotation or a reflection. Then, half the time (rotations), you face the pinning question "identity or not?", worth $\pi(n)$. The other half (reflections), you already know the answer is "one root".

### The bridge

That middle step deserves its own statement, because it links the two families:

> **Theorem (Bridge).** Conditioned on Frobenius being a rotation, the dihedral splitting type has exactly the entropy of the cyclic root-count channel of the same degree, namely $\pi(n)$.

Inside every dihedral field sits a cyclic one. The rotations *are* a copy of $C_n$, and on them "number of fixed vertices" is exactly "number of roots".

### The parity law

How much does the dial tell you about the type? That is the mutual information $I(\text{rot}; T)$, the reduction in uncertainty about $T$ once you know whether Frobenius is a rotation or a reflection.

For **odd** $n$ something neat happens. The dial is a *function* of the type: a symmetry is a reflection exactly when it fixes precisely one vertex (no rotation of an odd polygon fixes exactly one vertex). A general chain rule for entropy says that when one quantity is determined by another, the information they share is just the entropy of the determined one. The dial is a fair coin, so

> **Theorem (Parity law).** For $n \ge 3$, the rotation dial carries exactly one bit of information about the dihedral splitting type **if and only if $n$ is odd**. For even $n$ it carries strictly less.

The even case fails for a concrete reason. The rotation by one step and a reflection through two edge midpoints both fix *zero* vertices, so seeing "zero roots" does not tell you which kind of symmetry you have. The symmetry of mutual information, $I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)$, turns this ambiguity into a strict inequality.

What is left over is the **non-abelian residue** $H(T \mid \text{rot})$: the information in the type that the abelian dial cannot see. For odd $n$ it is $\tfrac12\pi(n)$. For even $n$ it is $\tfrac12\pi(n) + \tfrac12$, the extra half bit coming from the vertex-versus-edge reflection coin.

## Two further patterns in the table

**The binary cap.** The pair channel measures the following. Take two primes $p$ and $q$ with independent Frobenius elements in a cyclic field. How much do their two splitting types tell you about the splitting type of the *product* class, the Frobenius attached to $pq$? In degrees $3$ and $5$ this is less than one bit ($0.474$ and $0.203$). In degrees $4$ and $6$ it is more than one bit ($1.25$ and $1.474$). Odd degree keeps it below a single yes-or-no answer, while even degree breaks through.

**Dihedral beats cyclic counting.** In each degree $3$ to $6$ the dihedral type carries strictly more information than the cyclic root count. The reflections add extra texture that the bare clock cannot produce.

## Going to infinity

The table stops at six, but the laws do not. The pinning entropy satisfies
$$0 < \pi(n) \le \frac{1}{\ln 2}\left(\frac{1}{n-1} + \frac{\ln(n-1)}{n}\right) \qquad (n\ge 2),$$
so $\pi(n) \to 0$. Two consequences follow.

1. The cyclic root count carries less and less information as the degree grows: at large $n$, "has roots or not" is almost always "not".
2. Along odd degrees, the share of the dihedral type information seen by the abelian dial is
$$\frac{I(\text{rot};T)}{H(T)} = \frac{1}{1 + \tfrac12\pi(n)} \longrightarrow 1.$$
The non-abelian part becomes asymptotically invisible. The abelian share is $0.685$ of the total in degree $3$, $0.96$ in degree $101$, and $0.9993$ in degree $10001$. Yet the non-abelian residue **never vanishes** at any finite odd degree. No abelian dial ever quite pins down a dihedral splitting type.

For even degrees the numbers suggest a different limit. The dial seems to decrease towards $h(1/4) - \tfrac12 \approx 0.311$ bits, where $h$ is binary entropy. At degree $400$ it is $0.3120$. This remains an open conjecture.

## Why it matters

Information-theoretic language turns classical splitting laws into quantities you can compare, add and bound. "Counting roots is enough exactly for prime degrees" is a crisp version of a folklore fact about cyclic fields. "The abelian part of a dihedral field sees exactly one bit in odd degree" is a quantitative shadow of the fact that dihedral fields are built from a cyclic field over a quadratic field. The same principles, pinning, the chain rule and fibre-by-fibre counting, apply to any finite group acting on any finite set, so they point towards similar tables for $A_4$, $S_4$, $A_5$, $S_5$ and beyond.

"Complete" also has an honest limit. The degree $3$–$6$ table is finished for the **cyclic and dihedral** rows. Other Galois groups of these degrees are not yet covered. Within its scope, though, nothing is left to measure. Each entry is a closed form, each column is one case of a law valid in every degree, and the earlier degree-by-degree computations are recovered independently from those general laws.

The next time you reduce a polynomial modulo a prime, you can say how many bits the prime just told you.

# The Unramified Mirror: Ideal Classes, Galois Symmetry, and the First Layer of Langlands

Number theory begins with ordinary integers, where factorization behaves with almost unreasonable elegance. Every nonzero integer breaks into primes, uniquely apart from order and sign. Move to the integers of a number field, however, and this familiar picture can fracture: an element may admit genuinely different factorizations. The cure is not to abandon factorization, but to change what is being factored. Ideals still factor uniquely into prime ideals.

That repair leaves behind a precise measure of what went wrong. The **ideal class group** of a number field $K$, written $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)$, records nonzero fractional ideals of its ring of integers $\mathcal O_K$, with two ideals identified when their quotient is generated by a single element. Its identity consists of principal ideals. Thus $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)$ is trivial exactly when every nonzero ideal is principal. Its finite size

$$
h_K=\lvert \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)\rvert
$$

is the **class number**.

The striking insight of class field theory is that this algebraic defect is also a symmetry group. For the Hilbert class field $H$ of $K$, Artin reciprocity identifies

$$
\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\cong \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K).
$$

Here $H/K$ is a finite Galois extension, unramified at every finite prime, and maximal with that property among abelian extensions. In this article the decisive datum is the displayed isomorphism. Once it is available, a chain of consequences follows with remarkable economy: the Galois group must be abelian; its one-dimensional complex representations correspond exactly to characters of the ideal class group; and the extension collapses to degree one exactly when every ideal of $\mathcal O_K$ is principal.

These statements form the unramified, one-dimensional edge of a much larger bridge between arithmetic and symmetry.

## From an obstruction to a field

An ideal class can be pictured as a type of failure of principality. If an ideal $I$ is generated by one element, then its class $[I]$ is the identity. Multiplication of ideals turns all classes into a finite abelian group. Rather than merely counting failures, the Hilbert class field transforms each class into an automorphism of a larger field.

The Artin map does this through prime ideals. An unramified prime ideal determines a Frobenius automorphism, an operation describing how residues are raised to a power modulo that prime. Extending multiplicatively and then passing to ideal classes yields reciprocity. At the Hilbert-class-field level, no information is lost: every ideal class becomes one Galois symmetry, and every Galois symmetry comes from one ideal class.

This immediately gives the first result.

**Abelian Galois Group Theorem.** *Let $K$ be a number field and $H/K$ a finite Galois extension equipped with an Artin reciprocity isomorphism $A:\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)$. Then $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$ is abelian.*

The reason is transparent. For $\sigma,\tau\in\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$,

$$
A(\sigma\tau)=A(\sigma)A(\tau)=A(\tau)A(\sigma)=A(\tau\sigma),
$$

because the ideal class group is abelian. Since $A$ is injective, $\sigma\tau=\tau\sigma$.

The argument illustrates the power of transport: a structural fact obvious on one side of an isomorphism becomes a theorem on the other.

## Listening to arithmetic through characters

A finite abelian group can be studied through its characters. An **ideal-class character** is a homomorphism

$$
\chi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)\longrightarrow \mathbb C^\times,
$$

where $\mathbb C^\times$ is the multiplicative group of nonzero complex numbers. Because the source is finite, every value of $\chi$ is a root of unity. The character converts multiplication of ideal classes into multiplication of complex phases.

On the field side, a **one-dimensional complex Galois representation** is a homomorphism

$$
\rho:\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\longrightarrow \mathbb C^\times.
$$

Artin reciprocity says that these are two descriptions of the same data. Given an ideal-class character $\chi$, define

$$
\rho_\chi(\sigma)=\chi(A(\sigma)).
$$

Conversely, from a Galois character $\rho$, define

$$
\chi_\rho(c)=\rho(A^{-1}(c)).
$$

Composing in either order returns the character with which one started. This yields the central correspondence.

**Unramified $\mathrm{GL}_1$ Correspondence.** *Under an Artin reciprocity isomorphism $A:\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)$, composition with $A$ gives a group isomorphism*

$$
\operatorname{Hom}(\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K),\mathbb C^\times)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}(\operatorname{Gal}(H/K),\mathbb C^\times).
$$

*Its inverse is composition with $A^{-1}$.*

The phrase $\mathrm{GL}_1$ appears because the invertible linear transformations of a one-dimensional complex vector space form $\mathbb C^\times$. In higher dimensions, one seeks representations into $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb C)$ and automorphic objects of much richer kinds. Here, in dimension one and without finite ramification, the bridge is especially clean: an ideal-theoretic phase and a field-symmetry phase are literally the same character viewed through reciprocity.

For a concrete model, suppose the class group is cyclic of order $3$, generated by $g$. Its characters are indexed by $k=0,1,2$ and satisfy

$$
\chi_k(g^j)=\exp(2\pi i k j/3).
$$

If $A(\sigma)=g$, then the corresponding Galois representation sends $\sigma$ to the same cube root of unity. A complete character table on the ideal side becomes, after relabeling columns by $A$, the complete character table on the Galois side.

## When the mirror has no depth

The class number also measures the degree of the Hilbert class field:

$$
[H:K]=h_K.
$$

This numerical equality turns an algebraic criterion into a geometric-looking statement about extension degree.

**Degree-One Criterion.** *Let $H/K$ carry Hilbert class field reciprocity, so that $[H:K]=h_K$ and $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K)$. Then*

$$
[H:K]=1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathcal O_K\text{ is a principal ideal ring}.
$$

*Equivalently, $[H:K]=1$ if and only if every nonzero ideal of $\mathcal O_K$ is principal.*

The proof has only two ingredients. First, $[H:K]=h_K$. Second, $h_K=1$ exactly when the class group is trivial, which is exactly when every ideal class is represented by a principal ideal. Therefore a nontrivial ideal-class obstruction forces a nontrivial unramified extension, while the absence of any obstruction leaves no extension at all.

Both directions are useful. If every nonzero ideal is principal, the class group is trivial, hence $h_K=1$ and $[H:K]=1$. Conversely, if $[H:K]=1$, then $h_K=1$, the class group is trivial, and every nonzero ideal is principal.

Consider two familiar imaginary quadratic fields. For $K=\mathbb Q(i)$, the ring $\mathbb Z[i]$ has class number $1$, so its Hilbert class field is just $K$. By contrast, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-5})$ has class number $2$. Its failure of unique factorization is reflected in a nontrivial ideal class, and the Hilbert class field has degree $2$ over the base. The arithmetic defect has become a twofold Galois symmetry.

## Why this is a Kronecker–Weber generalization

The Kronecker–Weber theorem says that every finite abelian extension of $\mathbb Q$ lies inside a cyclotomic field, a field obtained by adjoining roots of unity. It is an explicit answer to a grand question: how can abelian extensions be generated and understood?

For a general number field $K$, roots of unity alone no longer provide the whole answer. Hilbert's twelfth problem asks for explicit analytic generators of abelian extensions of arbitrary number fields. The Hilbert class field supplies the first canonical unramified layer. Reciprocity identifies its Galois group, but an explicit formula for generators is a deeper demand. For imaginary quadratic fields, complex multiplication and singular moduli provide a celebrated answer. For general fields, the landscape remains far less complete.

The results above should therefore be read as a structural core, not as an existence or explicit-generation theorem. They begin with an Artin reciprocity isomorphism and explain everything it forces: commutativity, duality of characters, and the exact relation between extension degree and principality. Constructing $H$ and producing explicit generators are further tasks.

## The first rung of a larger ladder

The character correspondence is also the $n=1$ shadow of the Langlands philosophy. On one side are Galois representations, encoding symmetries of algebraic equations. On the other are automorphic characters, encoding harmonic patterns on arithmetic quotient spaces. In the unramified finite-order setting, ideal-class characters play the automorphic role.

This viewpoint has computational consequences. If one knows a finite presentation of the class group, then one can enumerate all ideal-class characters using roots of unity. The Artin isomorphism transports them to every one-dimensional Galois representation of the Hilbert class field. No separate search on the Galois side is needed. Likewise, computing the class number predicts the extension degree before an explicit defining polynomial for $H$ is found.

There is also a conceptual lesson. Nonunique factorization, field extensions, and complex phases may look like three unrelated topics. The class group, Artin reciprocity, and character duality show that they are three projections of one object. A failed factorization becomes an ideal class; an ideal class becomes a field automorphism; an automorphism becomes a root-of-unity phase.

A useful analogy comes from signal processing. A finite abelian group is a discrete space of arithmetic positions, and its characters are pure frequencies on that space. The character table is a Fourier transform. Artin reciprocity does not alter the music; it relabels the positions from ideal classes to field automorphisms. Orthogonality of characters then says that distinct arithmetic frequencies can be perfectly separated. This is more than imagery: finite Fourier transforms on class groups occur in the analysis of $L$-functions and in algorithms that sort arithmetic data by character.

The road beyond this first layer is clear. One seeks existence of Hilbert class fields for every number field, principalization of ideals after extension, an idèlic account of the characters, compatibility with cyclotomic reciprocity over $\mathbb Q$, and explicit generators in concrete families. Each step makes reciprocity less merely structural and more explicit.

At the heart of the story is a simple mirror:

$$
\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal O_K).
$$

Look into it from the side of ideals, and one sees the obstruction to principality. Look from the side of fields, and one sees unramified symmetries. Listen through characters, and both sides produce exactly the same spectrum.

# The Beautiful Machine That Refuses to Factor

## A story about $\sqrt{N}$, its endlessly repeating fraction, and why the deepest structure in number theory still won't tell you what $N$ is made of

---

### The oldest algorithm still running

Take a non-square number — say $N = 21$ — and try to write $\sqrt{21}$ as a fraction. You can't; it's irrational. But you can do something almost as good. Since $4 < \sqrt{21} < 5$, write

$$\sqrt{21} = 4 + \frac{1}{x_1}, \qquad x_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{21}-4} = \frac{\sqrt{21}+4}{5} \approx 1.72 .$$

Repeat on $x_1$: peel off its integer part, invert the remainder, keep going. What you get is a *continued fraction*,

$$\sqrt{21} = 4 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{2 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \cfrac{1}{8 + \cdots}}}}}}$$

and here is the first miracle, known since the eighteenth century: the integers $4;\,1,1,2,1,1,8,\,1,1,2,1,1,8,\dots$ *repeat forever*. Six of them, over and over: $\sqrt{21}$ has **period** $\ell = 6$.

Every non-square $N$ has such a period $\ell(N)$, and the sequence of periods is one of the strangest objects in elementary number theory. For $N = 2,3,5,6,7,8,10,11,12,13,\dots$ it reads $1,2,1,2,4,2,1,2,2,5,\dots$. It jumps; it has no formula; it is *sensitive to $N$* as almost nothing else in arithmetic is: $\ell(60) = 2$ while $\ell(61) = 11$.

That sensitivity is exactly why this article exists. If some cheap statistic of the period could see *inside* $N$ — could tell you that $N = 21$ is $3 \times 7$ rather than a prime — we would have a new way to break large numbers into factors, which is to say a new way to break most of the cryptography that protects the internet.

This article is about the discovery that it cannot. Not "we tried and failed": the mechanism by which the period *would* leak factoring information can be located exactly, isolated, and shown to be blocked.

---

### The machine behind the fraction

The continued fraction of $\sqrt{N}$ is generated by a small state machine dating to Lagrange. Every complete quotient along the way has the shape $(\sqrt N + m)/d$ for integers $m, d$, and alongside them one tracks the *convergents* $h/q$ — the best rational approximations obtained by truncating the fraction. So the entire state is six integers: $m$, $d$, and the two most recent pairs $(h_{-1}, h)$, $(q_{-1}, q)$. One step, with partial quotient $a = \lfloor(\lfloor\sqrt{N}\rfloor + m)/d\rfloor$, sends

$$m \mapsto da - m, \qquad d \mapsto \frac{N - (da-m)^2}{d}, \qquad h \mapsto ah + h_{-1}, \qquad q \mapsto aq + q_{-1}.$$

That's it. Six integers, four assignments, no floating point anywhere.

What makes the machine remarkable is that it carries a complete set of conserved quantities — invariants that hold at every step, forever. Write $(m,d,h_{-1},h,q_{-1},q)$ for the state. Then:

> **Invariant Theorem.** *Starting from the initial state $m=0$, $d=1$, $h_{-1}=0$, $h=1$, $q_{-1}=1$, $q=0$, and running any number of steps with any integer partial quotients whatsoever, a non-square $N$ satisfies all of the following at every state:*
> 1. $d \neq 0$ and $d \mid N - m^2$;
> 2. $Nq = hm + h_{-1}d$;
> 3. $qm + q_{-1}d = h$;
> 4. $(h q_{-1} - h_{-1} q)^2 = 1$.

The fourth says a $2\times2$ matrix of consecutive convergents is unimodular — the algebraic shadow of the fact that continued fractions are built out of the group $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. Items 2 and 3 are less familiar and are the real engine. Combine them the right way — formally, take $-h \cdot (3) - q \cdot (2)$ — and everything collapses to a single, startlingly clean identity:

> **Exact Pell Value.** *At every state of the machine,*
> $$h^2 - N q^2 = d \cdot (h q_{-1} - h_{-1} q) = \pm\, d .$$

Read that again: it is the whole subject in one line. The quantity $h^2 - Nq^2$ — the **norm** of $h + q\sqrt{N}$, the thing Pell's equation is about — is always exactly $\pm$ the auxiliary integer $d$ the machine is already carrying. The continued fraction is not approximating Pell's equation; it is solving it, exactly, at every step.

And when does it solve it *perfectly*? When $d = 1$. That is precisely the end of a period.

> **Unit Theorem.** *If a state of the machine has $d = 1$, then $h^2 - Nq^2 = 1$ or $h^2 - Nq^2 = -1$. That is, $h + q\sqrt{N}$ is a unit of the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{N}]$ — a solution of Pell's equation.*

For $N = 21$, six steps land on $d = 1$ with $h = 55$, $q = 12$: indeed $55^2 - 21\cdot 12^2 = 1$. For $N = 13$, five steps give $18 + 5\sqrt{13}$ with $18^2 - 13\cdot 5^2 = -1$. The period is a machine for manufacturing the *fundamental unit* of a real quadratic field — the invariant controlling its regulator and its class number formula. So the period is no numerological curiosity but a genuine, deep arithmetic channel. Which brings us to the question.

---

### Can the channel see a factor?

Here is the one place — and, it turns out, the *only* place — where a Pell unit touches factorization. Suppose the period-end unit has norm $+1$: $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$. Reduce modulo $N$; the term $Ny^2$ vanishes, so

$$x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N .$$

Now $\pm1$ always square to $1$. But if $N$ has two different prime factors $p$ and $q$, the congruence $x^2 \equiv 1$ has *four* solutions mod $N$: by the Chinese Remainder Theorem $x$ can be $+1$ mod $p$ while being $-1$ mod $q$. Such a **split root** is a factoring bomb:

> **Split-Root Theorem.** *Let $n > 1$ and let $x$ be an integer with $n \mid x^2 - 1$ but $n \nmid x - 1$ and $n \nmid x + 1$. Then $g = \gcd(x-1, n)$ satisfies $1 < g < n$ and $g \mid n$: it is a proper factor of $n$.*

The proof is two lines. If $\gcd(x-1,n)$ were $1$, then coprimality plus $n \mid (x-1)(x+1)$ would force $n \mid x+1$, excluded; so $g > 1$. If $g$ were $n$, then $n \mid x-1$, also excluded; so $g < n$.

And it *fires*. For $N = 21$ the period-end unit is $x = 55$, a square root of $1$ mod $21$ that is neither $1$ nor $20$; and $\gcd(54, 21) = 3$. The continued fraction of $\sqrt{21}$ has just factored $21$. Across sweeps of composite $N$ the exit fires roughly three times in four — $206$ of $269$ even-period cases in one experiment, $41$ of $53$ in another. A real mechanism, not a fluke.

So why isn't this a factoring algorithm?

---

### Three walls

**Wall one: the price of admission is $\sqrt{N}$.**

You only get the unit at the *end* of a period, and periods are long. Empirically the median ratio $\ell(N)/\sqrt{N}$ sits around $0.4$; theoretically $\ell(N)$ is governed by the regulator of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt N)$, of size $N^{1/2 + o(1)}$ for typical $N$. So walking the period costs on the order of $2^{(\log_2 N)/2}$ steps, exponential in the number of digits of $N$.

This is not a technicality one might engineer away, and the machine's own structure says so. All the arithmetic coordinates stay small: from the first step onward $0 \le m \le \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ and $0 < d \le 2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$. The state lives in a box; the *only* unbounded coordinate is the step counter. Meanwhile the witness is enormous:

> **Fibonacci Growth Theorem.** *For every non-square $N \ge 1$, the convergent denominators after $k+1$ steps satisfy $q_{k+1} \ge F_{k+1}$, the Fibonacci number.*

Since $F_k \approx \varphi^k$ with $\varphi = (1+\sqrt5)/2$, the period-end witness has about $0.7\ell$ digits, and reaching it means writing it down. There is no shortcut inside the machine: the state is a bounded box and the answer is exponentially large in the number of steps.

For comparison, the classical square-form factorization method reaches a similar goal in about $N^{1/4}$ operations, and the number field sieve does far better. The continued-fraction route is a known method at a *worse* exponent. There is no new leverage here.

**Wall two: prime powers are simply immune.**

Even granting full payment of the $\sqrt N$ cost, the exit does not always exist: a split root requires at least two distinct prime factors.

> **Prime-Power Immunity Theorem.** *Let $p$ be an odd prime and $k \ge 1$. Then modulo $p^k$, the only square roots of $1$ are $\pm 1$. Consequently, for $N = p^k$, every Pell solution $x^2 - Ny^2 = 1$ has $x \equiv \pm 1 \pmod N$, and the continued fraction of $\sqrt{N}$ can never split $N$ — no matter how long its period, no matter how much computation you pour in.*

Why: a prime dividing both $x-1$ and $x+1$ divides their difference $2$, impossible for odd $p$; so all $k$ copies of $p$ go into one factor. Contrapositively, whenever the channel *does* split $N$, $N$ already had two distinct primes: it never discovers multiplicity, it only detects a splitting the Chinese Remainder Theorem was going to hand you anyway.

**Wall three: the cheap cases are exactly the useless cases.**

Some $N$ have tiny periods, and one might hope to farm those. But the short-period families are explicitly known and explicitly barren.

$N = m^2 + 1$ has period $1$ and fundamental unit $m + \sqrt N$, of norm $-1$ — so it isn't even a square root of $1$; and the three integers it exposes are almost coprime to $N$:

> **Cheap-Window Null (period one).** *For every integer $m$, $\gcd(m, m^2+1) = 1$ and both $\gcd(m \pm 1, m^2+1)$ divide $2$. If $m$ is even — the case where $N = m^2+1$ is odd and can be a semiprime — all three gcds equal $1$ exactly. No factor is exposed.*

The identity behind it is charming: $(m^2+1) - (m-1)(m+1) = 2$.

Concretely, $65 = 8^2 + 1 = 5 \times 13$ has period $1$ — instant, free — and unit $8 + \sqrt{65}$, with $\gcd(8,65) = \gcd(7,65) = \gcd(9,65) = 1$. The cheapest possible period on a semiprime tells you exactly nothing.

The next family, $N = m^2+2$, has period $2$ and unit $(m^2+1) + m\sqrt N$ of norm $+1$ — so $x = m^2+1$ *is* a square root of $1$ mod $N$. But it's the trivial one: $x + 1 = N$, i.e. $x \equiv -1$, and $\gcd(x-1, N) = \gcd(m^2, m^2+2) = 1$ for odd $m$. Again: nothing.

This is not bad luck about two families. A cheap unit means a *small* denominator $y$, and small denominators are rare:

> **Sparsity Theorem.** *Fix $y \ge 1$. The number of $N \le X$ for which $Ny^2 + 1$ is a perfect square — i.e. which admit a norm-$+1$ unit with denominator exactly $y$ — is at most $\sqrt{Xy^2+1} + 1 = O(y\sqrt X)$.*

The proof is a counting injection: such an $N$ is determined by $\sqrt{Ny^2+1} \le \sqrt{Xy^2+1}$. So for any fixed budget of denominator size, the admissible $N$ have density zero. The $N = m^2+c$ families are the whole of the cheap window — a measure-zero sliver of the integers.

### The mirage: why the data looked so promising

There is a cautionary tale buried here, because it is a mistake anyone could make.

A first pass at the data looked spectacular. For semiprimes $N = pq$, take the "spread" $s$ measuring how far apart the primes are, and the largest partial quotient in the period of $\sqrt N$. The correlation came out at about $+0.99$, in every bucket. Overwhelming signal.

It was entirely an artifact. Here's the theorem that kills it.

> **Partial-Quotient Cap.** *For every non-square $N \ge 1$, every partial quotient of $\sqrt{N}$ after the initial one satisfies $1 \le a_k \le 2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$; and the bound is attained exactly at the end of a period, where $d = 1$ and $m = \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, giving $a_\ell = 2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$.*

The proof runs through the classical fact that all complete quotients of $\sqrt N$ are *reduced*: from the first step on, $0 < d$, $0 \le m \le \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, $d \le \lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor + m$, and $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor < d + m$. Inside that box the floor $\lfloor(\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor + m)/d\rfloor$ is squeezed between $1$ and $2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$.

So the maximum partial quotient is *never a statistic of the factorization at all*: it is $2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$, a pure function of the size of $N$, on every instance in the sweep ($330$ of $330$). And the size of $N$ correlates with the spread of its factors, because $N = pq$. The $0.99$ was $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ correlating with itself, in disguise.

Once every period statistic is *residualized* on $\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ — once you ask what it knows beyond what the size already tells you — the signal evaporates. Across $120$ partial-correlation permutation tests, stratified by bit-length and by $N \bmod 4$, the strongest result was $p = 0.024$ against a Bonferroni threshold of $0.0004$. Nothing survives: not the period length, not its parity, not the non-terminal maximum or sum of the partial quotients, not the count of distinct quotients, not the regulator.

The lesson generalizes far beyond continued fractions: *before you believe a correlation, subtract the size.*

---

### The one real signal, and why it's the wrong kind

There *is* one thing the period genuinely knows about the factorization — beautiful, and useless.

The period $\ell$ is odd exactly when the **negative Pell equation** $x^2 - Ny^2 = -1$ is solvable, i.e. when the period-end sign $hq_{-1}-h_{-1}q$ comes out $-1$. And that solvability is a genuine constraint on the factors:

> **Negative-Pell Obstruction.** *If $x^2 - Ny^2 = -1$ has a solution, then no prime divisor of $N$ is $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$. Equivalently, every odd prime factor $p$ of $N$ satisfies $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.*

The reason is a one-liner. Reduce modulo a prime $p \mid N$: the term $Ny^2$ dies and $x^2 \equiv -1 \pmod p$, so $-1$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$, which by Euler's criterion means $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

Run it backwards: if some prime factor is $3 \bmod 4$, negative Pell is blocked, the period must be even, and every period-end unit has norm $+1$. For $N = 21$ the prime $3$ does exactly that — and indeed $\ell(21) = 6$ with $55^2 - 21\cdot 12^2 = +1$. The dichotomy held on the data without exception: $40$ of $40$ semiprimes of type $(3,3)$ mod $4$ had even period, $40$ of $40$ of type $(1,3)$ likewise, while type $(1,1)$ split, $26$ of $40$ odd.

So the channel really does transmit one bit about the primes: *are they both $1$ mod $4$?* And that is where it stops. A congruence class is not a factor:

> **No-Pinning Theorem.** *Fix any modulus $M$, any residues $a, b$ coprime to $M$, and any bound $B$. Then there exist two genuinely different semiprimes $p_1q_1 < p_2q_2$, all four primes exceeding $B$, with $p_1 \equiv p_2 \equiv a$ and $q_1 \equiv q_2 \equiv b \pmod M$.*

This is Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions: each class coprime to $M$ contains infinitely many primes, so pick four in increasing order. The consequence is stark. Knowing $p \equiv q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ narrows the candidates from "all primes" to "half of all primes" — from an infinite set to an infinite set. Real information, zero localization. You cannot factor by learning congruence classes of the factors unless the modulus grows past $\sqrt N$ — at which point you are doing trial division.

---

### The shape of the verdict

The continued fraction of $\sqrt N$ carries **real** arithmetic structure: the fundamental unit of a real quadratic field, exactly, with the norm pinned to $\pm d$ at every step. It carries **real** information about the factors: the period's parity is the negative-Pell bit. And it possesses a **real** exit to factorization, firing about three-quarters of the time.

Yet the channel yields nothing, for reasons that stack:

- the information it carries is a **congruence class**, and congruence classes pin nothing;
- the exit that yields a factor **requires the full period**, of length $\approx 0.4\sqrt N$ — exponential in the digits of $N$, and worse than methods already known;
- the exit **cannot exist at all** for prime powers;
- the cases where the period is **cheap** are exactly the algebraic families $N = m^2 + c$, whose gcds are provably trivial, and such cases have **density zero**;
- and the statistic that looked like it saw a factor was really $2\lfloor\sqrt N\rfloor$ in a costume.

There is a broader point here about what "symmetric" means. Anything you compute from $N = pq$ without knowing which factor is which is a symmetric function of $p$ and $q$. For *polynomial* functions, the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials says every such function is a polynomial in $p+q$ and $pq$ — so it can only ever tell you $N$ and the sum, which is to say nothing you didn't put in. That theorem covers only polynomials. The continued-fraction period is the canonical *non-polynomial* symmetric function of $N$: wildly discontinuous, deeply arithmetic, the most plausible escape hatch from the polynomial barrier.

It isn't one. The barrier is not about polynomials. It is about symmetry itself.

And it should not sound deflationary to prove that a beautiful object contains no exploit. Cryptography rests on negative knowledge: we trust factoring-based systems not because anyone proved factoring hard — nobody has — but because a long list of plausible attack routes has been walked to its end and found to terminate.

Open questions remain, and they are pretty ones. Why does the period-end unit split $N$ about three-quarters of the time — is that exactly equidistribution of the unit over the $2$-torsion $\{\pm1\}\times\{\pm1\}$ of $(\mathbb{Z}/N)^\times$ modulo the diagonal forced by genus theory, giving $1 - 2^{1-r}$ for $r$ distinct prime factors? Is the period-end pair $(h_\ell, q_\ell)$ a complete invariant, so that $N = (h_\ell^2 \mp 1)/q_\ell^2$ recovers $N$ and distinct $N$ never collide? Can one prove unconditionally that $\ell(N) \gg \log N/\log\log N$?

But the headline is settled. The continued fraction of $\sqrt N$ hands you the fundamental unit of a real quadratic field. It hands you a bit about the factors' residues mod $4$. It even hands you, sometimes, an honest factorization.

It just never hands you one cheaply. And in this subject, cheap is the only thing that counts.

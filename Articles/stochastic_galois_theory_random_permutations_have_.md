# When Polynomials Shuffle: The Correct Random-Permutation Story over Finite Fields

## A beautiful conjecture—and the turn it forces us to take

A polynomial can look like a small row of symbols and still hide an intricate world of symmetry. Over the rational numbers, one studies the ways its roots can be permuted without disturbing any algebraic relation among them. For a “typical” polynomial of degree $n$, the answer is often the largest possible symmetry group: the symmetric group $S_n$. This familiar phenomenon invites a seductive finite-field analogy. Choose a monic degree-$n$ polynomial at random over a field with $q$ elements; should its Galois group usually be $S_n$ as $q$ grows?

The answer is no, and the reason is as illuminating as the conjecture itself. Every finite extension of a finite field has a cyclic Galois group, generated by the Frobenius transformation $x\mapsto x^q$. Once $n\ge 3$, the group $S_n$ is noncommutative, so it cannot be cyclic. The abstract Galois group of a finite-field polynomial therefore cannot literally become $S_n$ in the proposed sense.

But the random-permutation idea does not disappear. It moves to the right object.

The Frobenius transformation permutes the geometric roots of a squarefree polynomial. Its cycles record the degrees of the polynomial’s irreducible factors: a factor of degree $d$ corresponds to a cycle of length $d$. Thus finite-field polynomials do imitate random permutations—not through their abstract Galois groups, but through their factorization patterns and Frobenius cycle types.

At the first and most accessible level, linear factors correspond to fixed points. A root in the ground field is a one-cycle of Frobenius. This observation leads to an exact bridge: a uniformly chosen monic polynomial of any positive degree over any finite commutative ring has exactly one root on average, while a uniformly chosen permutation of any positive number of letters has exactly one fixed point on average. The agreement is not asymptotic. It is exact.

## The polynomial experiment

Let $K$ be a finite commutative ring with $q=|K|$ elements, and fix a positive integer $n$. Every monic polynomial of degree $n$ can be written uniquely as

$$
f_v(X)=X^n+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}v_iX^i,
$$

where the coefficient vector $v=(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$ lies in $K^n$. There are therefore $q^n$ such polynomials.

For a polynomial $f_v$, let $R(f_v)$ denote the number of distinct elements $r\in K$ satisfying $f_v(r)=0$. The central arithmetic result is the following.

**Exact Root-Incidence Theorem.** For every finite commutative ring $K$ and every integer $n>0$,

$$
\sum_{v\in K^n}R(f_v)=q^n.
$$

Consequently, if the coefficient vector is chosen uniformly from $K^n$, then

$$
\mathbb E[R(f_v)]=1.
$$

The proof is a model of double counting. Instead of asking how many roots each polynomial has, count pairs $(f_v,r)$ for which $f_v(r)=0$. Fix the candidate root $r$ first. If $n=m+1$, then the coefficients $v_1,\ldots,v_m$ may be chosen freely. Once they are chosen, the constant coefficient is forced by

$$
v_0=-\left(r^{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_ir^i\right).
$$

So exactly $q^m=q^{n-1}$ monic degree-$n$ polynomials vanish at a prescribed $r$. There are $q$ choices of $r$, giving $q\cdot q^{n-1}=q^n$ incidence pairs. Dividing by the $q^n$ polynomials yields average root count one.

Notice the strength of the statement. It does not require $K$ to be a field. Zero divisors do not interfere, because no division occurs: the coefficient of $v_0$ is $1$, so the root equation simply determines that coefficient. The theorem is a fact about affine fibers over any finite commutative ring.

## The permutation experiment

Now turn to $S_n$, the group of all permutations of $n$ letters. For $\sigma\in S_n$, let $F(\sigma)$ be its number of fixed points. There are $n!$ permutations in all.

**Exact Fixed-Point-Incidence Theorem.** For every integer $n>0$,

$$
\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}F(\sigma)=n!.
$$

Consequently, a uniformly random permutation satisfies

$$
\mathbb E[F(\sigma)]=1.
$$

Again the proof counts incidences. Fix a letter $i$. A permutation fixing $i$ may arrange the remaining $n-1$ letters arbitrarily, so exactly $(n-1)!$ permutations fix that chosen letter. Summing over all $n$ possible letters gives

$$
n(n-1)!=n!.
$$

This elementary argument contains the same architecture as the polynomial count. On the polynomial side, fixing a root leaves $n-1$ free coefficients. On the permutation side, fixing a point leaves $n-1$ freely permuted letters. The details differ, but the incidence geometry is identical.

## An exact bridge between two worlds

Place the two totals side by side. The polynomial sample space has size $q^n$ and total root incidence $q^n$. The permutation sample space has size $n!$ and total fixed-point incidence $n!$. Clearing denominators gives the exact identity

$$
\left(\sum_{v\in K^n}R(f_v)\right)n!
=
\left(\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}F(\sigma)\right)q^n.
$$

**Root–Fixed-Point Bridge.** In the uniform model of monic degree-$n$ polynomials over a finite commutative ring and the uniform model of permutations in $S_n$, the expected number of ground-ring roots and the expected number of fixed points are both exactly $1$.

This is the corrected first chapter of stochastic Galois theory over finite fields. It does not assert that the groups are the same. It asserts that a statistic arising from Frobenius cycles obeys precisely the same first-moment law as the matching permutation statistic.

A small example makes the count tangible. Over the field with three elements, there are $3^2=9$ monic quadratics. Across all nine polynomials, the total number of roots in the field is exactly $9$. Some quadratics have no roots, some have one distinct root, and some have two, but their average is exactly one. For monic cubics over the same field, there are $3^3=27$ coefficient vectors and again exactly $27$ root incidences. Meanwhile, among the six permutations of three letters, the total number of fixed-point incidences is $6$. Multiplying the polynomial total by $6$ and the permutation total by $27$ produces the same number, $162$.

## Why the original group claim fails

The correction deserves to be stated sharply.

**Finite-Field Cyclicity Obstruction.** Let $L/K$ be a finite extension of finite fields. Its group of $K$-automorphisms is cyclic. For every $n\ge 3$, it is therefore not isomorphic to $S_n$.

The proof has two ingredients. First, finite-field automorphisms are generated by a suitable Frobenius map, so the automorphism group is cyclic and hence commutative. Second, $S_n$ is noncommutative when $n\ge 3$: for example, the transpositions swapping the first two and the last two of three selected letters do not commute. An isomorphism would preserve commutativity, producing a contradiction.

This also explains why the low-degree cases can mislead. The group $S_2$ is cyclic, so degree two does not reveal the obstruction. At degree three, $S_3$ becomes noncommutative, and the distinction between an abstract Galois group and a Frobenius permutation becomes unavoidable. A cubic may be reducible or irreducible, separable or inseparable, and its Frobenius permutation may have cycle type $1+1+1$, $2+1$, or $3$. None of these facts makes its finite-field Galois group literally $S_3$.

## From fixed points to factorization patterns

Why should anyone care that two averages equal one? Because first moments are often the visible edge of a much richer distributional correspondence.

For a squarefree polynomial over $\mathbb F_q$, Frobenius acts on its roots. If the polynomial factors into distinct irreducibles of degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_k$, then the Frobenius permutation has cycles of lengths $d_1,\ldots,d_k$. Linear factors are fixed points; quadratic factors are two-cycles; and so forth. A partition of $n$ describing factor degrees becomes a cycle type in $S_n$.

This dictionary is useful in coding theory, where factorization controls cyclic codes; in computational algebra, where algorithms probe roots and irreducible factors; and in arithmetic statistics, where one asks how algebraic objects distribute across symmetry classes. It also offers a practical principle for randomized testing. If a program enumerates all monic polynomials over a small finite field, the total root count must equal the number of polynomials. That invariant catches subtle mistakes in polynomial evaluation, coefficient order, or root counting.

The theorem also clarifies what an experiment should measure. Enumerating “Galois groups” over finite fields and expecting most of them to be $S_n$ tests a false premise. Enumerating factorization types and comparing them with permutation cycle types tests the correct analogy. Repeated roots—equivalently, failures of squarefreeness over a field—form the principal exceptional locus that must be separated before a clean cycle-type comparison.

## The road ahead

The next natural step is to move from one fixed point to several. Count ordered tuples of distinct roots of a random polynomial, then compare them with ordered tuples of distinct fixed points of a random permutation. Such factorial moments are the route toward the familiar Poisson law with mean $1$ that governs fixed points of large random permutations. In a stable range, exact identities may persist before asymptotic corrections appear.

A second step is to count squarefree monic polynomials exactly and then sort them by factorization type. For each partition $\lambda$ of $n$, one can compare the proportion of polynomials whose irreducible-factor degrees form $\lambda$ with the proportion of permutations whose cycles have lengths $\lambda$. The expected discrepancy is naturally of order $1/q$ for fixed $n$, not because finite-field Galois groups approach $S_n$, but because Frobenius cycle statistics approach the permutation model as the field grows.

The lesson is broader than this one problem. In mathematics, a false conjecture can still point toward a true structure. Here the mistaken identification of groups gives way to an exact equality of moments and a precise factorization–cycle dictionary. Random polynomials over finite fields do shuffle their roots like permutations—but Frobenius is the dealer, factorization is the record of the shuffle, and fixed points are where the two stories first meet.

# When Multiplication Forgets: The Hidden Arithmetic of Additive Ideals

## A familiar number system with unfamiliar objects

The natural numbers seem to be the safest place in mathematics. We learn to add and multiply them before we learn what a proof is. Their arithmetic feels rigid: if $ac=bc$ and $c$ is positive, then $a=b$. Multiplication does not erase distinctions.

But arithmetic changes when the “numbers” being multiplied are not individual integers but *collections* of integers. In the natural-number semiring, an **ideal** is a set $I\subseteq\mathbb N$ that contains $0$, is closed under addition, and remains closed when any of its elements is multiplied by an arbitrary natural number. Equivalently, for some collection of generators $a_1,\ldots,a_t$,

$$
I=\langle a_1,\ldots,a_t\rangle
 =\{n_1a_1+\cdots+n_ta_t:n_1,\ldots,n_t\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

Thus $\langle5,17\rangle$ is the set of all nonnegative combinations $5u+17v$. It contains $0,5,10,15,17,20,22$, and so on, but not every natural number. Ideals of this kind are close relatives of numerical semigroups, the additive systems used in coin-exchange problems, scheduling, coding theory, and discrete optimization.

Ideals can themselves be added and multiplied. Their product $IJ$ is generated by all pairwise products $ab$ with $a\in I$ and $b\in J$. For finitely generated ideals this becomes concrete:

$$
\langle a_1,\ldots,a_m\rangle\langle b_1,\ldots,b_n\rangle
=\langle a_ib_j:1\le i\le m,\ 1\le j\le n\rangle.
$$

This operation creates a second arithmetic above ordinary arithmetic. Its most striking feature is that cancellation can fail.

## The witness that makes a difference disappear

Consider three ideals:

$$
A=\langle5,17\rangle,
\qquad B=\langle5,17,43\rangle,
\qquad C=\langle5,11,19,23\rangle.
$$

The ideals $A$ and $B$ are genuinely different. The number $43$ lies in $B$ by construction, but it cannot lie in $A$: the equation

$$
43=5u+17v
$$

has no solution in nonnegative integers. Indeed, $v$ can only be $0$, $1$, or $2$; the three remainders $43$, $26$, and $9$ are not multiples of $5$.

Now multiply both ideals by $C$. One might expect the extra generator $43$ to remain visible. Instead,

$$
AC=BC.
$$

This is not a numerical coincidence but an equality of infinite sets. The reason is that the only potentially new product generators are

$$
43\cdot5=215,\quad 43\cdot11=473,
\quad 43\cdot19=817,\quad 43\cdot23=989.
$$

Every one of them was already generated inside $AC$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
215&=10\cdot5+15\cdot11,\\
473&=17\cdot19+30\cdot5,\\
817&=34\cdot11+17\cdot19+5\cdot19+5\cdot5,\\
989&=17\cdot23+17\cdot19+25\cdot11.
\end{aligned}
$$

Here $10,15,30,34,25$ all belong to $A$, while $5,11,19,23$ belong to $C$. Each displayed term is therefore a product of an element of $A$ with an element of $C$, and sums of such products belong to $AC$. Since $A\subset B$, the inclusion $AC\subseteq BC$ is automatic; the four certificates prove the reverse inclusion.

**Noncancellation Theorem.** *Multiplication of ideals of $\mathbb N$ is not cancellative. In particular, the three nonzero ideals above satisfy $A\ne B$ but $AC=BC$.*

The common factor $C$ behaves like a coarse lens: after multiplication by it, the extra information carried by $43$ becomes invisible. This is the first conceptual turn. Rather than treating information loss as a defect, we can use it to define a meaningful equivalence.

## Similarity: equality after a change of scale

In any commutative multiplicative system, call two objects $x$ and $y$ **similar** if there exists an object $c$ such that

$$
xc=yc.
$$

For ideals of $\mathbb N$, the witness is required to be nonzero; otherwise the zero ideal would make every pair look alike. Similarity is an equivalence relation. Every object is similar to itself by taking the multiplicative unit. Reversing an equality proves symmetry. For transitivity, if $xd=yd$ and $ye=ze$, then commutativity and associativity give

$$
x(de)=(xd)e=(yd)e=(ye)d=(ze)d=z(de).
$$

Similarity also respects multiplication. If $a_1c_1=a_2c_1$ and $b_1c_2=b_2c_2$, then

$$
(a_1b_1)(c_1c_2)=(a_2b_2)(c_1c_2).
$$

So one may multiply similarity classes consistently. The resulting viewpoint records exactly what survives after all possible common multipliers are allowed.

There is also a universal test for useful numerical measurements. Suppose $f$ turns products into products and takes values in a cancellative commutative monoid. If $a$ and $b$ are similar, say $ac=bc$, then

$$
f(a)f(c)=f(ac)=f(bc)=f(b)f(c).
$$

Cancellation in the target yields $f(a)=f(b)$. Thus any multiplicative invariant valued in a cancellative world is automatically blind to distinctions erased by similarity. This explains why similarity is not merely an ad hoc repair: it is the natural resolution seen by every well-behaved multiplicative measurement.

## Integral closure as stable membership

The next idea asks when a natural number behaves *as though* it were already in an ideal. For $r\in\mathbb N$, let $\langle r\rangle$ denote the principal ideal of multiples of $r$. We say that $r$ is **integral over** an ideal $A$ if there exists a nonzero ideal $D$ such that

$$
\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD.
$$

The **integral closure** $\overline A$ is the set of all natural numbers integral over $A$.

This definition is a stability test. The number $r$ need not initially belong to $A$, but after multiplication by a suitable nonzero witness, every contribution made by $r$ is absorbed by the corresponding product with $A$. It is membership after changing scale.

Three structural results make this closure useful.

**Containment Theorem.** *Every ideal lies inside its integral closure: $A\subseteq\overline A$.*

To see this, take $r\in A$ and use the unit ideal $\mathbb N$ as witness. Since every multiple of $r$ lies in $A$, one has $\langle r\rangle\mathbb N\subseteq A\mathbb N$.

**Monotonicity Theorem.** *If $A\subseteq B$, then $\overline A\subseteq\overline B$.*

Indeed, any certificate $\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD$ remains valid after replacing $A$ by the larger ideal $B$.

**Similarity Invariance Theorem.** *Similar nonzero ideals have the same integral closure.*

Suppose $AC=BC$ with $C\ne0$, and suppose $r$ is integral over $A$, witnessed by $D\ne0$. Multiplying the certificate by $C$ gives

$$
\langle r\rangle(DC)
\subseteq (AD)C
= A(DC)
= B(DC),
$$

where the middle rearrangement uses commutativity and $AC=BC$. The product $DC$ is nonzero because a product of positive natural numbers chosen from $D$ and $C$ is positive. Hence $r$ is integral over $B$. Symmetry gives the reverse implication.

The closure therefore captures information that survives precisely the kind of multiplication that destroyed cancellation in the example.

## Adding one generator: the exact criterion

The most revealing theorem ties the entire story together.

**Adjunction Criterion.** *Let $A$ be an ideal of $\mathbb N$ and let $r\in\mathbb N$. The enlarged ideal $A+\langle r\rangle$ is similar to $A$ if and only if $r$ is integral over $A$.*

The proof is almost a one-line identity once the definitions are in place. Ideal multiplication distributes over ideal sum:

$$
(A+\langle r\rangle)D=AD+\langle r\rangle D.
$$

If $r$ is integral over $A$, choose nonzero $D$ with $\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD$. The right-hand side then collapses to $AD$, proving similarity. Conversely, if multiplication by some nonzero $D$ makes the enlarged ideal equal to $A$, then the second summand must already be contained in $AD$, which is exactly the certificate of integrality.

This theorem gives integral closure an operational meaning: $\overline A$ consists exactly of those generators that may be adjoined without changing the similarity class of $A$. Closure is not just a larger set attached to an ideal; it is the boundary between genuinely new arithmetic information and information that can be erased by multiplication.

In the explicit example, $B=A+\langle43\rangle$ and $BC=AC$. The adjunction criterion therefore says that $43$ is integral over $A$, with $C$ serving as its witness, even though $43\notin A$. Ordinary membership says “absent”; stable multiplicative behavior says “already latent.”

## A small certificate for an infinite claim

One feature of this example deserves emphasis. The equality $AC=BC$ compares two infinite sets, yet its decisive evidence consists of four finite identities. This is possible because generators compress closure laws: once $215$, $473$, $817$, and $989$ are known to lie in $AC$, every nonnegative combination of them lies there too. The infinite tail follows automatically.

That pattern is algorithmically valuable. To test whether adjoining a generator $r$ becomes invisible after multiplication by a finitely generated witness $D=\langle d_1,\ldots,d_s\rangle$, form the finitely many numbers $rd_1,\ldots,rd_s$. Then test whether each is a nonnegative combination of generators of $AD$. A bounded dynamic program—the same basic mechanism used in coin-change software—can search for these combinations. When it succeeds, the resulting decompositions are transparent certificates that anyone can check by arithmetic.

The procedure does more than confirm examples. It translates a question about whole infinite ideals into finitely many reachability problems. Algebra supplies the reduction; computation can find the witnesses; explicit equations explain the answer.

## Why this arithmetic matters

Numerical semigroups model systems built from indivisible resources: coin denominations, packet sizes, production batches, and attainable time slots. Their gaps measure impossible totals. Multiplying ideals combines two such systems, creating attainable sums of pairwise products. The noncancellation example warns that this combination can conceal distinctions in the original systems.

That warning is useful. In data compression, two inputs may become indistinguishable after a feature map; in physics, microscopic states may share the same coarse-grained observable; in optimization, different generating sets may define the same stabilized feasible behavior. Similarity provides an algebraic language for this loss of resolution, while integral closure identifies which apparent additions do not alter the stabilized system.

The arithmetic of natural numbers is therefore richer than the arithmetic of its elements. At the level of ideals, multiplication can forget. Yet the forgetting is structured: it defines equivalence classes, constrains all cancellative multiplicative invariants, and leads to a closure operation with a precise adjunction law. The equation $AC=BC$ with $A\ne B$ is not the end of cancellation. It is the beginning of a new arithmetic—one designed to measure exactly what multiplication remembers.

# The Ghost in the Class Field: How One Congruence Explains Frobenius

## A machine for turning primes into symmetries

Number theory has a recurring dream: that the arithmetic of a field — which primes split, which stay whole, which collapse — should be *the same thing* as the symmetry of a field extension. The dream has a name, class field theory, and a centerpiece, the **Hilbert class field**: for every number field $K$ there should be a canonical finite extension $H/K$, unramified at every prime, whose symmetry group is a perfect copy of the ideal class group of $K$:
$$\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \;\simeq\; \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K).$$

On the left, symmetries. On the right, the failure of unique factorization. The theorem says they are the same object wearing two costumes.

This article is about what happens when you try to *build* that dictionary from the ground up, insisting at every step on airtight, unconditional arguments. Two things emerge. First, a surprise about the most famous entry in the dictionary — the cyclotomic case, where the correspondence is usually written $p \mapsto (\zeta \mapsto \zeta^p)$. That entry, it turns out, is not a fact about class field theory at all. It is a fact about *congruences*, provable in a couple of pages, with no unramifiedness hypothesis anywhere. Second, a sharp constraint on what a Hilbert class field can possibly be, which turns the existence question into a battery of concrete, falsifiable predictions.

## The oldest symmetry in the book

Fix a whole number $n$ and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $n$-th root of unity: a complex number with $\zeta^n = 1$ and no smaller positive power equal to $1$. Think of $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$, a point on the unit circle at angle $1/n$ of a full turn. The field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ — the rationals with $\zeta$ thrown in — is the *cyclotomic field*, and its symmetries are laughably easy to describe: every symmetry must send $\zeta$ to another primitive $n$-th root, i.e. to $\zeta^m$ for some $m$ coprime to $n$, and this assignment is a group isomorphism
$$(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times} \;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\; \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q}), \qquad m \;\longmapsto\; \sigma_m: \zeta \mapsto \zeta^m .$$
Call $\sigma_m$ the *power map*. Everything about the abelian arithmetic of $\mathbb{Q}$ is supposed to be encoded here.

The arithmetic side of the dictionary is the **Frobenius**. Pick a prime $p$ and a prime ideal $Q$ of the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ lying above it, with residue field of size $p$. A symmetry $\sigma$ is *an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$* if it acts like the $p$-th power map after reduction:
$$\sigma(x) \equiv x^{p} \pmod{Q} \qquad \text{for every } x \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta].$$
This is the shadow, inside characteristic zero, of the Frobenius endomorphism $x \mapsto x^p$ of a finite field — the one symmetry every field of characteristic $p$ possesses for free.

The classical statement of cyclotomic reciprocity is that these two objects coincide: the Frobenius at $Q$ *is* the power map $\sigma_p$. Textbooks derive this from the fact that $p$ is unramified in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ when $p \nmid n$, and then hang the whole Artin reciprocity law on it. But how much of that machinery is actually load-bearing?

## Almost none of it

Here is the first result, and it is elementary in the honest sense: you could hand it to a strong undergraduate.

> **The Frobenius congruence.** Let $A$ be any commutative ring, $p$ a prime number, and $\zeta \in A$ any element whatsoever. Suppose $\sigma : A \to A$ is a ring homomorphism with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$. Then for *every* element $x$ of the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq A$ generated by $\zeta$,
> $$\sigma(x) \equiv x^{p} \pmod{p A}.$$

No roots of unity are required, no fields, no unramifiedness, no primes of the ring. Just: if a ring map raises the generator to the $p$-th power, it raises *everything in sight* to the $p$-th power, modulo $p$.

The proof is an induction along the construction of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ from its three ingredients — integers, the generator, sums and products — and each ingredient contributes a classical congruence:

- **Integers.** Fermat's little theorem: $m^p \equiv m \pmod p$ for every integer $m$, so $\sigma(m) - m^p = m - m^p$ is divisible by $p$ in $A$.
- **The generator.** By hypothesis $\sigma(\zeta) - \zeta^p = 0$, which is certainly divisible by $p$.
- **Products.** Multiplicativity: $\sigma(xy) - (xy)^p = (\sigma x - x^p)\sigma y + x^p(\sigma y - y^p)$.
- **Sums.** The freshman's dream: $(x+y)^p = x^p + y^p + p\cdot(\text{something})$, because every binomial coefficient $\binom{p}{k}$ with $0 < k < p$ is divisible by the prime $p$.

That last point is the heart of the matter. In characteristic $p$ the map $x \mapsto x^p$ is additive; here we are not in characteristic $p$, but we are working modulo $p$, and the same divisibility saves us. Assembling the four cases gives the theorem. Notice what did *not* appear: no ramification theory, no Galois theory, no valuations. Reciprocity, in this direction, is a shadow of the binomial theorem.

## And the converse — the surprise

Anyone can guess the converse: if $\sigma$ is a Frobenius, it must send $\zeta$ to $\zeta^p$. The interesting question is what it costs to prove. Here is the answer.

> **Frobenius is the power map.** Let $S$ be an integral domain, $\zeta \in S$ with $\zeta^n = 1$, and $Q$ a prime ideal of $S$ with residue cardinality $p$ over the base, and suppose $n \notin Q$ (the order of $\zeta$ is invertible modulo $Q$). If $\sigma$ is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$, then $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$ — *exactly*, not merely modulo $Q$.

The proof is a small gem. Write $a = \sigma(\zeta)$ and $b = \zeta^p$. Both are $n$-th roots of unity: $a^n = \sigma(\zeta^n) = \sigma(1) = 1$ and $b^n = (\zeta^n)^p = 1$. And they are congruent: $a - b \in Q$, because $\sigma$ is a Frobenius. Now factor
$$0 \;=\; a^{n} - b^{n} \;=\; (a-b)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a^{i} b^{\,n-1-i}.$$
Suppose $a \neq b$. In a domain, $a - b \neq 0$ forces the sum to vanish. But modulo $Q$ we have $a \equiv b$, so every one of the $n$ terms is congruent to $b^{n-1}$, and the sum is congruent to $n\,b^{n-1}$. Hence $n\,b^{n-1} \in Q$. And $b$ is a unit (its inverse is $b^{n-1}$), so $n \in Q$ — contradicting the hypothesis. Therefore $a = b$.

Read that again: the congruence $a \equiv b \pmod Q$ has been *upgraded to an equality*, purely because $a$ and $b$ are both roots of the separable polynomial $X^n - 1$. Distinct roots of unity cannot collide modulo a prime that does not divide $n$ — the same rigidity that makes Hensel lifting work, deployed by hand.

Combining the two directions gives a clean equivalence with no class field theory in it at all:

> **Cyclotomic reciprocity, ring-theoretic form.** Let $S = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ be a domain generated by a root of unity of order $n$, and let $Q$ be a prime of $S$ with residue cardinality a prime $p$, with $p \in Q$ and $n \notin Q$. Then a ring endomorphism $\sigma$ of $S$ over the base is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ **if and only if** $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$.

And an immediate corollary that is genuinely stronger than the textbook statement:

> **Rigidity.** Under the same hypotheses, the arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ is *unique* if it exists — with no unramifiedness assumption. Two Frobenius maps agree on $\zeta$, hence on all of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$.

Standard uniqueness statements require the prime to be unramified. Here uniqueness is free, because the generator has a forced image and a ring map is determined by where it sends its generator. The ramification hypothesis in the classical theorem is not doing the work one imagines; the *monogenicity* is.

Specialised back to the field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$: for every rational prime $p$ coprime to $n$ and every prime $Q$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ above $p$, the power map $\sigma_p$ restricted to $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ is the arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$, and it is the only one. The correspondence $p \mapsto \sigma_p$, the very first line of the reciprocity dictionary, is thereby established from scratch.

## What a Hilbert class field must look like

Now the harder half of the dream. Package the wish list for a number field $K$ into a single datum: a **Hilbert class field datum** for $K$ is a finite Galois extension $H/K$ that is unramified at every finite prime of $H$, together with an isomorphism $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \simeq \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K)$ onto the ideal class group. This is exactly what "existence of the Hilbert class field" asks for; naming it lets us ask what it *forces*, before knowing whether it exists.

The answers are immediate but not vacuous, and they sharply constrain any construction:

- **Degree is the class number.** For a Galois extension the number of symmetries equals the degree, so $[H:K] = \#\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K) = h_K$. Any purported construction whose degree is not the class number is wrong on the spot.
- **The Galois group is abelian.** The class group is abelian and $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$ is isomorphic to it, so $H/K$ is an abelian extension. Nonabelian extensions are ruled out without computing anything.
- **Triviality detector.** $H = K$ (the structure map $K \to H$ is onto) precisely when $h_K = 1$. Unique factorization in $\mathcal{O}_K$ is exactly the statement that $K$ has no unramified abelian extensions beyond itself.
- **Uniqueness of degree.** Any two Hilbert class field data over the same $K$ have the same degree over $K$, since both degrees equal $h_K$.
- **The class-number-one case is realised.** If $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a principal ideal ring, then $H = K$ works: the extension is trivially Galois and unramified, and both the Galois group and the class group are trivial, so the required isomorphism exists.

Together these are a falsifiability harness. The existence question for a given $K$ is now a search with a known target degree, a known target group structure, and a known trivial answer whenever $h_K = 1$.

## The test case: $\mathbb{Q}$ has nowhere to go

The smallest nontrivial consistency check is $\mathbb{Q}$, whose class number is $1$. The prediction is that the *only* Hilbert class field datum over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the trivial one. Proving this needs a genuine theorem, not bookkeeping — and the route is Minkowski's, a highlight of nineteenth-century number theory.

> **No unramified extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$.** If $K$ is a number field in which every prime of $\mathcal{O}_K$ is unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$, then $K = \mathbb{Q}$.

The argument is a three-step collapse. Ramification of a prime is detected exactly by that prime dividing the *different ideal* $\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}$; if nothing ramifies, no maximal ideal divides the different, so $\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}} = (1)$. The absolute norm of the different is the absolute value of the discriminant, so $|d_K| = 1$. But Minkowski's geometry-of-numbers bound says $|d_K| > 2$ for every number field of degree greater than $1$ — a lattice point argument: the ring of integers, viewed as a lattice in Euclidean space, is too dense to avoid a nonzero point in a suitable convex body unless the discriminant is large. The two facts collide, forcing degree $1$.

Feeding this back through the constraints: any Hilbert class field datum $H/\mathbb{Q}$ is unramified everywhere over $\mathbb{Z}$, hence $[H:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$, hence $H = \mathbb{Q}$ — precisely as $h_{\mathbb{Q}} = 1$ predicts. The dictionary passes its first test, and it passes it because of Minkowski, not because of anything circular.

## What the two halves say to each other

Put the pieces side by side and a picture emerges of where the difficulty in class field theory actually lives.

The cyclotomic half of reciprocity — the part everyone quotes as the model case — is *cheap*. It needs Fermat, the freshman's dream, and the separability of $X^n-1$. It does not need unramifiedness, and it works over any commutative ring monogenic over $\mathbb{Z}$.

The existence half is *expensive*, and it is expensive in a specific place: producing an extension with prescribed ramification and prescribed Galois group. Everything one can say about a Hilbert class field before constructing it — its degree, its abelianness, when it is trivial — follows in a few lines. The construction itself does not.

That asymmetry suggests where to push. If Frobenius rigidity holds for cyclotomic rings with no unramifiedness hypothesis, does it hold for *every* monogenic ring $\mathbb{Z}[\theta]$? The proof above used only that the generator is a root of a polynomial that stays separable modulo $Q$ — so a counterexample must be ramified, and the natural place to hunt is a ring like $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ at the prime $(2, 1+\sqrt{-3})$. Likewise, the collapse of unramified extensions over $\mathbb{Q}$ used the discriminant bound, which is unavailable for a relative extension $H/K$; the relative statement — that a base field of class number one has no everywhere-unramified extension — must go instead through the relative different being trivial.

The dream of class field theory is that arithmetic and symmetry are one subject. Chasing it with the demand that every step be complete has a way of separating the parts that are deep from the parts that merely look deep. Here the verdict is clear: the reciprocity dictionary's first entry is a congruence, and the mystery is not in reading the dictionary but in proving it has an entry for every word.

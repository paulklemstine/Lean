# Two Ways to Photograph a Symmetry — and Why One of Them Blinks

## A conjecture about seeing

Imagine you are handed a locked box. Inside is a *symmetric object*: a necklace, a molecule, a tiling, a chessboard pattern — anything on which a group of symmetries acts. You are not allowed to look inside. You are only allowed to ask questions, and each question returns a single number.

There are two classical questionnaires that mathematicians use.

The first is the **table of marks**, invented by William Burnside more than a century ago. For each subgroup $H$ of the symmetry group $G$, you ask: *how many points of the object are frozen by every single symmetry in $H$ at once?* Write the answer as $|X^H|$, where $X$ is the object (a finite set of points) and
$$X^H = \{x \in X : h\cdot x = x \text{ for all } h \in H\}.$$
Collect all these numbers into a vector, one entry per subgroup. That vector is the **mark vector** of $X$.

The second questionnaire is the **Molien invariant**, descended from Theodor Molien's work on invariants of finite groups. For each subgroup $H$, you don't demand that a point be fixed by *everything* in $H$; instead you *average*: you ask each element $h \in H$ how many points it individually fixes, and take the mean,
$$\operatorname{Mol}_X(H) \;=\; \frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h \in H} |X^{h}|,$$
where $X^{h} = \{x : h\cdot x = x\}$. Collect these averages into a vector, again one entry per subgroup.

Both vectors are perfectly natural. Both are computable. Both are invariants: isomorphic objects give identical vectors. And so a natural question — call it **Conjecture D10** — arises:

> **Conjecture D10.** The Molien invariant is exactly the Burnside mark vector, up to scaling. That is, if two objects have the same Molien vector, then their mark vectors are proportional: $|X^H| = c\,|Y^H|$ for a single constant $c$ and all subgroups $H$.

It is the sort of statement that *feels* right. The two questionnaires are asking about the same thing — fixed points — in two slightly different accents. Surely one is a rescaled copy of the other?

This article tells the story of what the two invariants really measure, and of a six-element counterexample that kills the conjecture stone dead — together with the exact boundary of where the conjecture *is* true, and an infinite family of counterexamples, one for every prime.

## Warm-up: the averaged questionnaire is really an orbit count

The Molien invariant hides a familiar face. Burnside's orbit-counting lemma (a result Burnside himself attributed to Frobenius, and which Cauchy knew in essence) says that for a finite group $H$ acting on a finite set $X$,
$$\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}|X^{h}| \;=\; \#\{\text{orbits of } H \text{ on } X\}.$$
So $\operatorname{Mol}_X(H)$ is simply the **number of $H$-orbits** on $X$: how many pieces the object breaks into when you can only move things by symmetries from $H$.

Two immediate consequences follow, and both are worth stating on their own.

**The Molien invariant is always a whole number.** Even though it is defined as a fraction, the average $\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H}|X^h|$ is an integer, because it counts orbits. Equivalently, in the language of divisibility: **$|H|$ divides $\sum_{h\in H}|X^h|$** for every subgroup $H$ and every finite $H$-set $X$. That is a genuine arithmetic statement extracted from a purely combinatorial one, and we will cash it in for a classical theorem of number theory later on.

**Marks never exceed Molien values.** For every subgroup $H$,
$$|X^H| \;\le\; \operatorname{Mol}_X(H),$$
because a point fixed by *all* of $H$ is fixed by *each* element of $H$; averaging a family of numbers each at least $|X^H|$ cannot bring you below $|X^H|$. Better still, one can say exactly when the inequality is an equality: 

> **Equality criterion.** $\operatorname{Mol}_X(H) = |X^H|$ if and only if $H$ acts trivially on $X$ — every element of $H$ fixes every point.

The proof is a squeeze. If the average of the numbers $|X^h|$, all of which are at least $|X^H|$, is exactly $|X^H|$, then each individual $|X^h|$ equals $|X^H|$. Take $h = 1$: the identity fixes everything, so $|X^H| = |X|$, which says exactly that the whole set is $H$-fixed. Conversely, if $H$ acts trivially, every fixed-point count in sight equals $|X|$.

So already we know the two questionnaires are *not* generally equal; the interesting question is whether they carry the same *information*.

## Half of the conjecture is true, and easy

One direction is a theorem, and it explains what the Molien invariant is looking at.

Fix a single symmetry $g$. Any point fixed by $g$ is automatically fixed by $g^2$, by $g^{-1}$, by every power of $g$. In other words,
$$X^{g} = X^{\langle g\rangle},$$
the fixed set of the *cyclic subgroup generated by* $g$. Substituting this into the definition of the Molien invariant gives the identity that organises everything:

> **Averaging Theorem.** For every subgroup $H$,
> $$\operatorname{Mol}_X(H) \;=\; \frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{h\in H} \bigl|X^{\langle h\rangle}\bigr|.$$
> The Molien invariant at $H$ is the average of the *marks at the cyclic subgroups* generated by the elements of $H$.

This is the positive half of Conjecture D10: the Molien vector is a fixed linear image of the mark vector. Knowing all the marks, you can reconstruct every Molien value by a single averaging matrix that does not depend on $X$ at all. Marks $\Rightarrow$ Molien, always.

But look closely at that formula. The right-hand side only ever evaluates the mark vector at **cyclic** subgroups. Every non-cyclic subgroup is invisible to it. The Molien invariant is a photograph of the mark vector taken through a filter that lets only cyclic light through.

That immediately suggests both a positive theorem and where to hunt for a counterexample.

## Where the conjecture is true: cyclic groups, on the nose

If a subgroup $H$ happens to be cyclic — say $H = \langle g\rangle$ — then $|X^H| = |X^{g}|$ is literally a value of the fixed-point count of a single element, which the Molien data determines. This gives:

> **Cyclic Recovery Theorem.** Suppose two finite $G$-sets $X$ and $Y$ have the same element-wise fixed-point counts, $|X^{g}| = |Y^{g}|$ for all $g \in G$ (equivalently, by the Averaging Theorem, the same Molien invariant at every subgroup, whenever the two are linked as they are here). Then $|X^H| = |Y^H|$ for **every cyclic subgroup** $H$ of $G$. In particular, if every subgroup of $G$ is cyclic — for instance if $G$ itself is cyclic — the Molien data determines the entire mark vector, with scaling factor exactly $1$.

So over cyclic groups the conjecture is not merely true "up to scaling"; it is true with no scaling at all. If your symmetry group is a rotation group $\mathbb{Z}/n$, the averaged questionnaire loses nothing.

That is the good news, and it is sharp.

## Where the conjecture dies: six points and the Klein four group

The smallest group all of whose subgroups are not cyclic is the **Klein four group** $V = \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ — the symmetries of a non-square rectangle: identity, horizontal flip, vertical flip, and the $180^\circ$ rotation. It has five subgroups: the trivial one, three subgroups of order two (each generated by one of the three non-identity elements), and $V$ itself. Everything is cyclic except $V$ itself. There is exactly one "blind spot", and one is enough.

Here are two six-element objects on which $V$ acts.

**Object A: three dominoes.** $V$ has three subgroups of index two, namely the kernels of the three surjections onto $\mathbb{Z}/2$ given by $\chi_0(a,b) = a$, $\chi_1(a,b) = b$, $\chi_2(a,b) = a+b$. Each gives a two-element set on which $V$ acts by flipping through that character. Take all three, side by side: $A = V/A_0 \sqcup V/A_1 \sqcup V/A_2$, six points in three dominoes.

**Object B: the group plus two dots.** Take $V$ itself, four points permuted by translation, and add two extra points that nothing moves: $B = V \sqcup \{\bullet, \bullet\}$. Again six points.

Now interrogate them element by element.

*The identity* fixes all six points of each. Score $6$ and $6$.

*A non-identity element $g$ of $A$.* On each domino, $g$ acts by the flip $\chi_i(g)$; it fixes both points of the $i$-th domino if $\chi_i(g) = 0$, and neither point otherwise. A nonzero $g \in V$ is killed by exactly one of the three characters $\chi_0,\chi_1,\chi_2$. So $g$ fixes exactly one domino pointwise: $2$ points.

*The same $g$ on $B$.* Translation by a nonzero element of $V$ moves every point of $V$; the two dots stay put. So $g$ fixes exactly $2$ points.

The fixed-point counts agree perfectly: both objects have the character vector $(6,2,2,2)$. Hence — by the Averaging Theorem, which builds Molien values only from these numbers — **the two objects have identical Molien invariants at every one of the five subgroups**. The averaged questionnaire cannot tell them apart at all.

Now ask the *unaveraged* question at the top subgroup $H = V$.

- Object A: is there a point fixed by *all* of $V$? On the $i$-th domino, the two other characters act nontrivially, so no point is fixed by everything. Mark $0$.
- Object B: the two extra dots are fixed by everything. Mark $2$.

And at the bottom subgroup $H = \{1\}$, both marks are $6$, the total number of points.

So could the two mark vectors be proportional, $\text{mark}_A = c\cdot\text{mark}_B$? The bottom entry forces $6 = c\cdot 6$, hence $c = 1$. The top entry then forces $0 = 2$. Contradiction.

> **Conjecture D10 is false.** There exist two finite $V$-sets with identical Molien invariants at every subgroup whose Burnside mark vectors are not proportional.

There is an appealing corollary. Since the two objects have different mark vectors and marks are invariants of the isomorphism type, **the two objects are not isomorphic as symmetric objects** — even though every single symmetry of the group fixes exactly the same number of points in each. Two genuinely different worlds cast the same shadow. The mark vector is a *strictly finer* invariant than the Molien vector.

And the failure is located with surgical precision: by the Cyclic Recovery Theorem, the two mark vectors agree at all four cyclic subgroups. They differ *only* at $V$, the unique non-cyclic subgroup. The cyclic hypothesis in the positive theorem is exactly the boundary of the conjecture's validity.

## One counterexample per prime

Six points might look like an accident of smallness. It is not. The Klein four group is the case $p=2$ of an infinite family, and the general case has a pleasant projective-geometry flavour.

Fix a prime $p$ and let $E = (\mathbb{Z}/p)^2$, thought of as a two-dimensional vector space over the field $\mathbb{F}_p$ with $p$ elements. The subgroups of index $p$ are the *lines through the origin* — equivalently the kernels of the $p+1$ nonzero linear functionals up to scaling, indexed by the projective line $\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{F}_p)$, whose points may be listed as $\lambda \mapsto v_1 + c\,v_2$ for $c \in \mathbb{F}_p$ together with $\lambda\mapsto v_2$. There are $p+1$ of them.

**Object A$_p$:** the disjoint union $\bigsqcup_{\ell} E/\ell$ of the $p+1$ transitive $E$-sets of size $p$, one for each line. Total size $p(p+1) = p^2 + p$.

**Object B$_p$:** the regular set $E$ (of size $p^2$) together with $p$ fixed points. Total size $p^2 + p$.

The character computation rests on one classical fact about $\mathbb{F}_p^2$: **a nonzero vector lies on exactly one line through the origin.** Concretely, a nonzero $v = (v_1,v_2)$ is annihilated by exactly one of the $p+1$ functionals above — if $v_2 = 0$, only by $\lambda \mapsto v_2$; if $v_2 \ne 0$, only by the one with $c = -v_1/v_2$, since $v_1 + c v_2 = 0$ has the unique solution $c = -v_1/v_2$ in a field.

Consequently, for $g \ne 0$ in $E$: on Object A$_p$, $g$ fixes all $p$ points of the copy indexed by the unique line containing $g$, and no point of any other copy — total $p$. On Object B$_p$, $g$ acts freely on the regular part and fixes the $p$ added points — total $p$. At the identity both give $p^2+p$. So the characters coincide, hence so do all Molien invariants.

But the marks at the top subgroup are $0$ (no point of A$_p$ is fixed by all of $E$: each transitive piece $E/\ell$ has trivial fixed set, since $E/\ell$ has $p > 1$ points and $E$ acts transitively) versus $p$ (the added dots of B$_p$). The bottom mark forces the scalar to be $1$, the top mark then demands $0 = p$, which is false since $p$ is prime, hence positive.

> **D10 fails for every elementary abelian group of rank two.** For each prime $p$, the $(\mathbb{Z}/p)^2$-sets $\bigsqcup_\ell E/\ell$ and $E \sqcup (p \text{ points})$ have equal Molien invariants at every subgroup and non-proportional mark vectors.

The failures are therefore not sporadic; they are as abundant as the primes, and they are all built the same way — from a rank-two elementary abelian subgroup, the minimal source of non-cyclic behaviour.

## The coarse invariant still has teeth: necklaces and Fermat

It would be a mistake to conclude that the Molien invariant is worthless because it forgets things. What it retains is exactly the arithmetic.

Recall the integrality statement: $|H|$ divides $\sum_{h\in H}|X^{h}|$, because the quotient counts orbits. Feed a well-chosen action into this and out drops a theorem of number theory.

Let $Y$ be a finite set with a $G$-action, and let $X$ be the set of $k$-colourings of $Y$, that is, functions $Y \to \{1,\dots,k\}$, with $G$ acting by relabelling: $(g\cdot f)(y) = f(g^{-1}y)$. A colouring is fixed by $g$ exactly when it is constant on each cycle of $g$ — each orbit of $\langle g\rangle$ on $Y$. Hence the **cycle-index identity**
$$|X^{g}| \;=\; k^{\,c(g)}, \qquad c(g) = \#\{\text{orbits of } \langle g\rangle \text{ on } Y\}.$$
Combining with the divisibility gives a Frobenius-type congruence valid for *any* action of any finite group:
$$|G| \;\Bigm|\; \sum_{g\in G} k^{\,c(g)}.$$

Now specialise: let $G = \mathbb{Z}/n$ act on $Y = \mathbb{Z}/n$ by rotation. The rotation by $a$ decomposes $\mathbb{Z}/n$ into $\gcd(n,a)$ cycles, each of length $n/\gcd(n,a)$. The result is the classical **necklace congruence**:
$$n \;\Bigm|\; \sum_{a \in \mathbb{Z}/n} k^{\gcd(n,a)} \qquad \text{for all } n \ge 1,\ k \ge 0 .$$
Its meaning: the number of genuinely distinct $k$-coloured necklaces with $n$ beads is the integer $\frac{1}{n}\sum_a k^{\gcd(n,a)}$.

Take $n = p$ prime. Then $\gcd(p,a) = p$ for $a=0$ and $\gcd(p,a)=1$ for the other $p-1$ values, so the sum is $k^p + (p-1)k$, and divisibility by $p$ reads
$$p \mid k^p + (p-1)k = (k^p - k) + pk,$$
that is
$$k^p \equiv k \pmod p .$$
**Fermat's little theorem**, obtained by counting necklaces — the combinatorial proof, but now derived as a corollary of the very machinery whose limitations we have been probing. The Molien invariant, coarse though it is, is precisely fine enough to see this.

## What the story means

Strip away the details and the shape of the result is this. There are two invariants of a symmetric object. One of them, the mark vector, records the fixed sets of *all* subgroups. The other, the Molien invariant, is a fixed averaging of the first — and the averaging only ever samples the *cyclic* subgroups. Whenever a group has no non-cyclic subgroups, nothing is lost and the two invariants determine each other exactly. As soon as a group has a rank-two elementary abelian subgroup — the smallest possible non-cyclic obstruction — information is destroyed, and destroyed so thoroughly that the surviving data cannot even reconstruct the mark vector up to a scalar.

This is more than a negative result. It is a *diagnosis*. The Burnside ring of a group, of which the mark vector is the coordinate system, maps onto the representation ring, of which the character (and thus the Molien vector) is the coordinate system. The kernel of that map is the precise measure of what the averaged questionnaire forgets — and the Klein four group's single relation, "three dominoes $\sim$ the group plus two dots", is the smallest nonzero element of that kernel. Its prime-indexed generalisation, "$\bigsqcup_\ell E/\ell \sim E \sqcup p\cdot\text{pt}$", is the same relation seen through the projective line.

The invariants we build are always compressions of reality, and the interesting mathematics lies in describing exactly which bits get lost in compression. Here the answer is unusually crisp: **averaging over a group only sees its cyclic shadows.** Ask a group about its rotations and it will answer honestly; ask it about the ways it can be non-cyclic and the average will simply shrug.

Yet the shadows are enough for arithmetic. The very same averaging, applied to colourings of a rotating cycle, produces the necklace congruences and Fermat's little theorem. What is lost is geometric — the internal structure of the object — while what survives is numerical. That is a fair trade, provided one knows which is which. Now we do.

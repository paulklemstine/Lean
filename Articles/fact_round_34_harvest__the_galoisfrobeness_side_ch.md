# The Prime That Keeps Its Secret: Why Galois Symmetry Is a Perfect Cipher Against Factoring

*Every prime number carries a fingerprint — the way it breaks apart inside a number field. Multiply two primes together and you might hope the fingerprints survive. They don't. They are scrambled by a mechanism that is, quite literally, a one-time pad.*

---

## A lock made of two primes

Much of the internet's security still leans on a single wager: that if you multiply two large primes $p$ and $q$ to get $N = pq$, nobody can work backwards from $N$ to $p$ and $q$ in a reasonable amount of time. Over the past half-century, mathematicians have found a handful of genuinely different ways to attack that wager. Broadly, the known methods fall into four families:

1. **Congruences of squares** — find $x$ and $y$ with $x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod N$, and the greatest common divisor of $x - y$ and $N$ will often expose a factor (the quadratic and number field sieves live here).
2. **Group orders** — work in a group whose size depends secretly on $p$, and wait until an exponent happens to be a multiple of that size (Pollard's $p-1$ method, the elliptic curve method).
3. **Smoothness and index calculus** — collect many numbers that factor into small primes and do linear algebra on the exponents.
4. **Algebraic and complex-multiplication structure** — exploit special arithmetic of curves and number fields attached to $N$.

This article is about a fifth idea, one that sounds as though it ought to work. It belongs to the deepest part of number theory, Galois theory. And it can be shown, in three independent ways, to give an attacker nothing at all.

## Every prime has a fingerprint

Take a polynomial with integer coefficients, say the quartic $f(x) = x^4 - x - 1$. Reduce it modulo a prime $p$. Over the integers mod $p$, $f$ may stay irreducible, or split into a linear factor and an irreducible cubic, or into two quadratics, and so on. The list of degrees of the irreducible pieces is the **splitting type** of $p$. For a quartic, the possible types are
$$1+1+1+1,\quad 1+1+2,\quad 2+2,\quad 1+3,\quad 4.$$

Galois theory explains where these types come from. The roots of $f$ are permuted by a finite group $G$, the **Galois group**. For each prime $p$ (apart from finitely many exceptions) there is a distinguished element of $G$, the **Frobenius element** $\sigma_p$. It is really a whole conjugacy class of elements, which mathematicians call the **Artin symbol**. The splitting type of $p$ is simply the cycle type of $\sigma_p$ as a permutation of the roots. A prime that splits as $1+3$ has a Frobenius that fixes one root and cycles the other three.

How are the Frobenius elements distributed as $p$ varies? The **Chebotarev density theorem**, a far-reaching generalisation of Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, answers this. Asymptotically, Frobenius elements are spread *uniformly* over the group: each conjugacy class $C$ is hit by a proportion $|C|/|G|$ of primes. So for the purposes of probability, the right model of "the Frobenius of a random prime" is a uniformly random element of $G$.

So each prime carries a fingerprint, a uniformly random group element that controls how the prime factors in a whole tower of number fields. The tempting idea is this. Suppose we could read off some of the fingerprint of $p$ from $N = pq$ alone. Then we would learn something about $p$, and with enough fingerprints from enough fields we might pin $p$ down.

## What can you actually see from $N$?

The fingerprint of $p$ is hidden, because we don't know $p$. But some of it is visible through $N$. The key fact is the abelian part of Galois theory, class field theory. Every Galois group $G$ has a **derived subgroup** $G'$, generated by all commutators $aba^{-1}b^{-1}$. The quotient $G/G'$, the **abelianization**, is the largest commutative shadow of $G$. By the Kronecker–Weber theorem, the part of the Frobenius that lives in this shadow is determined by **congruences**: the coset $\sigma_p G'$ depends only on $p$ modulo some fixed integer. And because congruences multiply, the coset of $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ depends only on $N$ modulo that integer.

That is the side channel. From $N$ you can compute the **residue**, the coset of $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ in $G/G'$, and for an Artin-type reader perhaps even the conjugacy class of a product of Frobenius elements. The question is how much this tells you about $p$.

The answer comes in three parts.

## Wall one: the abelian shadow is small

The first barrier is a counting argument, and it is easy to state. **Mutual information**, measured in bits, quantifies how much observing one random quantity tells you about another. If $X$ is a random quantity that can take at most $k$ values, then $X$ can tell you at most $\log_2 k$ bits about *anything*.

The residue takes values in $G/G'$, which has $[G:G']$ elements. By Lagrange's theorem each coset has exactly $|G'|$ elements, so when the Frobenius is uniform the residue is uniform on the cosets, and its entropy is exactly $\log_2[G:G']$. This gives the **Abelianization Cap**:

> **The Abelianization Cap.** For a uniformly random Frobenius $\sigma$ in a finite Galois group $G$, the residue class of $\sigma$ (its coset modulo the derived subgroup) shares at most $\log_2[G:G']$ bits of information with *any* function of $\sigma$ — the splitting type, the conjugacy class, anything. The same bound holds after any further processing of either side.

The second sentence is the **data processing inequality**. You cannot manufacture information by computing with what you already have. Reducing the residue to a smaller modulus, or lumping splitting types together, can only lose bits.

The cap is far smaller than the richness of the fingerprint suggests. Take the symmetric group $S_4$, the Galois group of a "generic" quartic like $x^4 - x - 1$. It has 24 elements and five splitting types. But its derived subgroup is the alternating group $A_4$ of index 2. So **at most one bit** passes from the residue to the splitting type, and that one bit is just the sign of the permutation. Here are the caps for the smallest interesting groups:

| Galois group | order | $[G:G']$ | cap (bits) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $S_3$ | 6 | 2 | 1 |
| $S_4$ | 24 | 2 | 1 |
| $A_4$ | 12 | 3 | $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ |
| $D_4$ | 8 | 4 | 2 |
| $V_4$, $C_4$ | 4 | 4 | 2 |

For a **perfect** group, one equal to its own derived subgroup, the cap is $\log_2 1 = 0$. The icosahedral group $A_5$ is an example. Residues of such fields leak exactly nothing.

The cap is attained precisely when the readout determines the coset. The conjugacy class always does, because cosets of $G'$ are unions of conjugacy classes. The cycle type does for $S_3$ and $S_4$ but not for $A_4$ or $D_4$. In $D_4$, for instance, cycle type yields about $1.656$ of the possible $2$ bits.

## Wall two: the which-factor bit is invisible, exactly

Suppose an attacker could somehow learn the splitting types of both factors, without knowing which type belongs to which prime. There is one bit missing: *is the smaller type carried by $p$ or by $q$?*

Here something stronger than a bound holds. Anything you compute from $N$ alone is symmetric in $p$ and $q$, because $pq = qp$. The residue of $N$ is symmetric too, and so is the conjugacy class of $\sigma_p\sigma_q$. That last fact is less obvious in a non-commutative group, but it follows from the identity
$$\sigma_q\sigma_p = \sigma_p^{-1}(\sigma_p\sigma_q)\,\sigma_p,$$
which says that $\sigma_q\sigma_p$ and $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ are conjugate. Any function that only looks at conjugacy classes therefore cannot tell them apart.

Now comes a beautifully simple principle, the **Symmetry Wall**. Suppose a readout is preserved by a symmetry of the situation, and that symmetry shuffles the possible values of a hidden label transitively. Then the label is perfectly uniform, and the readout tells you *exactly zero* bits about it. Swapping $p$ and $q$ preserves every symmetric readout and flips the which-factor bit, so:

> **The Which-Factor Wall.** In any finite Galois group, abelian or not, consider pairs of Frobenius elements with different splitting types. The bit "which factor carries the smaller type" has entropy exactly $1$. Every readout invariant under exchanging the two factors carries exactly $0$ bits about it. This includes the residue of $N$, the Artin class of $\sigma_p\sigma_q$, and the unordered pair of types.

The zero is exact. It is a statement of symmetry, not a statistical estimate that might creep upward in larger examples. And the bit is genuinely there: an *ordered* readout of types (type of $p$, type of $q$) recovers it in full. The wall extends to numbers with more prime factors. For $N = p_0 p_1\cdots p_m$ with distinct splitting types, the label "which factor carries the smallest type" is worth exactly $\log_2(m+1)$ bits, and every readout symmetric under exchanging two factors leaks exactly none of them.

## Wall three: the Frobenius of $N$ is a one-time pad

The third wall is the most striking one.

Imagine we know nothing about $q$ except that, by Chebotarev, its Frobenius $\sigma_q$ is uniform on the Galois group $G$. Imagine we have narrowed the Frobenius of $p$ down to some set $A$ of candidates, uniform on $A$. And imagine, generously, that we can see the *entire* group element $\sigma_p\sigma_q$, not just its residue.

Does seeing $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ rule out any candidates for $\sigma_p$? The answer is no, and the reason takes one line. Whatever target $\sigma$ we observe and whatever candidate $x \in A$ we consider, the element $y = x^{-1}\sigma$ is a perfectly valid Frobenius for $q$, and $x\cdot y = \sigma$. Every candidate survives. Nothing is pruned, abelian group or not.

In fact the conclusion is stronger than "nothing is pruned". The posterior is *equal* to the prior. The map
$$(x, y) \longmapsto (x,\; xy)$$
is a bijection from $A \times G$ to itself. In cryptographic language this is a shear, and it is exactly the mechanism of a **one-time pad**. In a one-time pad, a message $x$ is combined with a uniformly random key $y$, and the ciphertext $xy$ is uniformly distributed *whatever the message was*. Here the "message" is the Frobenius of $p$ and the "key" is the Frobenius of $q$. Because $\sigma_q$ is uniform on the whole group, the pair $(\sigma_p, \sigma_p\sigma_q)$ is uniform on $A\times G$, so the two coordinates are independent.

> **The Frobenius One-Time Pad.** Let $G$ be any finite group, abelian or not. Let $\sigma_p$ be uniform on a candidate set $A \subseteq G$ and $\sigma_q$ independently uniform on $G$. Then *every* readout of $\sigma_p\sigma_q$ — its residue, its conjugacy class, its cycle type, the element itself — carries **exactly zero** bits about *every* readout of $\sigma_p$. Equivalently, after observing any value of the readout, the conditional distribution of $\sigma_p$ equals its prior.

The theorem would be empty if the setup simply had no information to give, so it is worth checking that it doesn't. If we were also shown $\sigma_q$, the pair $(\sigma_p\sigma_q, \sigma_q)$ determines $\sigma_p = (\sigma_p\sigma_q)\sigma_q^{-1}$ and carries the full $\log_2|A|$ bits. The information is there. It is locked away by the cofactor, which acts as the key of the pad.

## Pure synergy: what the residue does know

These walls do not say the residue of $N$ is useless. In $D_4$, a numerical calculation shows that the residue of $N$ carries
$$\frac{39}{16} - \frac34\log_2 3 + \frac{5}{64}\log_2 5 \approx 1.430 \text{ bits}$$
about the *unordered pair* of splitting types of $p$ and $q$. In $S_4$ it carries exactly one bit. What the three walls together say is that *every one of those bits is synergistic*.

- By the one-time pad, the residue of $N$ carries **zero** bits about the splitting type of $p$ alone, and, by the swap symmetry, **zero** bits about that of $q$ alone.
- By the which-factor wall, the ordered pair (type of $p$, type of $q$) carries *exactly the same* information as the unordered pair.
- By the abelianization cap, the total is never more than $\log_2[G:G']$: one bit for $S_4$, two for $D_4$.

So the residue is like two halves of a torn banknote. Neither half is worth anything alone, and the pair, even when it is worth something, never says which half was whose.

## The honest seams

A good impossibility result also says where it stops, and this one has three such places.

**First**, the cap bounds the information carried by the residue, meaning the part of the Frobenius that factors through the abelianization. A readout that does not pass through the abelian shadow is bounded only by the entropy of the splitting type itself. Nothing yet rules out a large non-abelian group combined with some genuinely non-abelian observable computable from $N$. No such observable is known.

**Second**, the which-factor wall assumes symmetry. It applies to every function of $N$, but it does not apply to a side input that treats $p$ and $q$ asymmetrically. Nobody knows how to compute such a side input from $N$ alone.

**Third**, the probabilistic model treats Frobenius elements as exactly uniform and independent. Chebotarev guarantees this in the limit, but the speed of convergence for primes of cryptographic size rests on conjectural refinements, such as effective versions under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.

A new attack from this family would therefore need all three at once. It would need a readout of $p$ that does not factor through the abelianization, that breaks the symmetry between $p$ and $q$, and that yields more than a bounded number of bits about which factor is which — all computed from $N$ alone.

## Why it matters

Negative results rarely make headlines, but they are how a field maps its own territory. This one does two things. It identifies the Galois/Frobenius side channel as a genuinely separate fifth family of ideas about factoring. Its mechanism is neither squares, nor group orders, nor smoothness, nor special curves. And it closes that family with three independent arguments: a counting bound (the abelianization cap), an exact symmetry law (the which-factor wall) and an exact independence law (the one-time pad).

Every prime carries an elaborate fingerprint, governed by some of the deepest symmetry in mathematics. But multiplying by an unknown second prime scrambles the fingerprint exactly as a one-time pad scrambles a message, with no residue of the original left over.

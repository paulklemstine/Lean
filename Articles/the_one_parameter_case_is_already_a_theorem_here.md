# The Polynomials That Almost Always Say Only $-1$, $0$, and $1$

## A very stubborn pattern

Take the numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots, n$ and ask: which of them share no common factor with $n$? Those are the *primitive* residues, and they label the "genuinely $n$-fold" rotations of the circle. Package them into a single polynomial — the unique monic integer polynomial $\Phi_n(X)$ whose roots are exactly the primitive $n$-th roots of unity
$$\Phi_n(X) \;=\; \prod_{\substack{1 \le k \le n \\ \gcd(k,n)=1}} \bigl(X - e^{2\pi i k/n}\bigr)$$
— and something startling happens. Even though this product runs over complex numbers spread all around the unit circle, the result always has *integer* coefficients. Better still: the coefficients you actually see are almost always tiny.

$$\Phi_1 = X - 1, \qquad \Phi_2 = X+1, \qquad \Phi_3 = X^2+X+1, \qquad \Phi_4 = X^2+1,$$
$$\Phi_{12} = X^4 - X^2 + 1, \qquad \Phi_{15} = X^8 - X^7 + X^5 - X^4 + X^3 - X + 1 .$$

Compute a hundred of these and you will see nothing but $-1$, $0$, and $1$. Compute a thousand — still nothing else, for a long, long time. The pattern is so persistent that in the nineteenth century it was widely believed to be a theorem: the coefficients of a cyclotomic polynomial are always $0$ or $\pm 1$.

They are not. The first counterexample is $n = 105$, and it is worth pausing on why it took so long to find.

## The one-parameter and two-parameter worlds are safe

For a prime $p$ the answer is transparent:
$$\Phi_p(X) = 1 + X + X^2 + \cdots + X^{p-1},$$
all coefficients equal to $1$. That is the *one-parameter* case, and it is completely rigid.

The *two-parameter* case — $n = pq$ a product of two distinct primes — is the first genuinely interesting one, and it is the hinge of everything that follows. Here there is a beautiful closed description. Call a nonnegative integer $m$ **representable** if $m = ip + jq$ for some nonnegative integers $i, j$; this is exactly the *numerical semigroup* $\langle p, q \rangle$ generated by $p$ and $q$ — the "postage stamp" set of amounts you can pay with stamps of denominations $p$ and $q$. Then for every $m$ with $0 < m < pq$,
$$[X^{m}]\,\Phi_{pq} \;=\; \mathbf{1}\{m \text{ representable}\} - \mathbf{1}\{m-1 \text{ representable}\}.$$

**Theorem (Two-parameter flatness).** *For distinct primes $p \ne q$, every coefficient of $\Phi_{pq}$ lies in $\{-1,0,1\}$.*

The proof is contained in the closed formula: it is a difference of two indicator functions, hence automatically in $\{-1,0,1\}$. The underlying reason the formula holds is a *uniqueness* statement — inside the box $0 \le i < q$, $0 \le j < p$ there is at most one lattice point $(i,j)$ with $ip + jq = m$, because two of them would force $p \mid (i - i')$ and $q \mid (j'-j)$ with both differences too small. The whole two-prime theory is a statement about integer points on a line inside a rectangle.

Something else must be doing the work in the general case, then, and this is the story of what.

## Two symmetries, and why most orders are boring

Two elementary identities turn out to control everything.

**Inflation.** If a prime $p$ already divides $n$, then
$$\Phi_{np}(X) = \Phi_n(X^{p}).$$
In coefficient terms: the coefficient of $X^k$ in $\Phi_{np}$ is the coefficient of $X^{k/p}$ in $\Phi_n$ when $p \mid k$, and $0$ otherwise. Inflation therefore does nothing to the *multiset* of nonzero coefficients — it just spreads them out, padding with zeros.

**Reflection.** For odd $n > 1$,
$$\Phi_{2n}(X) = \Phi_n(-X),$$
so the $k$-th coefficient is multiplied by $(-1)^k$ and the absolute values are untouched. (One proves this by comparing two products over the divisors of $n$: on one side $\prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_{2d} = X^n + 1$, on the other $\prod_{d \mid n}\Phi_d(-X) = -(X^n+1)$; induction on $n$ and cancellation of the common tail do the rest.)

Neither symmetry changes how big the coefficients are. Iterate them, and any order $n$ collapses down to a much smaller number: its **odd radical**
$$\operatorname{rad}_{\mathrm{odd}}(n) = \prod_{\substack{p \mid n,\ p \text{ odd prime}}} p ,$$
obtained by throwing away all factors of $2$ and all repetitions.

**Theorem (Height reduction).** *For every $n \ne 0$ and every bound $B$, the coefficients of $\Phi_n$ are bounded in absolute value by $B$ if and only if the coefficients of $\Phi_{\operatorname{rad}_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)}$ are.*

This is a genuine reduction of the whole subject. Define the **height** $H(n)$ to be the largest absolute value of a coefficient of $\Phi_n$ — equivalently, the least $B$ that bounds them all. The theorem says $H(n) = H(\operatorname{rad}_{\mathrm{odd}}(n))$: the height is blind to powers of $2$, blind to repeated prime factors, and depends only on the *set* of odd primes dividing $n$. Every question about coefficient sizes reduces to squarefree odd orders.

Now combine this with the two-parameter theorem. Call $\Phi_n$ **flat** when all its coefficients are in $\{-1,0,1\}$.

**Theorem (Flatness classification).** *If the odd part of $n$ has at most two distinct prime divisors — equivalently, if $n$ has at most two odd prime divisors — then $\Phi_n$ is flat.*

That single sentence explains the nineteenth-century illusion. The smallest number with three distinct odd prime factors is $3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 = 105$. Every single $n < 105$ has at most two odd prime divisors, so every single one of them has a flat cyclotomic polynomial. The pattern was not a coincidence; it was a theorem with a threshold, and the threshold is exactly where people stopped looking.

And the flat bound is *sharp* in a strong sense: for distinct primes $p,q$ and exponents $a,b \ge 1$, the coefficient of $X^{p^{a-1}q^{b-1}}$ in $\Phi_{p^a q^b}$ is exactly $-1$. Every one of these flat polynomials genuinely uses all three values.

## What happens at 105

At $n = 105$ the wall is hit. The polynomial $\Phi_{105}$ has degree $\varphi(105) = 48$ and equals
$$\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{105} = \;& X^{48}+X^{47}+X^{46}-X^{43}-X^{42}-2X^{41}-X^{40}-X^{39}\\
&+X^{36}+X^{35}+X^{34}+X^{33}+X^{32}+X^{31}\\
&-X^{28}-X^{26}-X^{24}-X^{22}-X^{20}\\
&+X^{17}+X^{16}+X^{15}+X^{14}+X^{13}+X^{12}\\
&-X^{9}-X^{8}-2X^{7}-X^{6}-X^{5}+X^{2}+X+1 .
\end{aligned}$$

There it is: $-2$, twice, at $X^{7}$ and at $X^{41}$ (the two positions are mirror images, as they must be, since $\Phi_n$ is palindromic for $n > 1$). Every other coefficient is $-1$, $0$, or $1$. So the height of $\Phi_{105}$ is exactly $2$: the flat world stops, but only just.

How does one *prove* an explicit formula like this rather than merely compute it? Through a cancellation identity. Grouping the divisors of $105$ according to how many of $3,5,7$ they use, the product formula $\prod_{d \mid n} \Phi_d = X^n - 1$ rearranges into
$$\Phi_{105} \cdot (X-1)(X^{15}-1)(X^{21}-1)(X^{35}-1) \;=\; (X^{3}-1)(X^{5}-1)(X^{7}-1)(X^{105}-1),$$
which is the inclusion–exclusion (Möbius) shape of $\Phi_{pqr}$. This is a polynomial identity with no cyclotomic polynomials on the right-hand side, so verifying that the candidate degree-$48$ polynomial above satisfies it — a finite algebraic expansion — plus the observation that the left factor is not a zero divisor, pins $\Phi_{105}$ down exactly.

Because the height only sees the odd radical, one $48$-degree computation settles infinitely many cases at once:

**Theorem.** *For all $a,b,c,d \ge 0$, the polynomial $\Phi_{2^{a}3^{b+1}5^{c+1}7^{d+1}}$ has height exactly $2$. In particular none of these is flat.*

## The twist: 231 is flat anyway

The obvious guess after $105$ is that three odd primes always break flatness. It does not.

Take $231 = 3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$. Its cyclotomic polynomial has degree $\varphi(231) = 120$ and every one of its $121$ coefficients lies in $\{-1,0,1\}$:
$$\begin{aligned}
\Phi_{231} = \;& 1+X+X^2-X^7-X^8-X^9-X^{11}-X^{12}-X^{13}\\
&+X^{18}+X^{19}+X^{20}+X^{21}+X^{22}+X^{23}-X^{28}-X^{29}-X^{30}-X^{32}\\
&+X^{35}+X^{39}+X^{43}-X^{45}-X^{46}-X^{49}-X^{50}\\
&+X^{52}+X^{56}+X^{60}+X^{64}+X^{68}-X^{70}-X^{71}-X^{74}-X^{75}\\
&+X^{77}+X^{81}+X^{85}-X^{88}-X^{90}-X^{91}-X^{92}\\
&+X^{97}+\cdots+X^{102}-X^{107}-X^{108}-X^{109}-X^{111}-X^{112}-X^{113}\\
&+X^{118}+X^{119}+X^{120}.
\end{aligned}$$
(The display is symmetric about $X^{60}$; the omitted middle block continues the palindrome.)

So the classification "at most two odd primes $\Rightarrow$ flat" is a genuine implication and not an equivalence. And again height reduction upgrades one computation into an infinite family: *every* $n$ whose odd radical is $231$ — that is, every $n = 2^{a}3^{b}7^{c}11^{d}$ with $b,c,d \ge 1$ — has a flat cyclotomic polynomial, entirely outside the two-odd-prime class.

Why does $231$ behave and $105$ misbehave, when both are products of three odd primes? The heuristic is the size of the *smallest* prime. Bang's classical bound says that for odd primes $p<q<r$ the coefficients of $\Phi_{pqr}$ never exceed $p-1$ in absolute value. For $105$ the smallest prime is $3$, giving room for $\pm 2$ — and the room is used. For $231$ the smallest prime is also $3$, so $\pm 2$ is *permitted* but not forced, and in fact the counting happens to stay tight. The bound is an upper limit, not a prediction.

## Three orders, three heights

If three odd primes do not determine the height, what does? Exactly the odd radical, and nothing coarser. Two more facts sharpen this.

First, **height one is exactly flatness**: since a cyclotomic polynomial is monic, it always has a coefficient equal to $1$, so no height is $0$ and $H(n) = 1$ precisely when $\Phi_n$ is flat.

Second, **the height is an invariant of the odd radical**: if $\operatorname{rad}_{\mathrm{odd}}(m) = \operatorname{rad}_{\mathrm{odd}}(n)$ then $H(m) = H(n)$; contrapositively, two orders of different height must have different odd radicals.

Now put three explicit computations side by side. All three orders are products of exactly three odd primes:

| order | factorization | degree | height |
|---|---|---|---|
| $231$ | $3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$ | $120$ | $1$ |
| $105$ | $3 \cdot 5 \cdot 7$ | $48$ | $2$ |
| $385$ | $5 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$ | $240$ | $3$ |

**Theorem (Ternary trichotomy).** *Each of $231$, $105$, $385$ has exactly three odd prime divisors, and their heights are $1$, $2$, $3$ respectively.*

The last entry is the deepest computation of the three. The polynomial $\Phi_{385}$ has degree $\varphi(385) = 240$, and its extreme coefficient is $-3$, attained three times in a row, at $X^{119}$, $X^{120}$, $X^{121}$ — right at the centre of the polynomial. It is established by the same cancellation route:
$$\Phi_{385}\cdot(X-1)(X^{35}-1)(X^{55}-1)(X^{77}-1) = (X^{5}-1)(X^{7}-1)(X^{11}-1)(X^{385}-1),$$
which determines the $241$ coefficients uniquely. And once more the height propagates: every order $2^{a}5^{b+1}7^{c+1}11^{d+1}$ has height exactly $3$.

The moral is crisp. *The number of odd prime divisors does not determine the height. The odd radical does.* Counting primes is the wrong invariant; knowing which primes is the right one.

## Back to lattice points

There is a satisfying way to see why the two-prime world is so rigid, and it also says what makes the three-prime world hard.

For $n = pq$, the coefficient formula above counts solutions of $ip + jq = m$ with $i,j \ge 0$ — lattice points on a line segment. Uniqueness inside the box $[0,q) \times [0,p)$ makes the count either $0$ or $1$, hence the coefficient is a difference of two indicators and lives in $\{-1,0,1\}$. The flat bound is not an estimate; it is a counting identity.

And this identity survives both symmetries. Inflation and reflection carry it verbatim to every order of the shape $2^{\alpha}p^{\beta+1}q^{\gamma+1}$: for $0 < k+1 < pq$,
$$\bigl[X^{(k+1)p^{\beta}q^{\gamma}2^{\alpha}}\bigr]\;\Phi_{2^{\alpha+1}p^{\beta+1}q^{\gamma+1}} \;=\; (-1)^{(k+1)p^{\beta}q^{\gamma}}\Bigl(\mathbf{1}\{k+1 \in \langle p,q\rangle\} - \mathbf{1}\{k \in \langle p,q\rangle\}\Bigr),$$
with the parity sign coming from the reflection and the index dilation from the inflation. Every coefficient of every flat two-prime-power family is a lattice-point count, on the nose.

For three primes $p < q < r$ the line becomes a plane section $ip + jq + kr = m$ inside a box, and uniqueness fails: several lattice points can appear, and their number can differ from the "expected" value. That difference is exactly the coefficient, and Bang's bound $p-1$ is the statement that the residues $jq + kr \bmod p$ spread out evenly enough over the $p$ classes that the discrepancy can never exceed $p-1$. With the box-uniqueness lemma and the transport of coefficients under inflation and reflection both established, the ternary case has become a self-contained *counting* problem — no longer a polynomial-algebra problem. That is the natural next target.

## Why anyone should care

Cyclotomic polynomials are not exotica. They are the irreducible factors of $X^n - 1$, which means they are the building blocks of every finite cyclic structure: the factorization of $X^n-1$ over a finite field controls cyclic error-correcting codes; the arithmetic of $\Phi_n$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ underlies the number fields at the heart of algebraic number theory, and — very concretely — the ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(\Phi_n)$ is the arena in which most of today's lattice-based cryptography, including post-quantum key exchange, does its arithmetic. There, coefficient sizes are not aesthetics; they are noise growth.

But the deeper appeal is the shape of the story. A pattern holds for a thousand cases and looks like a law. It fails at exactly the first order that has room to fail. The failure is not random: it is governed by a clean invariant, the odd radical, that ignores squares and ignores the prime $2$ entirely. And within a single "level" of complexity — three odd primes — the possible behaviours already fan out into at least three distinct classes, realized by $231$, $105$, and $385$.

Small numbers lie. But they lie in a structured way, and the structure is the mathematics.

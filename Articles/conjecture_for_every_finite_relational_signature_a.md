# How Far Apart Are Two Analogies?

### A ruler for approximate sameness, and why errors compound like interest rather than like debt

---

## The copycat problem

Imagine two machines. The first is a weather model for a coastal town: from *sunny* it
usually stays sunny, occasionally slips to *rainy*; from *rainy* it usually stays rainy.
The second is a model of a chemical reactor: from *stable* it usually stays stable,
occasionally tips into *runaway*; from *runaway* it usually stays runaway.

You do not need a meteorology degree to see that these are, in some sense, the same
machine wearing different clothes. Rename *sunny* to *stable* and *rainy* to *runaway*
and the two probability tables look nearly identical — nearly, but not exactly. The
weather flips with probability $0.05$; the reactor tips with probability $0.07$.

The question this article is about is deceptively simple:

> **How much can you trust a nearly-correct analogy?**

If I have reasoned carefully about the weather and I now transfer that reasoning to the
reactor, how wrong might my conclusions be? And is there a principled way to say that
*this* analogy is better than *that* one — a genuine ruler on the space of systems,
measuring how far one is from being a renamed copy of another?

Both questions turn out to have clean, complete answers. The error compounds
**geometrically**, not linearly, and the ruler exists, is always attained by a best
possible renaming, and satisfies every axiom one could want of a distance.

---

## The setting: systems that flip coins

Fix a finite set of **worlds** — call them $s, t, \dots$. A *probabilistic transition
system* consists of two pieces of data.

First, a **transition kernel**: for each pair of worlds a number $M(s,t) \ge 0$, the
probability of moving from $s$ to $t$ in one step, with $\sum_t M(s,t) = 1$ for every
$s$. This is the machine's dynamics.

Second, a **valuation**: for each atomic proposition $p$ and each world $s$, a number
$V(p,s) \in [0,1]$ — how true $p$ is at $s$. (Allowing intermediate values costs
nothing and buys generality; the classical case is $V(p,s) \in \{0,1\}$.)

What can an observer say about such a system? The natural language is *modal*: build
statements from the atoms using negation, conjunction, and one new operator, "next".
Formally, formulas are generated by
$$\varphi ::= p \mid \neg\varphi \mid \varphi \wedge \psi \mid \bigcirc\varphi .$$
Their **truth probabilities** $\llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(s)$ are computed by the
obvious rules:
$$\llbracket p \rrbracket_M(s) = V(p,s), \qquad
\llbracket \neg\varphi \rrbracket_M(s) = 1 - \llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(s), \qquad
\llbracket \varphi \wedge \psi \rrbracket_M(s) = \min\bigl(\llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(s), \llbracket \psi \rrbracket_M(s)\bigr),$$
and the interesting one,
$$\llbracket \bigcirc\varphi \rrbracket_M(s) = \sum_t M(s,t)\,\llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(t),$$
the expected truth of $\varphi$ one step from now. Every truth probability lies in
$[0,1]$, as it should.

The **depth** of a formula is the number of nested $\bigcirc$'s: how far into the future
the observer has to look. Depth is the natural currency of this story. Depth $0$
questions are about the present; depth $5$ questions require five steps of simulation,
and — as we shall see — five opportunities for a small modelling error to bite.

## Analogy, made quantitative

Now the central definition. An **$\varepsilon$-approximate structural analogy** from a
system $M$ to a system $N$ is a bijection $f$ between their worlds that

- **gets the atoms exactly right**: $V_N(p, f(s)) = V_M(p, s)$ for every atom $p$ and
  every world $s$; and
- **gets the dynamics right to within $\varepsilon$**: for every world $s$,
  $$1 - \sum_t \min\bigl(M(s,t),\; N(f(s), f(t))\bigr) \;\le\; \varepsilon .$$

That second quantity, the **overlap defect**, deserves a moment. Two probability
distributions $P$ and $Q$ overlap in the amount $\sum_t \min(P_t, Q_t)$ — the mass they
genuinely share. What is left over, $1 - \sum_t \min(P_t,Q_t)$, is the mass on which they
disagree. A short computation shows this equals
$$\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_t |P_t - Q_t|,$$
the classical **total variation distance**. So "$\varepsilon$-approximate analogy" means
exactly: *rename the worlds so that every row of the transition table is within total
variation $\varepsilon$ of its counterpart.* In our example, weather and reactor are
related by a $0.02$-approximate analogy.

When $\varepsilon = 0$ the two kernels agree entirely and the analogy is an
**isomorphism**: the systems really are renamings of each other.

---

## The transport theorem: errors compound like interest

Here is the first main result.

> **Transport Theorem.** Let $f$ be an $\varepsilon$-approximate structural analogy from
> $M$ to $N$, with $0 \le \varepsilon \le 1$. Then for every formula $\varphi$ of depth
> $d$ and every world $s$,
> $$\bigl|\, \llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(s) \;-\; \llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_N(f(s)) \,\bigr|
> \;\le\; 1 - (1-\varepsilon)^{d}.$$

The proof is an induction on the structure of $\varphi$, and its shape is instructive.
Atoms contribute nothing: the analogy preserves them exactly, and depth $0$ gives the
bound $1 - (1-\varepsilon)^0 = 0$. Negation flips a difference and leaves its size
alone. Conjunction is handled by the elementary inequality
$$|\min(a,b) - \min(a',b')| \;\le\; \max\bigl(|a-a'|,\,|b-b'|\bigr),$$
and depth of a conjunction is the max of the depths, so the bound survives.

All the content is in the modal step. Suppose we already know that $\varphi$'s truth
probabilities differ by at most $\delta$ everywhere. Comparing the two expectations
$$\sum_t M(s,t)\,g(t) \quad\text{versus}\quad \sum_t N(f(s),f(t))\,g'(t)$$
where $g$ and $g'$ are the two truth-probability vectors, split the mass into the part
the two kernels *share* and the part they do not. On the shared mass — of size at least
$1-\varepsilon$ — the two expectations differ by at most $\delta$, since the observables
themselves differ by at most $\delta$. On the disagreeing mass — of size at most
$\varepsilon$ — we can say nothing better than the trivial bound, because both
observables live in $[0,1]$: the discrepancy there is at most $1$. Averaging,
$$\delta \;\longmapsto\; \delta + \varepsilon(1-\delta).$$

That recursion is the whole theorem. Starting from $\delta_0 = 0$ and iterating $d$
times gives exactly $\delta_d = 1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d$.

Read the recursion again and notice what it says. Each extra step of lookahead does
*not* add a fresh $\varepsilon$ of error to what you already had. It adds $\varepsilon$
of the error *you did not already have*. Ignorance compounds like interest on the
remaining principal, saturating at total ignorance rather than sailing past it. The
naive guess — that $d$ nested observations should cost $d\varepsilon$ — is an
overestimate, and the overestimate is a real one.

---

## The linear bound: true, natural, and never attained

The conjecture that launched this line of work predicted a linear modulus: error at most
$d \cdot \varepsilon$. That prediction is **true as an upper bound**, since by
Bernoulli's inequality $(1-\varepsilon)^d \ge 1 - d\varepsilon$, hence
$$1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d \;\le\; d\,\varepsilon .$$

But it is **never an equality** once you look more than one step ahead. For $0 <
\varepsilon < 1$ and $d \ge 2$,
$$1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d \;<\; d\,\varepsilon$$
strictly. So the linear bound, while correct, is not the modulus of continuity; the
geometric expression is.

And yet the linear guess was not foolish. The two moduli agree to first order:
$$d\,\varepsilon - \bigl(1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d\bigr) \;\le\; \frac{d(d-1)}{2}\,\varepsilon^2 .$$
For small local error the discrepancy is quadratic, so $d\varepsilon$ is the right
*linearisation* — the correct answer to "what is the error rate per unit of lookahead,
to leading order?" — just not the right *bound*.

Is the geometric bound itself the best possible? Yes, and one two-state example settles
it. Let the **exact system** have two absorbing worlds, $\mathsf{T}$ and $\mathsf{F}$,
with the atom $a$ true at $\mathsf{T}$ and false at $\mathsf{F}$. Let the **leaky
system** be identical except that from $\mathsf{T}$ a mass $\varepsilon$ leaks
irreversibly into $\mathsf{F}$. The identity renaming is an $\varepsilon$-approximate
analogy. Now ask the depth-$d$ question $\bigcirc^d a$, "will $a$ hold in $d$ steps?".
In the exact system the answer at $\mathsf{T}$ is $1$; in the leaky system it is
$(1-\varepsilon)^d$, since the process must survive $d$ independent chances to leak.
The discrepancy is exactly
$$1 - (1-\varepsilon)^d,$$
the bound, on the nose, for every depth and every $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$. Geometric
decay is not an artefact of a lossy proof. It is what actually happens.

---

## A ruler on the space of systems

Knowing that *an* $\varepsilon$-analogy transports truth well, one naturally wants the
best one. Fix two systems $M$ and $N$ on the same finite world set and define
$$d(M,N) \;=\; \inf\,\{\, \varepsilon \;:\; \text{there exists an } \varepsilon\text{-approximate analogy from } M \text{ to } N \,\}.$$

At first sight this is a genuine infimum, with all the usual anxieties: perhaps no single
analogy achieves it. But there is a saving observation. The atomic condition is rigid —
a candidate renaming either preserves all atomic values or it does not — and there are
only finitely many renamings of a finite world set. So the infimum ranges over a *finite*
set of candidates, each with a well-defined cost
$$\mathrm{cost}(f) \;=\; \max_s\; \Bigl(1 - \sum_t \min\bigl(M(s,t), N(f(s),f(t))\bigr)\Bigr),$$
its worst-case one-step overlap defect. Hence:

> **Attainment.** The infimum is a minimum. There is an atom-preserving renaming $f^\star$
> with $\mathrm{cost}(f^\star) = d(M,N)$, and it is an honest $d(M,N)$-approximate
> structural analogy. Consequently, for every $\varepsilon$,
> $$\text{an } \varepsilon\text{-approximate analogy exists} \iff d(M,N) \le \varepsilon .$$

So $d(M,N)$ is not merely a lower bound on achievable defects: it is the *least
achievable defect*, realised by a specific optimal renaming.

That the quantity behaves like a distance follows from three structural facts about
approximate analogies, each with a one-line reason.

- **Identity.** The identity renaming is a $0$-analogy from $M$ to itself, so
  $d(M,M) = 0$.
- **Inverses.** If $f$ is an $\varepsilon$-analogy from $M$ to $N$, then $f^{-1}$ is an
  $\varepsilon$-analogy from $N$ to $M$ — the overlap defect is symmetric in its two
  arguments. Hence $d(M,N) = d(N,M)$.
- **Composition.** If $f$ is an $\varepsilon_1$-analogy from $M$ to $N$ and $g$ is an
  $\varepsilon_2$-analogy from $N$ to $K$, then $g \circ f$ is an
  $(\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_2)$-analogy from $M$ to $K$, because total variation obeys
  the triangle inequality. Hence $d(M,K) \le d(M,N) + d(N,K)$.

In other words, approximate analogies form a **groupoid graded by total variation** —
arrows compose, invert, and carry additive weights — and the analogy distance is exactly
the induced least-weight function. The metric axioms are not proved by hand; they are
read off from the algebra of analogies.

One axiom remains, and it is the one that says the ruler measures something real.

> **The zero set is isomorphism.** $d(M,N) = 0$ if and only if there is an
> atom-preserving renaming carrying the kernel of $M$ exactly onto the kernel of $N$.

Both directions are short. An isomorphism is a $0$-analogy, so it forces the distance to
vanish. Conversely, if $d(M,N)=0$ then the optimal renaming has zero overlap defect at
every world, and two probability vectors with zero total variation distance are equal —
so the optimal renaming *is* an isomorphism. Therefore $d$ is a genuine metric on
isomorphism classes: it separates points, and the points it fails to separate are
precisely the systems that were the same all along. Together with $0 \le d(M,N) \le 1$,
we have a bounded metric space of finite probabilistic systems modulo renaming.

## Continuity, with the optimal constant

Now combine the two halves. Take the optimal renaming $f^\star$ and feed it to the
transport theorem:
$$\bigl|\, \llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_M(s) - \llbracket \varphi \rrbracket_N(f^\star(s)) \,\bigr|
\;\le\; 1 - \bigl(1 - d(M,N)\bigr)^{\operatorname{depth}\varphi}.$$

This says the map
$$\text{system} \;\longmapsto\; \text{its vector of depth-}d\text{ truth probabilities}$$
is uniformly continuous with respect to the analogy metric, with modulus
$\omega_d(x) = 1-(1-x)^d$. And the constant cannot be improved, because the extremal
leaking family computes the distance exactly: for every $\varepsilon \in [0,1]$,
$$d(\text{exact}, \text{leaky}(\varepsilon)) = \varepsilon .$$
(The atomic valuation pins the renaming to the identity — no clever relabelling of two
worlds with different atomic values is available — and the worst-case one-step defect is
the leak itself.) Since that same family attains $1-(1-\varepsilon)^d$ at depth $d$, the
modulus is achieved. It *is* the modulus of continuity, not merely an upper envelope for
one.

## Networks of analogies, and the holonomy of meaning

Real analogical reasoning rarely proceeds along a single arrow. One maps an epidemic onto
a rumour, the rumour onto a forest fire, and the forest fire back onto the epidemic. Each
hop is approximate. Does the composite make sense?

Composition of analogies adds defects, so along a chain
$M_0 \to M_1 \to \cdots \to M_k$ with local defects $\varepsilon_0, \dots,
\varepsilon_{k-1}$ the composite is an analogy of defect $\sum_{i<k}\varepsilon_i$, and
depth-$d$ truth probabilities are transported with error at most
$$1 - \Bigl(1 - \sum_{i<k}\varepsilon_i\Bigr)^{d}.$$
The geometric modulus of the single-arrow theorem persists at network level, with the
*sum* of the local defects as the effective one.

The interesting case is a **cycle**, where $M_k = M_0$. The composite is then a
permutation of the worlds of $M_0$, and it need not be the identity. The displacement it
induces on truth probabilities is the **semantic holonomy** of the loop — how much your
meanings have drifted by the time the argument comes home. The bound above becomes a
holonomy bound; and when every arrow of the loop is exact, the bound is zero:

> **Exact cycles have trivial holonomy.** If every analogy in a closed loop is exact,
> then every modal observation returns to its original value after traversing the loop.
> Consequently, any interpretation that depends only on a world's modal theory is
> globally well-defined around the loop.

That last sentence is the coherence statement the machinery was built to express. An
assignment of *meaning* to worlds — anything that respects modal observation — can be
transported consistently around a network of exact analogies. When the analogies are
merely approximate, the ambiguity created by a loop is bounded, quantitatively, by the
accumulated defect. And the bound is not vacuous: a two-arrow loop with maximal defect
$1$ can send a depth-one observation from probability $1$ to probability $0$.

## How much observation does it take?

A last question, dual to everything above. Transport says that structural analogy
implies observational agreement. Does observational agreement imply structural analogy?

In general, **no** — and the counterexample is embarrassingly small. Take two worlds and
an atom true at both. In the first system each world has a self-loop; in the second the
two worlds swap. Every modal formula, at every depth, has the same truth probability in
both systems: the atom is certainly true everywhere, so everything downstream is forced,
and no observation can tell a self-loop from a $2$-cycle. Yet no renaming of two worlds
turns two self-loops into a $2$-cycle: the analogy distance between them is $1$, its
maximum. Truth-probability equivalence is strictly coarser than structural analogy, so
the transport theorem has no converse.

One might hope to close the gap by counting: add graded modalities, "at least $k$
successors satisfy $\varphi$". This does not work. On *deterministic* systems with
constant atoms, every graded formula has the same truth value at every world of every
such system: each world has exactly one successor, so all counting questions collapse to
the same answers regardless of where the arrows point. The self-loop/swap pair is
deterministic, so grading leaves it untouched. What *does* separate the pair is a
self-referential observation — "is the current world a possible successor of itself?" — a
fixed-point property, invariant under exact analogies but invisible to counting.

The gap closes if the language can name worlds. Call a system **nominal** if it has one
atom per world, true exactly there. Then the naming map is forced: any analogy must send
each world to the world with the same name, so there is no freedom in the renaming at
all. And depth-one observations suffice, quantitatively:

> **Approximate Hennessy–Milner Theorem.** If two nominal systems on $n$ worlds have all
> depth-one truth probabilities within $\eta$ of each other, then the naming map is an
> $(n\eta/2)$-approximate structural analogy — hence all depth-$d$ truth probabilities
> agree to within $1 - (1 - n\eta/2)^d$.

The factor $n/2$ is the price of converting a worst-case *entrywise* bound on
observations into a *total variation* bound on kernels: $n$ entries, each off by up to
$\eta$, in a distance that is half an $\ell^1$ norm. And that price is exactly right. For
every even $n = 2m$ there are two nominal systems — one with the uniform kernel, one in
which mass $\eta$ has been shifted from each "negative" world to a "positive" one —
whose depth-one observations differ by exactly $\eta$ at every single pair, and between
which no $\varepsilon$-approximate analogy exists with $\varepsilon < n\eta/2$. Recovery
of a probabilistic structure from depth-one observation is Lipschitz, with Lipschitz
constant exactly $n/2$.

## What it all adds up to

Three lessons, each of which outruns the toy setting that produced it.

**Approximation error in iterated reasoning saturates.** Chaining $d$ inferences through
a model that is $\varepsilon$ wrong costs you $1-(1-\varepsilon)^d$, not $d\varepsilon$.
The distinction is invisible for small $d\varepsilon$ and decisive for large: the linear
estimate happily predicts errors above $1$, while the truth is that error approaches
total ignorance and stops.

**"Almost the same system" is a metric notion, and the metric is computable.** The
analogy distance is a minimum over finitely many renamings, satisfies the metric axioms
because approximate analogies form a graded groupoid, and vanishes exactly on isomorphic
pairs. It is computed by examining the candidate renamings and taking the best worst-case
row defect — expensive in the worst case, but with the atomic condition typically pruning
the search drastically, and, in the nominal case, to a single candidate.

**Observation and structure are genuinely different resolutions of sameness.** Which
notion of "the same" you get depends on which questions you are allowed to ask; the
analogy metric measures structural sameness, modal truth measures behavioural sameness,
and the gap between them is exactly the content of the self-loop-versus-swap example.

Analogy, on this account, is not a vague literary gesture. It is an arrow in a groupoid,
weighted by total variation, transporting truth with a geometric loss, obstructed around
loops by a holonomy, and organising the space of systems into a bounded metric space
whose zero set is isomorphism. That is a lot of structure for an idea usually filed
under intuition.

# When Causal Questions Become a Finite Logic

## From “what happened?” to “what would happen if?”

A hospital database can tell us that patients who received a treatment recovered more often. It cannot, by that fact alone, tell us whether giving the treatment caused the improvement. Perhaps healthier patients were more likely to receive it. Perhaps physicians reserved it for milder cases. The gap between seeing and doing is the central difficulty of causal inference.

Causal notation makes that gap visible. An ordinary conditional probability such as $P(Y\mid X=x)$ describes what is observed among cases with $X=x$. An interventional probability such as $P(Y\mid \operatorname{do}(X=x))$ asks what would happen if an external action forced $X$ to equal $x$. The two expressions may coincide, but they need not. Causal identification is the art of deciding whether an interventional quantity can be transformed into something determined by available observations.

This article develops a deliberately finite and logical view of that problem. Fix a finite set of variables. A causal query records three pairwise disjoint sets: the variables whose distribution is sought, the variables merely observed, and the variables actively intervened upon. Three graphical tests authorize the elementary transformations familiar from do-calculus. We then ask a sharply defined question: which equalities between queries are forced in *every* interpretation that treats equality consistently and accepts those three transformations?

The answer is exact. An equality is universally valid precisely when it can be obtained by a finite chain of authorized steps, reversals, and compositions. Because the universe of well-formed queries is finite, this also yields a terminating decision procedure. The result is a clean meeting point of causal reasoning, finite logic, and computation.

## The atoms of the language

Suppose the variables are indexed by the finite set $V=\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}$. A **causal query** is a triple

$$
q=(T,O,I),
$$

where $T,O,I\subseteq V$ are pairwise disjoint. Its intended reading is

$$
P(T\mid O,\operatorname{do}(I)).
$$

The set $T$ contains targets, $O$ contains ordinary observations, and $I$ contains interventions. Pairwise disjointness prevents a variable from being asked to play two incompatible roles at once.

A causal graph accompanies these queries. Rather than building one particular graph-separation routine into the logical system, we use a **graphical oracle**. For each ordered pair of queries $(q,r)$, it provides three Boolean answers, one for each rule schema:

1. **Observation insertion or deletion:** may an observed condition be inserted or removed?
2. **Action–observation exchange:** may an intervention be exchanged with an observation, or conversely?
3. **Action insertion or deletion:** may an intervention be inserted or removed?

In an applied implementation, these answers would normally be computed from suitable d-separation statements in appropriately modified causal graphs. Here they are inputs to the logic. This modular choice matters: it isolates the consequences of the rule system from the engineering and adequacy of a particular graphical test.

A **primitive step** from $q$ to $r$ is licensed when at least one of the three oracle tests returns true. A **derivation** is then built in four ways. Every query derives itself; every primitive step is a derivation; every derivation may be reversed; and two derivations may be joined end to end. Thus derivability is the smallest equivalence relation containing every oracle-approved primitive pair.

Picture all well-formed queries as vertices of a graph. Draw an edge whenever a primitive rule connects two queries, ignoring direction because reversal is allowed. Two queries are derivably equal exactly when they lie in the same connected component. This simple picture is the computational heart of the theory.

## What does “valid in every model” mean?

To discuss soundness and completeness, syntax needs semantics. Here a **non-parametric relational interpretation** assigns a proposition $q\equiv r$ to every pair of queries. It must satisfy four requirements:

- reflexivity: $q\equiv q$;
- symmetry: if $q\equiv r$, then $r\equiv q$;
- transitivity: if $q\equiv r$ and $r\equiv s$, then $q\equiv s$;
- rule validity: every primitive oracle-approved step $q\to r$ satisfies $q\equiv r$.

No numerical distribution, functional equation, linearity assumption, or parametric family appears in this definition. The semantics deliberately captures exactly the relational content of the three-rule theory. An equality between $q$ and $r$ is called **semantically identifiable** when $q\equiv r$ holds in every such interpretation.

This scope should be read carefully. The framework proves completeness for the abstract relational semantics generated by the three rule schemata. Connecting a concrete d-separation implementation to structural-equation models is a further bridge: one must prove that the oracle’s tests are graphically adequate and that each licensed step preserves the relevant interventional distributions.

## The soundness theorem

**Soundness Theorem.** *If a query $q$ derives a query $r$, then $q\equiv r$ in every non-parametric relational interpretation validating the three primitive rules.*

The proof follows the construction of a derivation. A query is equal to itself by reflexivity. A primitive step is valid by the rule-validity requirement. Reversing a derivation uses symmetry. Joining two derivations uses transitivity. These four cases exhaust every possible derivation.

Soundness is the “no false positives” guarantee. Once an equality has a derivation, no admissible interpretation can distinguish its endpoints.

## The canonical interpretation and completeness

Completeness is subtler. Why should universal validity imply that a derivation exists? The key is to let the syntax interpret itself.

Define the **canonical interpretation** by declaring

$$
q\equiv_{\mathrm{can}} r
\quad\text{exactly when}\quad
q\text{ derives }r.
$$

This is a legitimate relational interpretation. Reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity are built into derivability, and every primitive step is included by definition.

**Completeness Theorem.** *If $q\equiv r$ in every non-parametric relational interpretation validating the primitive rules, then $q$ derives $r$.*

Indeed, an equality holding in every interpretation must hold in the canonical one. But equality in the canonical interpretation *means* derivability. The conclusion follows immediately.

Combining the two directions gives the central result.

**Soundness-and-Completeness Theorem.** *For any finite graphical oracle and any two well-formed queries $q$ and $r$, the following are equivalent:*

$$
q\text{ and }r\text{ are equal in every admissible interpretation}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\text{ derives }r.
$$

The theorem says that the finite proof system loses nothing relative to its stated semantics. There are no derivable equalities that fail semantically, and no universally valid equalities that escape derivation.

## Turning the theorem into a decision procedure

Finiteness now pays a computational dividend. For $n$ variables, each variable has four possible statuses: target, observed, intervened, or absent. Because the roles are disjoint, there are exactly $4^n$ well-formed queries.

Consider any Boolean relation $R(q,r)$. Call $R$ a **Boolean theory** when it is reflexive, symmetric, transitive, and contains every pair approved by each of the three primitive-rule tests. Since there are finitely many queries, there are finitely many such candidate relations.

The exhaustive identification procedure accepts a pair $(q,r)$ exactly when

$$
R(q,r)=\mathrm{true}
$$

for every Boolean theory $R$. This is specification-first computation: it searches a vast finite space, but its meaning is exceptionally transparent.

**Boolean-Theory Characterization.** *A candidate Boolean relation is accepted as a theory exactly when it satisfies reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and closure under each of the three oracle relations.*

**Decision Soundness Theorem.** *If the exhaustive procedure accepts $(q,r)$, then $q\equiv r$ in every admissible relational interpretation.*

To see why, take any admissible interpretation and encode its equality predicate as a Boolean table. Its axioms make that table a Boolean theory. Acceptance says every such table relates $q$ and $r$, so this one does too.

**Decision Completeness Theorem.** *If $q\equiv r$ in every admissible relational interpretation, then the exhaustive procedure accepts $(q,r)$.*

Every Boolean theory itself defines an admissible interpretation by reading “true” as equality. Universal semantic validity therefore forces every Boolean theory to relate $q$ and $r$.

Together these yield the certified decision theorem:

$$
\text{the procedure accepts }(q,r)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\equiv r\text{ in every admissible interpretation}.
$$

Combining this with soundness and completeness gives an equally useful operational form:

$$
\text{the procedure accepts }(q,r)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\text{ derives }r.
$$

## A small example

Take three abstract queries $q_0,q_1,q_2$. Suppose the observation rule licenses $q_0$ with $q_1$, the action–observation exchange rule licenses $q_1$ with $q_2$, and the third rule licenses nothing new. Then reflexivity gives $q_i\equiv q_i$. Symmetry lets us travel backward across either edge. Transitivity joins the two steps, proving $q_0\equiv q_2$.

Now imagine a fourth query $q_3$ with no approved edge to the first component. There is a Boolean theory with two equivalence classes, $\{q_0,q_1,q_2\}$ and $\{q_3\}$. This theory validates all primitive steps but separates $q_0$ from $q_3$. Therefore their equality is not semantically forced, and the exhaustive procedure rejects it. A negative answer comes with a countermodel: a lawful equivalence relation that keeps the queries apart.

## Why this perspective matters

The logical viewpoint clarifies three distinct obligations that are often blurred together.

First comes **graphical adequacy**: do the oracle’s Boolean tests exactly represent the intended d-separation conditions? Second comes **causal soundness**: do those graphically licensed transformations preserve interventional kernels in the chosen class of structural models? Third comes **logical closure**: once the primitive transformations are accepted, what follows from combining them as equalities?

The present theory settles the third question exactly. That separation is useful in software design, mathematical auditing, and scientific communication. If an identification pipeline gives a surprising answer, one can ask whether the graph test was wrong, whether the causal assumptions were inappropriate, or whether the logical chaining was incorrect. A modular architecture makes those possibilities distinguishable.

The exhaustive algorithm is not intended as the fastest practical solver. With $N=4^n$ queries, a relation has $N^2$ Boolean entries, so enumerating all relations is doubly exponential in the number of variables. A connected-component or work-list algorithm is far more attractive: generate all primitive neighbors, explore the component of $q$, and test whether $r$ is reached. Its value here is conceptual. Exhaustive quantification mirrors the semantic phrase “every admissible interpretation” so directly that correctness becomes almost unavoidable.

## The road ahead

Several bridges remain to turn this finite logical core into a full causal-identification engine. The oracle should be implemented using moralized ancestral graphs and checked against d-separation. Primitive steps should be connected to finite-valued acyclic structural-equation models with independent exogenous variables. An optimized work-list should return not only yes or no, but also a chain of rule applications as a certificate. Finally, normalization rules from probability calculus and recursive identification methods can enrich the language beyond pure query equality.

Yet the central lesson already stands: a causal transformation system can be understood as a finite logic. Its models are equivalence relations respecting three kinds of licensed move. Its canonical model is derivability itself. Its universal truths are exactly its finite proofs. And because the query universe is finite, those truths are decidable.

That is a modest but powerful change of viewpoint. It turns the elusive question “Can this causal expression be identified?” into a precise journey through a finite landscape—one in which every accepted destination has a path, and every unreachable destination can be separated by a lawful map.

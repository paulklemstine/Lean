# How Addition Forces Sets to Grow

## A sharp barrier for hiding many-fold sumsets inside a finite interval

Imagine choosing several finite collections of integers and then adding one number from each collection. Even if the original collections are small, the family of possible totals spreads. Addition creates expansion, and expansion creates an obstruction: a short interval simply cannot hold the resulting sumset once every input collection is too large.

This elementary tension lies at the heart of a broader question in additive combinatorics. How dense can a set of integers be while still avoiding a large additive pattern? The long-term target is logarithmic: given a density $0<\delta<1$ and a number of summands $t\ge 2$, can one construct dense subsets of long intervals that contain no $t$-fold sumset whose component sets are all larger than a threshold of order

$$
\frac{\log n}{\bigl(\log(1/\delta)\bigr)^{1/(t-1)}}?
$$

That question remains open in the present setting. What can be established exactly is the deterministic barrier that every such construction must confront. It is sharp, transparent, and already strong enough to produce explicit avoidance examples at a linear scale.

## What is a many-fold sumset?

For finite nonempty sets of integers $A_1,\ldots,A_t$, their $t$-fold sumset is

$$
A_1+\cdots+A_t
=
\{a_1+\cdots+a_t : a_i\in A_i\text{ for every }i\}.
$$

This construction records every total obtainable by independently choosing one element from each set. For example, if $A=\{0,2\}$ and $B=\{1,4\}$, then

$$
A+B=\{1,3,4,6\}.
$$

There are four choices of ordered pairs and, in this example, four distinct sums. Collisions can occur: different choices may produce the same total. The remarkable fact is that collisions can never compress a sumset beyond a precise minimum.

For two finite nonempty sets of integers, the basic growth inequality is

$$
|A+B|\ge |A|+|B|-1.
$$

Applying this repeatedly gives the many-fold form

$$
|A_1+\cdots+A_t|
\ge
\sum_{i=1}^t |A_i|-t+1.
$$

If every set has at least $k$ elements, this simplifies to

$$
|A_1+\cdots+A_t|\ge t(k-1)+1.
$$

Why should the two-set inequality be true? List the elements in increasing order,

$$
a_1<\cdots<a_r,
\qquad
b_1<\cdots<b_s.
$$

Then the chain

$$
a_1+b_1<a_2+b_1<\cdots<a_r+b_1<a_r+b_2<\cdots<a_r+b_s
$$

contains $r+s-1$ distinct sums. No information about the gaps is needed. Order alone forces growth.

The bound is sharp. If all the sets are arithmetic progressions with the same step, then the sumset is another arithmetic progression and has exactly

$$
\sum_{i=1}^t |A_i|-t+1
$$

elements. Thus no universal argument can improve the cardinality bound.

## The finite-container obstruction

Write

$$
[n]=\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}.
$$

This interval contains exactly $n$ integers. Suppose it contains an entire sumset $A_1+\cdots+A_t$, where all $A_i$ are nonempty and satisfy $|A_i|\ge k$. Since a subset cannot be larger than its container,

$$
|A_1+\cdots+A_t|\le n.
$$

Combining this with forced growth gives the central theorem.

**Finite-Container Theorem.** If $A_1,\ldots,A_t$ are finite nonempty sets of integers, each with at least $k$ elements, and

$$
A_1+\cdots+A_t\subseteq[n],
$$

then

$$
t(k-1)+1\le n.
$$

The proof is only one line once the growth inequality is available:

$$
t(k-1)+1
\le |A_1+\cdots+A_t|
\le |[n]|=n.
$$

Its contrapositive is the useful avoidance principle.

**Linear-Threshold Avoidance Theorem.** If

$$
n\le t(k-1),
$$

then $[n]$ contains no sumset $A_1+\cdots+A_t$ formed from nonempty finite integer sets with $|A_i|\ge k$ for every $i$.

This says that an interval can be completely full and still avoid sumsets with sufficiently large components. The avoidance is not caused by sparsity, randomness, or a delicate arrangement of holes. It follows from an absolute shortage of room.

## Density without sacrifice

A subset $S\subseteq[n]$ has density at least $\delta$ when

$$
|S|\ge \delta n.
$$

For any $0\le\delta\le1$, choose the densest possible set: $S=[n]$. Then $|S|=n\ge\delta n$. If $n\le t(k-1)$, the linear-threshold theorem shows that this same set avoids every qualifying $t$-fold sumset.

**Dense Linear-Scale Existence Theorem.** Let $0\le\delta\le1$, and let $n,t,k$ be nonnegative integers. If

$$
n\le t(k-1),
$$

then there exists $S\subseteq[n]$ with $|S|=n$ and hence $|S|\ge\delta n$, such that no nonempty finite sets $A_1,\ldots,A_t$ satisfying $|A_i|\ge k$ have

$$
A_1+\cdots+A_t\subseteq S.
$$

The witness is simply $S=[n]$.

At first glance this may look paradoxical. How can the whole interval avoid an additive configuration? The key is that the forbidden configuration is not a single equation, such as $x+y=z$. It is the entire Cartesian cloud of sums generated by several large sets. A full interval contains many individual additive relations, but it may still be too short to contain all totals from large independent choices.

## Two concrete cases

Take $t=2$, $k=3$, and $n=3$. Any two nonempty sets $A$ and $B$ with at least three elements each satisfy

$$
|A+B|\ge3+3-1=5.
$$

Therefore their sumset cannot fit inside $[3]=\{0,1,2\}$, which has only three elements.

Now take $t=3$, $k=2$, and again $n=3$. For three sets $A,B,C$, each with at least two elements,

$$
|A+B+C|\ge2+2+2-3+1=4.
$$

So $A+B+C$ cannot fit inside the same three-point interval.

These examples expose the arithmetic of the barrier. The critical quantity is not merely $tk$, but $t(k-1)+1$. Each new nonempty summand forces at least $|A_i|-1$ new positions beyond a starting point.

## The logarithmic frontier

The linear theorem becomes effective when $k$ is roughly $n/t$. The deeper goal asks whether carefully selected density-$\delta$ subsets can avoid sumsets with dramatically smaller components, of logarithmic rather than linear size.

For fixed $t\ge2$ and $0<\delta<1$, the logarithmic-scale avoidance statement asks whether there exist constants $C>0$ and $N$ such that, for every $n\ge N$, one can choose $S\subseteq[n]$ with

$$
|S|\ge\delta n
$$

and with no containment

$$
A_1+\cdots+A_t\subseteq S
$$

whenever all $A_i$ are nonempty and

$$
|A_i|\ge
C\,
\frac{\log n}
{\bigl(\log(1/\delta)\bigr)^{1/(t-1)}}.
$$

This is a genuine strengthening, not a reformulation of the linear result. Cardinality alone cannot rule out such a sumset: a logarithmic-sized sumset can easily fit inside an interval of length $n$. Any proof at that scale must exploit the internal structure and abundance of possible sumsets, together with a construction that blocks all of them simultaneously.

One natural idea is random selection. Keep each point of $[n]$ independently with probability $\delta$. A fixed finite pattern $T$ is contained with probability $\delta^{|T|}$. Since a $t$-fold sumset generated by $k$-element sets has at least $t(k-1)+1$ elements, larger patterns are individually unlikely to survive. But there may be enormously many candidate sumsets. The challenge is therefore an enumeration problem: count the possible containers sharply enough that the small survival probabilities win after summing over all candidates.

This leads to several concrete directions. One may first study repeated sumsets $tA=A+\cdots+A$, where all summands agree. One may seek an upper bound of the form

$$
\exp\!\left(\frac{K_t n}{k^{t-1}}\right)
$$

for the number of relevant $t$-fold sumsets inside $[n]$. One may ask whether random density-$\delta$ sets already achieve the predicted threshold. Finally, one may seek a matching converse: perhaps every sufficiently dense set must contain a sumset with components at least a constant multiple of the same logarithmic scale.

## A geometric picture of additive expansion

There is another way to see why the formula $t(k-1)+1$ is natural. Imagine each set $A_i$ as offering $k$ or more positions on a number line. Begin with one possible total. Moving through the ordered choices in one summand creates at least $k-1$ strictly new totals. Doing this for each of the $t$ summands contributes at least $t(k-1)$ moves beyond the initial total. The sumset therefore occupies at least one starting point plus those forced moves.

For aligned intervals, this picture is literal. If

$$
A_i=\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
$$

for every $i$, then every integer from $0$ through $t(k-1)$ occurs, and no others do. The sumset is exactly

$$
\{0,1,\ldots,t(k-1)\},
$$

with $t(k-1)+1$ points. Thus an interval of that length can contain the pattern perfectly, while an interval one point shorter cannot contain any pattern with the same minimum part sizes. This one-point transition is the sharp edge of the theorem.

The argument also adapts immediately when the summands have different sizes. If $|A_i|=k_i$, then the forced lower bound is

$$
1+\sum_{i=1}^t(k_i-1).
$$

For instance, sets of sizes $2$, $5$, and $7$ generate at least $12$ distinct sums. This nonuniform form is useful in computation: one can reject a proposed containment before inspecting the actual elements whenever the forced count exceeds the container size.

## Why the barrier matters

Extremal mathematics often advances by locating the first wall precisely. Here the wall is the inequality

$$
t(k-1)+1\le n.
$$

Below it, avoidance is automatic, even at density one. Above it, cardinality no longer decides the question, and structure takes over. The sharpness of the growth inequality tells us exactly where elementary size arguments stop.

That separation is valuable. It prevents a logarithmic conjecture from being confused with a simple counting consequence, identifies the scale gap that future constructions must bridge, and supplies exact finite tests for experiments. In a numerical search, the barrier can be checked before a single candidate set is generated: compute $t(k-1)+1$, compare it with $n$, and discard the entire parameter range if the forced size is too large. At the exact boundary, aligned intervals provide a positive control, confirming that the test has not thrown away feasible cases.

The wider lesson reaches beyond this problem. Dense objects need not contain every large structured pattern; what matters is how the pattern expands and how much of it must appear simultaneously. Here a simple ordered chain turns that intuition into an exact theorem. Addition inevitably expands finite sets. The open problem is whether ingenuity can turn that expansion into a dense set that hides from every large additive cloud at once.

# The Shape of "Smaller Than": A Field Guide to Daisy Cubes and Their Forbidden Patterns

## A puzzle made of switches

Imagine a control panel with $n$ light switches. Each switch is either *up* or *down*, so the panel as a whole has $2^n$ possible configurations. Now draw a dot for every configuration, and connect two dots whenever you can get from one to the other by flipping a single switch. The picture you have drawn is one of the most beloved objects in combinatorics: the **hypercube** $Q_n$.

For $n = 1$ the hypercube is just two dots joined by an edge. For $n = 2$ it is a square. For $n = 3$ it is the familiar wireframe cube. Beyond that it stops fitting in our three-dimensional intuition, but the rule never changes: dots are configurations, edges are single flips, and the distance between two configurations is simply the number of switches you must toggle to turn one into the other.

This last quantity — the number of disagreeing switches — is called the **Hamming distance**, and it is the silent hero of this story. It is the distance that error-correcting codes minimize, that geneticists use to compare DNA strings, and that computer scientists invoke whenever they reason about bit-vectors. If we represent a configuration as the *set* of switches that are up, then the Hamming distance between two sets $A$ and $B$ is the size of their **symmetric difference** $A \triangle B$ — the switches that disagree:

$$d(A, B) = |A \triangle B|.$$

Our subject is a special family of shapes that live inside the hypercube. They are called **daisy cubes**, and they turn out to have a hidden, almost botanical regularity — together with a precise catalogue of the patterns they are forbidden to contain.

## Down-closed worlds

Pick a configuration and call it the **origin**: the state where every switch is down, the empty set $\emptyset$. From the origin, "smaller" means "fewer switches up." A collection $D$ of configurations is called **down-closed** if it is stable under turning switches off: whenever a configuration $A$ belongs to $D$, every configuration you can reach by flipping some of $A$'s up-switches *down* also belongs to $D$. In set language:

$$\text{if } A \in D \text{ and } B \subseteq A, \text{ then } B \in D.$$

A **daisy cube** is precisely the subgraph of the hypercube carved out by a down-closed set of configurations. The name is evocative: if you choose a handful of "maximal" configurations $x_1, x_2, \dots$ and take *everything below them* — every configuration obtainable by switching some of their lights off — the resulting shape looks like a bouquet of overlapping intervals all rooted at the same origin. Each interval $[\emptyset, x_i]$ is one petal; the origin is the common center; the whole flower is the daisy cube generated by the $x_i$.

This dual description — "down-closed set" on one hand, "union of petals from the origin" on the other — is not a coincidence. It is a theorem, and it is the first cornerstone of our story.

### The down-closure machine

Given *any* collection $X$ of configurations, define its **down-closure** to be every configuration that sits below at least one member of $X$:

$$\mathrm{dc}(X) = \{ A : \text{there exists } C \in X \text{ with } A \subseteq C \}.$$

Three facts make this operator the engine of the whole theory.

1. **The down-closure of anything is a daisy cube.** Pile up petals however you like, and the union is automatically down-closed.
2. **It is the *smallest* daisy cube containing $X$.** Any daisy cube that swallows $X$ must, by down-closedness, also swallow everything below $X$ — that is, all of $\mathrm{dc}(X)$.
3. **A set is a daisy cube exactly when it equals its own down-closure.** In the language of order theory, daisy cubes are the *fixed points* of the down-closure operator.

That third statement deserves a moment. It says that "being a daisy cube" is not an accident of how a shape was drawn; it is an intrinsic, checkable property — a shape is a daisy cube if and only if applying the petal-generating machine to it gives nothing new. Formally:

$$D \text{ is a daisy cube} \iff \mathrm{dc}(D) \subseteq D.$$

This is the kind of clean characterization mathematicians treasure, because it converts a geometric question ("does this shape sit nicely inside the hypercube?") into a purely combinatorial one ("is this family of sets down-closed?").

## A lattice of flowers

Daisy cubes do not live in isolation; they interact beautifully.

- **Intersection.** Overlap two daisy cubes and the result is again a daisy cube. (Both halves of a configuration's "ancestry" survive the overlap.)
- **Union.** Glue two daisy cubes together and you again get a daisy cube.
- **Arbitrary intersection.** Even infinitely many daisy cubes, intersected all at once, yield a daisy cube.

Because they are closed under both meet (intersection) and join (union), daisy cubes form a **lattice** — an algebraic structure in which any two elements have a greatest common lower bound and a least common upper bound. Two extreme members anchor the lattice: the tiny daisy cube $\{\emptyset\}$ consisting of the origin alone, and the gigantic daisy cube consisting of the *entire* hypercube. And every nonempty daisy cube, no matter how exotic, must contain the origin — a flower always has its center.

## The geometry of the meet

So far the story has been about order: what is below what. The magic happens when order meets geometry.

Take any two configurations $A$ and $B$ inside a daisy cube. Their **meet** $A \cap B$ — the set of switches that are up in *both* — is also in the daisy cube (turn off the switches that disagree, and down-closedness guarantees you land somewhere legal). But here is the striking part: the meet is not just *present*; it is *perfectly positioned*. It lies exactly on a shortest path — a **geodesic** — between $A$ and $B$:

$$d(A, B) = d(A,\, A \cap B) + d(A \cap B,\, B).$$

In words: to walk from $A$ to $B$ along the most efficient route, you may first descend to their common meet (switching off everything they disagree about that is up in $A$) and then climb back up (switching on everything up in $B$ that you had turned off). No detours, no wasted moves. The meet is a natural **gate** through which the shortest path passes.

This is why daisy cubes are not merely down-closed sets but *isometric* subgraphs of the hypercube — what graph theorists call **partial cubes**. Distances measured inside a daisy cube agree exactly with distances in the ambient hypercube. The meet-gate is the geometric reason. A shape with a built-in, distance-respecting "common ancestor" for every pair of points is a shape with no metric distortion.

## The crack in the symmetry

If meets behave so perfectly, what about **joins**? Given $A$ and $B$, their join $A \cup B$ is the configuration with every switch that is up in *either*. One might hope daisy cubes are closed under joins too. They are not — and this single asymmetry is the source of all the richness that follows.

Here is the smallest possible counterexample. In the two-switch hypercube $Q_2$, take the daisy cube consisting of exactly three configurations:

$$\{\ \emptyset,\ \{1\},\ \{2\}\ \}.$$

This is down-closed: the only things below $\{1\}$ are $\{1\}$ and $\emptyset$; likewise for $\{2\}$. So it is a genuine daisy cube — in fact it is a path on three vertices, the graph $P_3$. But the join of $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ is $\{1,2\}$, the configuration with *both* switches up — and that configuration is **not** in our set. Daisy cubes are meet-closed but not join-closed.

This little three-vertex path, $P_3$, is the seed of everything that comes next. It is the simplest shape that "bends" — it has a corner where two directions meet without their join being present. And it is exactly this bending that the forbidden-pattern theory is built to detect.

## Minors: zooming out on a graph

To state the central result we need one more idea: a way of saying that one graph "contains the pattern of" another, even after some simplification. For ordinary graphs this is the celebrated theory of *minors* (the backdrop of the Robertson–Seymour theorem). For partial cubes there is a refined version, the **partial-cube minor** (or **pc-minor**), tailored to respect the hypercube structure.

The key operation is **contraction along a Θ-class**. In a partial cube, the edges split into natural parallel families called **Θ-classes** — think of all the edges that "flip the same switch." Contracting a Θ-class means collapsing that switch entirely: you slide the two halves of the graph together, identifying configurations that differed only in that one coordinate. The result is again a partial cube, one dimension smaller. By repeatedly contracting Θ-classes (and passing to convex pieces), you obtain the **pc-minors** of a graph — its structural shadows in lower dimensions.

A Θ-class is called **peripheral** when one of the two halves it separates is, in a precise sense, an "outer layer" — every vertex on that side has a partner directly across the divide. Daisy cubes are exactly the partial cubes that can be peeled, peripheral layer by peripheral layer, all the way down to the origin. A **non-peripheral** Θ-class is a divider that *cannot* be peeled cleanly — and its presence is the unmistakable fingerprint of a shape that has failed to be a daisy cube.

## The catalogue of forbidden flowers

We can now state the question that organizes the entire project:

> Which partial cubes are the *minimal obstructions* to being a daisy cube?

A **minimal forbidden pc-minor** is a partial cube $G$ that is itself not a daisy cube — it contains a non-peripheral Θ-class — yet is just barely so: *every* proper pc-minor of $G$ (everything you reach by contracting at least one Θ-class) *is* a daisy cube. These are the irreducible "bad patterns." If you can describe them all, you have described, by their absence, exactly what it takes to be a daisy cube.

The answer is a single, elegant infinite family. Recall that $P_3$ is the bending three-vertex path, that $\square$ denotes the **Cartesian product** of graphs (the operation that builds $Q_n$ as $n$ copies of a single edge multiplied together), and that $P_3^{\,r}$ means $r$ copies of $P_3$ multiplied together — an $r$-dimensional grid of three-position dials. The main theorem reads:

> **Characterization of minimal forbidden pc-minors.** A finite partial cube $G$ is a minimal forbidden pc-minor for the class of daisy cubes **if and only if** there exist integers $r \ge 2$ and $s \ge 1$ such that
> $$G \;\cong\; (P_3^{\,r} \,\square\, Q_s) \setminus \{u, v\},$$
> where $u$ and $v$ are the two antipodal corners of the $P_3^{\,r}$ grid lying in the same copy of $Q_s$.

Let us unpack the construction. Start with $P_3^{\,r}$: an $r$-dimensional grid where each axis runs through three positions, low–middle–high. Multiply it by an $s$-cube $Q_s$, which simply attaches an ordinary $s$-dimensional hypercube to every grid point. Now delete two special vertices: the "all-low" corner and the "all-high" corner of the grid, both taken in the *same* attached cube. The two deleted corners are the antipodes of the grid — as far apart as the grid allows.

Why does this produce an obstruction? Because $P_3$ is exactly the smallest shape that bends without being join-closed, and stacking $r \ge 2$ of these bends while surgically removing the two extreme corners manufactures a non-peripheral Θ-class that *cannot* be peeled — the configuration is not a daisy cube. Yet the surgery is so minimal that contracting *any* single Θ-class repairs the defect: every proper pc-minor falls back into the daisy-cube class. The family is, simultaneously, broken at the top and pristine everywhere below — the very definition of a minimal forbidden pattern.

The two parameters have clean meanings. The exponent $r \ge 2$ counts how many independent "bends" conspire to create the obstruction; you need at least two, because a single $P_3$ is itself a perfectly good daisy cube. The dimension $s \ge 1$ records a hypercube's worth of harmless extra directions riding along — the antipodal deletion has to happen *inside* one such cube, which is why $s$ starts at one. As $r$ and $s$ range over all their allowed values, the construction sweeps out **every** minimal obstruction, with no exceptions and no duplicates.

## Why this is satisfying

Characterizations by forbidden patterns are among the most powerful tools in mathematics. A graph is planar exactly when it avoids two small patterns ($K_5$ and $K_{3,3}$). A matrix is totally unimodular exactly when it avoids a short list of submatrices. Each such theorem turns an open-ended search ("is my object nice?") into a finite or well-organized checklist ("does it avoid these specific patterns?").

The daisy-cube characterization is a member of this distinguished company. It says that the sprawling, intricate world of partial cubes contains a clean sub-world — the daisy cubes — and that the boundary between the two is governed by a *single understandable family* of obstructions, parametrized by two integers. The deep structural facts that make it work are exactly the ones we met along the way:

- Daisy cubes are the **fixed points of the down-closure operator** — being down-closed is intrinsic, not a drawing artifact.
- Daisy cubes are **isometric** in the hypercube — they are genuine partial cubes, with distances preserved.
- Their **meets lie on geodesics** — every pair of points has a perfectly placed common gate.
- They are **meet-closed but not join-closed** — and that single asymmetry, embodied by the bending path $P_3$, is what makes nontrivial obstructions exist at all.

From the humble image of a control panel full of switches, we have arrived at a precise law about which shapes can and cannot appear. The daisy, it turns out, has a very particular geometry — and a complete list of the patterns it must never grow.

## Coda: from switches to structure

There is a broader lesson here, one that recurs across mathematics. Start with something concrete and finite — bit-strings, subsets, on/off switches. Ask a structural question — which sub-collections behave coherently? Discover that the answer hides an algebra (a lattice), a geometry (geodesics through meets), and a sharp dividing line (forbidden minors). The daisy cube is a small object, but it is a perfect specimen of how order, distance, and combinatorics braid together. And like any good field guide, the characterization gives us not just a description of the species we want, but a complete portrait of the patterns that would spoil it.

# Eleven Clocks and a Single Remainder: How One Number Tells You Everything About a Prime's Fate

*The tenth rung of the abelian ladder — the full cyclotomic field built from the eleventh roots of unity — confirms a striking law: in an abelian world, a prime's remainder knows its entire destiny.*

---

## A prime walks into a bigger number system

Pick a prime number, say $p = 3$. In the ordinary integers it is indivisible — that is what "prime" means. But mathematicians have long known that primes behave differently when you enlarge the number system. Throw in $i = \sqrt{-1}$, and $5$ suddenly factors as $(2+i)(2-i)$, while $3$ stays stubbornly whole. Primes that were atoms in one world can shatter into fragments in another.

The question of *how* a prime breaks apart — into how many pieces, and of what size — is called its **splitting type**. For about two centuries, number theorists have treated splitting types as the fingerprints of number fields. In modern cryptography the same structures quietly underpin lattice-based schemes built over cyclotomic rings, where the way the polynomial $x^n + 1$ or $\Phi_m(x)$ falls apart modulo a prime decides how fast multiplication can be done and how the algebra decomposes.

This article is about one specific enlarged world — the field generated by an eleventh root of unity, written $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$ — and about a crisp information-theoretic fact: **the remainder $p \bmod 11$ carries every last bit of information about how $p$ splits.** Not most of it. All of it, exactly $\log_2 5 - 3/5 \approx 1.7219$ bits.

## The eleventh roots of unity

Picture a clock with eleven equally spaced marks on the unit circle in the complex plane. The mark at angle $2\pi/11$ is the number $\zeta_{11} = e^{2\pi i/11}$, and the other marks are its powers $\zeta_{11}^2, \zeta_{11}^3, \dots$. All ten non-trivial marks are roots of a single polynomial,

$$\Phi_{11}(x) = x^{10} + x^9 + \cdots + x + 1,$$

the eleventh **cyclotomic polynomial** ("circle-dividing"). Adjoining $\zeta_{11}$ to the rational numbers produces a number system of dimension $10$ — degree ten.

The symmetries of this system are wonderfully simple. Any symmetry must send $\zeta_{11}$ to another primitive eleventh root $\zeta_{11}^a$, with $a \in \{1, 2, \dots, 10\}$, and composing symmetries multiplies the exponents modulo $11$. So the symmetry group — the Galois group — is just the group of nonzero remainders modulo $11$ under multiplication, written $(\mathbb{Z}/11)^\times$. It is a cyclic group of order ten: every element is a power of $2$, since the powers $2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1$ run through all ten remainders.

## Frobenius: the prime's own symmetry

Here is the miracle that makes everything work. For each prime $p \neq 11$, there is a distinguished symmetry, the **Frobenius element** of $p$, and in cyclotomic fields it is as concrete as can be: it is the symmetry $\zeta_{11} \mapsto \zeta_{11}^{p}$. In other words, the Frobenius of $p$ *is* the remainder $p \bmod 11$.

And the Frobenius controls the splitting. If $t$ is the **order** of $p$ modulo $11$ — the smallest $t \ge 1$ with $p^t \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$ — then $p$ breaks into $10/t$ prime fragments, each of "size" (residue degree) $t$. Since $t$ must divide $10$, there are exactly four possible fates:

| order $t$ | remainders $p \bmod 11$ | fate of $p$ | how $\Phi_{11}$ factors mod $p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | splits completely into 10 pieces | ten linear factors $[1^{10}]$ |
| $2$ | $10$ | 5 pieces of size 2 | five quadratics $[2^5]$ |
| $5$ | $3, 4, 5, 9$ | 2 pieces of size 5 | two quintics $[5,5]$ |
| $10$ | $2, 6, 7, 8$ | stays prime (inert) | irreducible $[10]$ |

Call this order the **Frobenius type** $T(p)$. The last column is the down-to-earth version: you can see a prime's type simply by factoring the polynomial $\Phi_{11}(x)$ with coefficients reduced modulo $p$. Over $\mathbb{F}_{23}$ (and $23 \equiv 1$) it splits into ten linear pieces; over $\mathbb{F}_{43}$ ($43 \equiv -1$) into five quadratics; over $\mathbb{F}_3$ into two quintics; over $\mathbb{F}_2$ it does not factor at all.

## How often does each fate occur?

A classical theorem — Dirichlet's theorem on primes in progressions, a special case of the Chebotarev density theorem — says primes are equidistributed among the ten remainders mod $11$. So each remainder occurs with probability $1/10$, and the four types occur with probabilities

$$\Pr[T=1] = \tfrac{1}{10},\quad \Pr[T=2] = \tfrac{1}{10},\quad \Pr[T=5] = \tfrac{4}{10},\quad \Pr[T=10] = \tfrac{4}{10}.$$

There is a beautiful reason behind the numbers $1, 1, 4, 4$: in a cyclic group of order $n$, the number of elements of order $d$ is Euler's totient $\varphi(d)$ — and indeed $\varphi(1)=1$, $\varphi(2)=1$, $\varphi(5)=4$, $\varphi(10)=4$. This holds for every cyclic Galois group, so every cyclic field has type densities $\varphi(d)/n$.

The prediction was tested against **295,946 unramified primes**, and the observed frequencies sat right on top of $1/10, 1/10, 4/10, 4/10$.

## Measuring "how much the remainder knows"

Now for the information theory. Claude Shannon taught us to measure uncertainty in **bits**. If a random quantity $X$ takes values with probabilities $q_1, q_2, \dots$, its entropy is

$$H(X) = -\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i.$$

The Frobenius type has entropy

$$H(T) = -2\cdot\tfrac{1}{10}\log_2\tfrac{1}{10} - 2\cdot\tfrac{4}{10}\log_2\tfrac{4}{10} = \log_2 5 - \tfrac{3}{5} \approx 1.7219 \text{ bits}.$$

That is how uncertain you are about a random prime's fate before learning anything.

The **mutual information** $I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$ measures how many of those bits a second observation $Y$ reveals. Think of a "channel": the remainder $p \bmod 11$ goes in, and we ask how much of the type $T(p)$ comes out.

**The degree-ten full-pinning theorem.** *For primes with uniformly distributed Frobenius in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$,*
$$I(p \bmod 11;\ T) = H(T) = \log_2 5 - \tfrac{3}{5} \approx 1.7219 \text{ bits, exactly}.$$
*Moreover, the type is completely determined once the remainder is known — $H(T \mid p \bmod 11) = 0$ — while the type leaves exactly $H(p \bmod 11 \mid T) = 8/5$ bits of the remainder undetermined.*

The intuition is short: the type is a *function* of the remainder (the order of $p \bmod 11$), so knowing the remainder leaves no doubt about the type. The remainder has $\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ bits, and exactly $1.72$ of them are "about the type"; the remaining $1.6$ bits distinguish, say, $3$ from $4$ from $5$ from $9$ — remainders that share the same fate.

The precise decimals are not left to floating-point luck: from exact integer comparisons such as $2^{23219} < 5^{10000}$ and $5^{100000} < 2^{232193}$ one gets $2.3219 < \log_2 5 < 2.32193$, and hence the certified enclosure
$$1.7219 < I(p \bmod 11;\ T) < 1.72193.$$

## Why abelian is the magic word

Why should this be true, and how far does it extend? The real engine is a general law.

**The abelian full-pinning law.** *Let the Galois group $A$ be any finite abelian group, let $c$ be any readout that tells all elements of $A$ apart (like the remainder), and let $T$ be any splitting-type readout. Then the residue pins the type: $I(c;T) = H(T)$; the type is certain given the residue: $H(T \mid c) = 0$; and the information lost is exactly the residual uncertainty: $\log_2|A| - I(c;T) = H(c \mid T)$.*

In a non-abelian world things are different. There, the most an "abelian" residue can see is the image of Frobenius in the *abelianization* — the group with all commutators crushed to the identity. Different Frobenius classes can look identical through that lens, and the residue then fails to pin the type. Abelian fields are exactly those where nothing is crushed: the residue is a perfect label.

This single law covers every rung of the "abelian ladder" checked so far — fields of degree $2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9$ and now $10$, built both as real subfields of cyclotomic fields and as full cyclotomic fields — with no exceptions, because it is a theorem about *all* abelian groups at once.

## A ladder inside the ladder: the real subfield and one extra bit

$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$ contains a degree-five subfield made of real numbers, generated by $\zeta_{11} + \zeta_{11}^{-1} = 2\cos(2\pi/11)$. Its Galois group is $(\mathbb{Z}/11)^\times / \{\pm 1\}$, cyclic of order five, and the type $T^+(p)$ there is the order of $p$ up to sign. Two remainders ($\pm1$) give type $1$; the other eight give type $5$. So

$$H(T^+) = \log_2 5 - \tfrac{8}{5} \approx 0.7219 \text{ bits},$$

and the same remainder pins this type too. Going up from the real subfield to the full field adds **exactly one bit** of type information: $H(T \mid T^+) = 1$, matching $\log_2$ of the degree $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11}) : \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})^+] = 2$.

There is an elegant explanation via the Chinese Remainder Theorem: the cyclic group of order $10$ is a product $C_2 \times C_5$, the order of an element is the product of the orders of its two components, and the two components are independent. Entropy adds over independent pieces, so

$$H(T) = H_{C_2} + H_{C_5} = 1 + \left(\log_2 5 - \tfrac{8}{5}\right).$$

## From four hundred checks to every prime

The polynomial side was tested on 400 primes: in every case, the degrees of the irreducible factors of $\Phi_{11}$ mod $p$ matched the predicted pattern. (A small cautionary tale from the computation: the first comparison reported 400 mismatches out of 400 — not because the mathematics failed, but because one side wrapped the pattern in an extra layer of brackets. The pattern itself was perfect.)

The experiment can now be retired in favor of a theorem valid for **every prime and every conductor**:

**Factor-pattern theorem.** *For any $n$ and any prime $p$ not dividing $n$, the polynomial $\Phi_n(x)$ factors over $\mathbb{F}_p$ into exactly $\varphi(n)/t$ irreducible factors, each of degree $t$, where $t$ is the order of $p$ modulo $n$.* 

There is a lovely mirror image inside the group: multiplying the elements of $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$ by $p$ shuffles them into cycles, and every cycle has length exactly $t$, with $\varphi(n)/t$ cycles in all. The factorization of the polynomial is the cycle structure of the permutation. A consequence: if $p \equiv q \pmod n$, then $\Phi_n$ factors with the same pattern modulo $p$ and modulo $q$ — the remainder alone determines the shape.

## Semiprimes: when two fates multiply

Cryptographers care about numbers like $N = pq$, products of two primes. Suppose we observe only $N \bmod 11$. How much do we learn about the *pair* of fates $\{T(p), T(q)\}$?

Model $p$ and $q$ as having independent, uniformly distributed remainders. The remainder of $N$ is the product of the two remainders, and multiplication mixes things up: $N \equiv 1$ could come from $1\cdot 1$, from $10 \cdot 10$, from $3 \cdot 4$, and so on. The exact answer is

$$I\big(N \bmod 11;\ \{T(p),T(q)\}\big) = \log_2 5 + \tfrac{12}{25}\log_2 3 - \tfrac{47}{25} \approx 1.20271 \text{ bits}.$$

This value has a story. An empirical measurement returned $1.2002$; an enumeration produced $1.1999$; and an earlier closed-form calculation predicted $1.2027$. The exact law settles the matter: it lies within $3 \times 10^{-5}$ of $1.2027$ and strictly above both $1.2002$ and $1.1999$. The earlier closed form was right; the other two figures were finite-sample estimates, and the apparent "wall" of $0.0005$ between them is just the gap between two estimates, not a feature of the mathematics.

Comparing with the single-prime channel gives a clean **attenuation law**:

$$H(T) - I\big(N \bmod 11;\ \{T(p),T(q)\}\big) = \tfrac{32}{25} - \tfrac{12}{25}\log_2 3 \approx 0.519 \text{ bits}.$$

Multiplying two primes together blurs about half a bit of fate information.

## The wall that isn't there

One more question: does it help to know *which* prime has which type — the ordered pair $(T(p), T(q))$ rather than the unordered set? Remarkably, no. At degree ten the ordered and unordered channels carry *exactly* the same information about $N \bmod 11$. This "which-factor wall" is zero.

And it is zero for a structural reason that reaches far beyond degree ten. Swapping $p$ and $q$ leaves $N$ unchanged but swaps the ordered pair. A neat entropy lemma shows that forgetting the order of a swap-symmetric pair costs exactly the probability that the two entries differ — and it costs that *same* amount whether or not you also know $N$. The two costs cancel in the mutual information. The only requirement is that the residue cannot tell $gh$ from $hg$. In an abelian group that is automatic. In a non-abelian group it still holds whenever the residue is read through the abelianization, because $gh$ and $hg$ differ by a commutator. This explains in one stroke why previously computed which-factor walls for the non-abelian groups $A_4$ (the rotations of a tetrahedron) and $D_4$ (the symmetries of a square) were also exactly zero.

## What the tenth rung teaches

Step back and the picture is satisfying. Every prediction made in advance for degree ten came true: the densities $1,1,4,4$ out of ten; the full-pinning value $\log_2 5 - 3/5$; the per-class certainty; the factor patterns. Two predictions were upgraded from "checked on data" to "true always": the polynomial patterns now hold for every prime and every conductor, and the zero which-factor wall holds for every group read through its abelianization. And one number was corrected: the semiprime law is $1.20271$, not the enumerated $1.1999$.

The broader lesson is about what "knowing the remainder" means. In an abelian number field, a prime's remainder is not a hint about its fate — it *is* its fate, written in a different alphabet. The remainder holds a few extra bits beyond that (exactly $8/5$ of them at degree ten), but nothing about the splitting is left unknown. The frontier now moves to non-abelian fields — where the residue sees only a shadow of the Frobenius — and to open conjectures: that the gap between a cyclotomic field and its real subfield is always a power of two, namely $2^{1 - v_2(p-1)}$ bits, and that multiplying primes always blurs fate information for every prime conductor $\ell \ge 5$.

Eleven marks on a circle, ten symmetries, four fates — and one remainder that knows them all.

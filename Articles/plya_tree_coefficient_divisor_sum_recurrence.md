# Counting Trees with a Single Multiplication Trick

## A puzzle that grows like a forest

Imagine you are handed a single dot on a page and told: this is a tree with one
node. Now grow it. Add a second node, joined to the first. There is only one way
to do that, so there is exactly one tree with two nodes. Add a third node, and
two genuinely different shapes appear: a straight little chain, or a "Y" with two
children hanging off the root. With four nodes there are four shapes, with five
there are nine, with six there are twenty.

These numbers — $1, 1, 2, 4, 9, 20, 48, 115, 286, 719, \dots$ — form one of the
most famous sequences in combinatorics. They count *rooted, unlabelled trees*:
branching diagrams with a distinguished root at the top, where we do not care
about the names of the nodes or the left-to-right order of the branches, only the
abstract shape. Mathematicians call them **Pólya trees**, after George Pólya, who
in the 1930s built the counting machinery that tames them. Computer scientists
meet the same numbers whenever they reason about hierarchical data structures:
hash trees, Merkle trees, prefix trees, and the tree-shaped key schedules that
appear in cryptography.

The shapes multiply alarmingly fast. By the time you reach $15$ nodes there are
$87{,}811$ distinct trees. By $30$ nodes the count is in the hundreds of billions.
You cannot enumerate them one by one. So how does anyone know that the tree count
on $16$ nodes is *exactly* $87{,}811$ and not $87{,}810$?

The answer is a beautiful recurrence — a rule that computes each new count from
all the previous ones using nothing more exotic than addition, multiplication,
the divisors of a number, and a single division at the end. This article is about
that rule, why it works, and the one delicate fact hiding inside it that makes
the whole thing tick.

## The recurrence in plain sight

Let us write $a(n)$ for the number of Pólya trees on $n$ nodes, so
$$a(1) = 1,\quad a(2) = 1,\quad a(3) = 2,\quad a(4) = 4,\quad a(5) = 9, \dots$$

The recurrence needs one auxiliary quantity. For each whole number $k$, form a
weighted sum over the **divisors** of $k$ — the numbers that divide $k$ evenly —
where each divisor $d$ is weighted by $d$ itself times the tree count $a(d)$:
$$c(k) \;=\; \sum_{d \mid k} d \cdot a(d).$$
For example, the divisors of $6$ are $1, 2, 3, 6$, so
$$c(6) = 1\cdot a(1) + 2\cdot a(2) + 3\cdot a(3) + 6\cdot a(6).$$
This object $c(k)$ is known as the **Euler-transform coefficient** of the sequence.
Intuitively, it bundles together not just the trees of size $k$ but the
"echoes" of smaller trees that can be repeated to fill up $k$ nodes — a divisor
$d$ of $k$ corresponds to using a size-$d$ building block $k/d$ times.

With $c$ in hand, the entire sequence is generated by one clean formula. Pólya's
divisor-sum recurrence states that for every $n \ge 1$,
$$n \cdot a(n+1) \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} c(k)\,\cdot\,a(n+1-k),$$
starting from $a(1) = 1$. To get the next tree count $a(n+1)$, you sum the
products $c(k)\,a(n+1-k)$ as $k$ runs from $1$ to $n$, and then divide by $n$.

Let us watch it run. To find $a(2)$ we take $n = 1$:
$$1 \cdot a(2) = c(1)\,a(1) = (1\cdot a(1))\cdot a(1) = 1, \quad\text{so } a(2) = 1.$$
To find $a(3)$ we take $n = 2$. We need $c(1) = 1$ and $c(2) = 1\cdot a(1) + 2\cdot a(2) = 1 + 2 = 3$. Then
$$2 \cdot a(3) = c(1)\,a(2) + c(2)\,a(1) = 1\cdot 1 + 3\cdot 1 = 4,\quad\text{so } a(3) = 2.$$
For $a(4)$, take $n = 3$, with $c(3) = 1\cdot a(1) + 3\cdot a(3) = 1 + 6 = 7$:
$$3 \cdot a(4) = c(1)\,a(3) + c(2)\,a(2) + c(3)\,a(1) = 2 + 3 + 7 = 12,\quad\text{so } a(4) = 4.$$
And on it goes, each line producing the next tree count, all the way to
$a(16) = 87{,}811$ and beyond. The machine never looks at a tree; it only
manipulates earlier numbers.

## Why divisors? The hidden symmetry of branches

The appearance of divisors is not an accident — it is the fingerprint of
**symmetry**. A rooted tree is, at its core, a root with a *collection of
subtrees* dangling beneath it. Because we do not care about the order of the
branches, that collection is an unordered multiset: the same subtree shape may be
repeated several times, and repetition is exactly where symmetry — and divisors —
enter.

Pólya captured this with a single equation relating the generating function
$A(z) = \sum_{n\ge 1} a(n)\, z^n$ to itself:
$$A(z) \;=\; z \,\exp\!\Big(\sum_{i \ge 1} \tfrac{1}{i}\, A(z^i)\Big).$$
The factor $z$ plants the root; the exponential of the sum builds the unordered
multiset of children; and the appearance of $A(z^i)$ — the generating function
evaluated at $z^i$ — is the algebraic shadow of using a subtree $i$ times over.
When you take the logarithmic derivative of this identity, those $z^i$ terms
collapse precisely into the divisor sum $c(k) = \sum_{d \mid k} d\,a(d)$, and the
recurrence above falls out. The divisors are the bookkeeping of how a smaller
shape can be copied to tile a larger one.

## The miracle of the final division

Here is the subtle part — the place where the formula could, in principle, fail,
and where its truth becomes genuinely surprising.

The recurrence ends with a division by $n$:
$$a(n+1) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n} c(k)\,a(n+1-k).$$
But $a(n+1)$ is a *count of trees*. It must be a whole number. There is no such
thing as $4.5$ trees. So the sum on the right, a jumble of products of divisor
sums and earlier counts, must be divisible by $n$ — **every single time**, for
every $n$, with no remainder ever left over. This is called the **integrality** of
the Euler transform, and it is not obvious at all. Looking at the formula cold,
there is no visible reason why a division by $7$, or by $12$, or by $1000$, should
always come out exact.

That it does is a deep structural fact. It can be seen from the combinatorial
meaning — the left side literally counts objects, so it cannot be a fraction — and
it can also be attacked through number-theoretic arguments about cyclic symmetry
(the kind of "necklace counting" that explains why $n$ divides certain sums of
powers). In the formalized version of this work, the exact integer identity
$$n \cdot a(n+1) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c(k)\,a(n+1-k)$$
— with no division and no rounding — was verified rigorously for every $n$ from
$1$ through $13$, confirming that the sum is perfectly divisible by $n$ across
that range. The general statement, that this exactness never breaks for *any*
$n$, stands as a clean and precise open challenge.

## What was actually proved

To pin all of this down with total certainty, the sequence $a(n)$ was given a
direct, self-contained definition built purely from the recurrence — not from any
external table — and several precise statements about it were established beyond
doubt.

First, a **correctness table**: the first sixteen values produced by the
recurrence are
$$0, 1, 1, 2, 4, 9, 20, 48, 115, 286, 719, 1842, 4766, 12486, 32973, 87811,$$
(using the convention $a(0) = 0$, the empty tree). These match the canonical tree
counts entry for entry.

Second, a **general recurrence theorem**: for *every* $n \ge 1$, the defined
sequence satisfies
$$a(n+1) = \Big\lfloor \tfrac{1}{n}\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{n} c(k)\,a(n+1-k)\Big\rfloor,$$
where the division is whole-number division. This holds with no exceptions and no
upper bound on $n$ — it is the engine that defines the whole sequence.

Third, the **exactness theorem**: for $1 \le n \le 13$, the division leaves no
remainder, so the pristine integer identity $n\cdot a(n+1) = \sum_{k} c(k)\,a(n+1-k)$
holds on the nose.

Fourth, **positivity**: for $1 \le n \le 15$, the count $a(n)$ is at least $1$ —
there is always at least one tree of each size, as common sense demands.

## Why a cryptographer cares about counting trees

This might look like pure recreational mathematics, but tree counts sit close to
the foundations of modern data security. A **Merkle tree** — the structure
underlying blockchains, certificate transparency logs, and verifiable backups —
is a rooted tree of hash values. **Hash trees** and **prefix trees (tries)**
organize keys for fast, tamper-evident lookup. **Tree-indexed key derivation**
schemes branch a master secret into a hierarchy of subkeys whose shape is, quite
literally, a rooted tree.

Whenever you want to reason about such a system — How many distinct tree shapes of
a given size exist? How likely is a random structure to collide with another? How
much entropy does a tree-shaped key schedule carry? — you are counting Pólya
trees. The divisor-sum recurrence is the most efficient honest way to get those
counts, and the integrality phenomenon at its heart is a small instance of the
larger principle that good combinatorial structures hide exact arithmetic
symmetries. The Euler transform that appears here is the same operation that turns
"how many connected building blocks of each size" into "how many total assemblies
of each size," a translation that appears throughout the analysis of hierarchical
and self-similar systems.

## The growth that never stops

How fast do these numbers climb? The ratio of consecutive counts,
$a(n+1)/a(n)$, settles toward a specific constant near $2.9557652856\ldots$,
sometimes called **Otter's constant** after Richard Otter, who in 1948 worked out
the precise asymptotics. The full growth law is
$$a(n) \sim C \cdot \alpha^{n} \cdot n^{-3/2},$$
where $\alpha \approx 2.9557$ and $C$ is another constant. So tree counts grow
almost geometrically, multiplied by roughly $2.96$ at each step, with a gentle
polynomial drag of $n^{-3/2}$. That single number $\alpha$ encodes the asymptotic
"branching richness" of all rooted trees at once — a fitting capstone to a
sequence that begins with a single lonely dot.

## The shape of a good idea

What makes this story satisfying is the contrast between the wildness of the
objects and the tameness of the rule that counts them. The trees themselves
proliferate beyond any hope of listing. Yet they are governed by an utterly
finite recipe: take divisors, weight them, convolve, divide once. The recipe is so
disciplined that it can be written down completely, executed by hand for small
cases, and checked with machine certainty.

And lurking inside that tidy recipe is a genuine mystery — the unbroken
divisibility by $n$, the guarantee that you never count a fraction of a tree. It
has been confirmed exactly through the thirteenth step and is believed to hold
forever. That gap between "verified far" and "proved always" is where the next
chapter of the story waits to be written. For now, the humble forest of rooted
trees stands as a small monument to a recurring theme in mathematics: the most
explosive complexity often answers to the simplest of laws.

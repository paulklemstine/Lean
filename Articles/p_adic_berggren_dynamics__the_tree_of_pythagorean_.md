# The Tree of Right Triangles, Seen Through a Prime

## How the infinite family of Pythagorean triples turns into a finite clockwork when you look at it modulo $p$ — and what that clockwork tells us

### A tree with every right triangle on it

Everyone meets $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$ early. Fewer people meet the astonishing fact that *every* right triangle with whole-number sides can be reached from that one by a short recipe, and reached in exactly one way.

The recipe is three matrices. Write a triple as a column vector $(a,b,c)$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$, and apply any of

$$
B_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\ 2&-1&2\\ 2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\ 2&1&2\\ 2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_3=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\ -2&1&2\\ -2&2&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Feed in $(3,4,5)$ and you get $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$. Feed those back in and you get nine more; then twenty-seven; and so on forever. Every *primitive* triple — one whose three numbers share no common factor — appears exactly once somewhere in this infinite ternary tree. The tree was discovered by Berggren in 1934 and rediscovered by Barning and by Hall decades later, and it is one of the most satisfying structures in elementary number theory: a complete, non-redundant catalogue of an infinite family, generated by three $3\times 3$ integer matrices.

Why do these matrices work? Because of a hidden geometry. Define the *Lorentz form*
$$
q(a,b,c) = a^2 + b^2 - c^2 .
$$
A Pythagorean triple is exactly a point where $q=0$: a point on the **null cone**, the light cone of a two-dimensional spacetime. And each of $B_1, B_2, B_3$ leaves $q$ untouched: for every vector $v$ and every one of the three matrices $B$, we have $q(Bv)=q(v)$. They are Lorentz transformations with integer entries. The tree of Pythagorean triples is an orbit of a discrete group of symmetries of a light cone, and its three generators are the three ways of "bouncing" a light ray around a hyperbolic triangle.

The three matrices are not interchangeable. Their characteristic polynomials tell them apart:
$$
\det(xI-B_1) = \det(xI-B_3) = (x-1)^3,\qquad
\det(xI-B_2) = (x+1)\bigl(x^2-6x+1\bigr).
$$
So $B_1$ and $B_3$ have all eigenvalues equal to $1$: they are **unipotent**, parabolic shears, the analogue of a light ray grazing the horizon. The middle generator $B_2$ has eigenvalues $-1$ and $3\pm2\sqrt2$. Now $3+2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2 \approx 5.828$ is the square of the fundamental unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, an irrational number greater than one. That makes $B_2$ **hyperbolic**: a genuine Lorentz boost, stretching one null direction and shrinking another, sending points racing off to infinity along the cone.

That much has been understood, in its real and hyperbolic-geometric form, for a long while. This article is about a different question, and its answer turns out to be surprisingly clean.

### What happens if you look at the tree modulo a prime?

Number theorists have a reflex: when an object over $\mathbb{Z}$ is too big to grasp, reduce it modulo a prime $p$, or modulo $p^k$, and look at the finite shadow. Do it for a whole prime power tower at once and you are working *$p$-adically*: two integers are close if their difference is divisible by a high power of $p$.

Reduce the Berggren tree this way and something drastic happens. The infinite tree, which has $3^d$ vertices at depth $d$, lands inside the finite set $(\mathbb{Z}/p^k)^3$. The three moves become permutations of a finite set — permutations, not just maps, because each matrix has an integer inverse (for instance $B_2^{-1}$ has entries $1,2,-2;\,2,1,-2;\,-2,-2,3$) and each inverse also preserves $q$. So an infinite, freely branching, chaotic-looking tree collapses into a finite clockwork: a permutation group acting on a finite phase space, where every point is periodic and nothing escapes to infinity.

The whole question is: *what does that clockwork look like?* Three things need answering. How big is the phase space? What is the period of each generator? And does the reduction remember anything of the real, hyperbolic geometry?

### The phase space has exactly $p^2$ points

The states of the reduced system are the solutions of $a^2+b^2 \equiv c^2 \pmod p$. Counting them is a small gem.

**Theorem (Null-cone census).** *For every odd prime $p$, the number of vectors $v\in(\mathbb{Z}/p)^3$ with $v_0^2+v_1^2-v_2^2 = 0$ is exactly $p^2$.*

Here is the argument in one breath. Slice the cone by the "light-cone coordinate" $u = c-a$. If $u \ne 0$, then from $b^2 = c^2-a^2 = (c-a)(c+a) = u\,(c+a)$ we can solve: $c+a = b^2/u$ is determined by $b$, and then $a$ and $c$ are determined too. So the slice is a graph over $b$ and contains exactly $p$ points. If $u=0$, the equation forces $b^2=0$, hence $b=0$ and $c=a$: the slice is the isotropic line $(s,0,s)$, again exactly $p$ points. Every one of the $p$ slices has $p$ points, so the cone has $p\cdot p = p^2$ points, of which $p^2-1$ are nonzero. Beautifully, the degenerate slice is not an exception — it has the same size as all the others, which is precisely the statement that the ternary form $a^2+b^2-c^2$ is nondegenerate and isotropic.

Since each nonzero null vector spans a line with $p-1$ nonzero multiples, there are $(p^2-1)/(p-1) = p+1$ null directions: the "circle at infinity" of the hyperbolic plane over $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is a set of $p+1$ points. That number will reappear.

### The shears become clocks with $p$-power periods

Write $B_1 = I + N_1$. The remainder
$$
N_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-2&2\\ 2&-2&2\\ 2&-2&2\end{pmatrix}
$$
satisfies $N_1^3 = 0$: it is nilpotent of index three, exactly as the eigenvalue computation predicted. Nilpotence makes powers easy. If $X^3=0$ then, in any ring,
$$
(1+X)^n = 1 + nX + \binom{n}{2}X^2 ,
$$
because all later binomial terms carry a factor $X^3$. Take $n = p^k$ with $p$ odd. Then $p^k \equiv 0$ modulo $p^k$, and $\binom{p^k}{2} = p^k\cdot\frac{p^k-1}{2}$ is also divisible by $p^k$ because $p^k$ is odd. Both correction terms vanish:

**Theorem (Unipotent order).** *For every odd prime $p$ and every $k\ge1$, $B_1^{\,p^k} = I$ and $B_3^{\,p^k} = I$ modulo $p^k$. Conversely, if $B_1^{\,m}=I$ modulo $p^k$ then $p^k$ divides $m$. So the order of $B_1$ modulo $p^k$ is exactly $p^k$.*

The converse comes from reading off a single entry: the $(0,1)$ entry of $I + mN_1 + \binom m2 N_1^2$ is $-2m$, and $2m \equiv 0$ modulo the odd number $p^k$ forces $p^k \mid m$.

An order that is a pure power of $p$, with no factor coprime to $p$, is the hallmark of what group theorists call *pro-$p$* behaviour. It is the exact algebraic fingerprint of "unipotent". And it is sharp in a pleasing way: $B_1^{\,p^k} = I$ modulo $p^k$ but **not** modulo $p^{k+1}$. Each application of the shear buys you exactly one extra digit of $p$-adic precision, never two.

The shears also leave something standing still. $B_1$ fixes the vector $(0,1,1)$ — and modulo $p$ its fixed set is precisely the line $\{(0,t,t)\}$, all $p$ of those vectors and no others. Note that $q(0,t,t) = 0$: the fixed line lies *on* the cone. A parabolic isometry of the hyperbolic plane fixes exactly one point on the boundary circle, and here we see it, in miniature, over a finite field. Likewise $B_3$ fixes the null line through $(1,0,1)$.

### The boost becomes a rotation — or stays a boost — depending on $p \bmod 8$

The middle generator is the interesting one. Over the reals $B_2$ is a boost with stretch factor $3+2\sqrt2$; it has infinite order and its orbits on the boundary circle accumulate at the two fixed null directions. Modulo $p$ it cannot possibly have infinite order. What replaces the boost?

Change basis. With $W$ the matrix whose columns are $(1,-1,0)$, $(1,1,0)$, $(0,0,1)$ one checks directly that
$$
B_2\,W = W\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&0\\ 0&3&2\\ 0&4&3\end{pmatrix} .
$$
So $B_2$ acts as multiplication by $-1$ on the spacelike vector $(1,-1,0)$ and, on the remaining plane, as the $2\times2$ matrix
$$
U=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\4&3\end{pmatrix} = 3I + 2J,\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\2&0\end{pmatrix},\quad J^2 = 2I .
$$
The matrix $J$ *is* $\sqrt2$, realised concretely; $U$ is multiplication by $3+2\sqrt2$ in the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, and $\det U = 9-8=1$ says that this element has norm one.

Now reduce modulo $p$ and turn the Frobenius crank. In characteristic $p$, raising a sum to the $p$-th power distributes over commuting terms, so
$$
U^p = 3 + 2\cdot 2^{(p-1)/2}\, J .
$$
By Euler's criterion $2^{(p-1)/2}$ is $+1$ if $2$ is a square modulo $p$ and $-1$ if it is not. Two worlds open up:

* **Split case ($2$ is a square modulo $p$).** Then $U^p = U$, so $U^{p-1}=I$, and since $p-1$ is even the sign block contributes nothing: $B_2^{\,p-1} = I$.
* **Inert case ($2$ is not a square modulo $p$).** Then $U^p = 3-2J = U^{-1}$ (using norm one), so $U^{p+1}=I$, and $p+1$ is even: $B_2^{\,p+1}=I$.

**Theorem (Spectral period).** *For every odd prime $p$, the order of $B_2$ modulo $p$ divides $p-1$ if $2$ is a quadratic residue modulo $p$ and divides $p+1$ otherwise. In both cases it divides $p^2-1$.*

Quadratic reciprocity converts the condition into a congruence, and the whole dichotomy becomes a statement you can read off the last three binary digits of $p$:

**Theorem (The $\bmod\ 8$ dichotomy).** *$B_2$ has a nonzero eigenvector lying on the null cone modulo $p$ if and only if $2$ is a square modulo $p$, if and only if $p \equiv \pm1 \pmod 8$.*

In the split case there really is a $\sqrt2$ in $\mathbb{Z}/p$, and then $(1,1,\sqrt2)$ is a null vector — indeed $1+1-2=0$ — and an eigenvector: $B_2\,(1,1,\sqrt2) = (3+2\sqrt2)\,(1,1,\sqrt2)$. The boost survives reduction: it still stretches a null direction, now by a finite-order scalar, and the orbit of that eigenvector is just the geometric progression of the eigenvalue, closing up after exactly $\operatorname{ord}(3+2\sqrt2)$ steps. In the inert case the two null directions of the real boost are *conjugate over a quadratic extension*: they do not exist in $\mathbb{Z}/p$, and the boost has become an elliptic rotation with no fixed direction at all, period dividing $p+1$.

This is the local incarnation of a familiar dichotomy — split versus inert primes in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ — and it says something concrete about Pythagorean triples. Whether the "doubling move" $B_2$ behaves like a stretch or a rotation on the mod-$p$ triples is decided by $p \bmod 8$: $p = 7, 17, 23, 31, 41, 47$ are stretches; $p = 3, 5, 11, 13, 19, 29$ are rotations.

The contrast with the shears is total: $B_2$ has **no** nonzero fixed vector modulo any odd prime. Setting $B_2v=v$ and subtracting yields $2(v_1+v_2)=2(v_0+v_2)=2(v_0+v_1+v_2)=0$, and since $2$ is invertible these force $v=0$. So we get a clean counting form of the spectral classification: modulo $p$, the unipotent generator fixes $p$ points — a whole isotropic line — and the hyperbolic generator fixes exactly one.

### The clockwork never mixes

Here comes the punchline that separates the $p$-adic picture from the real one. Over the reals, the boost's orbits on the boundary circle are infinite and accumulate densely at the fixed points. Modulo $p$ they cannot: every orbit of $B_2$ has at most $p+1$ points, since $B_2^{\,m}=I$ for some $m \le p+1$. But the punctured null cone has $p^2-1 = (p-1)(p+1)$ points. A single orbit cannot cover it.

**Theorem (No ergodicity).** *For every odd prime $p$ and every starting vector $v$, the hyperbolic generator is not transitive on the $p^2-1$ nonzero null vectors modulo $p$: the reduced dynamics splits into at least $p-1$ distinct orbits.*

That is a genuinely negative result about mixing, and it is worth pausing on. The real Berggren dynamics on the boundary is as chaotic as a hyperbolic isometry can be; its reduction is a disjoint union of short cycles. Chaos is not preserved by reduction — it is shredded into arithmetic.

Counting the exact number of cycles is a pretty combinatorial problem still open in general. Direct enumeration gives $2$ orbits for $p=3$, $4$ for $p=5$, $10$ for $p=7$ (six of length $6$ and four of length $3$), $10$ for $p=11$, $12$ for $p=13$, and $36$ for $p=17$.

### Deeper into the prime: everything freezes

So far, modulo $p$. What about modulo $p^k$, that is, $p$-adically? The tool is Hensel's principle in the crudest possible form. If a matrix $A$ with integer entries satisfies $A \equiv I$ modulo $p^j$ with $j\ge1$, write $A = I + p^jN$; then
$$
A^p = I + p^{j+1}N + (p^jN)^2\cdot(\text{something}),
$$
and both correction terms are divisible by $p^{j+1}$. Each $p$-th power buys one more digit. Iterating from the already-proved congruence $B_2^{\,p^2-1}\equiv I \pmod p$:

**Theorem ($p$-adic periodicity).** *For every odd prime $p$ and every $k\ge0$, $B_2^{\,(p^2-1)p^k} \equiv I$ modulo $p^{k+1}$. Consequently, for every integer vector $v$ and every $i$, the $i$-th coordinate of $B_2^{\,N}v - v$ is divisible by $p^{k+1}$, where $N=(p^2-1)p^k$; that is, $|B_2^Nv-v|_p \le p^{-(k+1)}$.*

Read that as a statement in the $p$-adic metric: the hyperbolic Berggren move is **periodic to any prescribed $p$-adic precision**. Take $p=5$, $k=2$: after $N = 24 \cdot 25 = 600$ applications of $B_2$, the triple $(3,4,5)$ has grown to a number with 460 digits, but it agrees with $(3,4,5)$ to three $5$-adic digits. The infinite hyperbolic escape of the real picture is, from the $p$-adic vantage point, an almost-return. The stretching factor $3+2\sqrt2$ is a $p$-adic unit, and units have finite order in every finite quotient; expansion at the archimedean place is compatible with recurrence at every finite place.

How fast does the period grow with depth? For the shears we know exactly: one factor of $p$ per digit. For the boost the same pattern holds for $p=3,5,7,11,\dots$ — the orders modulo $p$, $p^2$, $p^3$ are $4,12,36$ for $p=3$ and $6,30,150$ for $p=5$ — but it is not a law. Two primes below $3000$ misbehave: at $p=13$ the order is $14$ both modulo $13$ and modulo $169$, only reaching $182$ modulo $13^3$, and at $p=31$ the order is $30$ modulo both $31$ and $961$, reaching $930$ only modulo $31^3$. These are exactly the primes for which the unit $3+2\sqrt2$ is trivial to two $p$-adic digits at once — a Pythagorean cousin of the notorious Wieferich primes, and a reminder that $p$-adic depth is governed by delicate arithmetic and not by a clean formula.

### The one prime where everything dies — and why triples have the parity they do

The prime $2$ has been excluded throughout, and for a spectacular reason.

**Theorem (Collapse at $2$).** *Modulo $2$, all three Berggren matrices are the identity. Hence every word in the generators reduces to the identity, and every vertex of the tree is congruent to the root $(3,4,5)\equiv(1,0,1)$ modulo $2$.*

Look at the matrices again: every off-diagonal entry is even and every diagonal entry is odd. The whole infinite tree squashes to a single point; the "dynamical system" modulo $2$ is one fixed point and nothing else. This is exactly the degenerate scenario one always worries about when reducing a rich system, and here it is realised at precisely one prime.

The degeneracy is not a defect — it is a classical fact in disguise. Because each move preserves the residue class mod $2$, every triple in the tree has the shape (odd, even, odd):

**Theorem (Parity of the tree).** *For every word $w$ in the Berggren moves, the triple $w\cdot(3,4,5)$ has odd first leg, even second leg, and odd hypotenuse. In particular, no vertex of the tree has two odd legs.*

That is the familiar statement that in a primitive Pythagorean triple one leg is even — but derived here as a fixed-point theorem for the mod-$2$ dynamics rather than by the usual congruence bookkeeping. The failure of the $p$-adic programme at $p=2$ *is* the parity structure of Pythagorean triples.

The same nuance appears one level up. The nilpotent part $N_1$ has $N_1^2$ with every entry equal to $\pm4$, so $N_1^2 = 0$ whenever $4=0$ in the ring: modulo $2$ and modulo $4$ the nilpotency index drops from $3$ to $2$. The unipotent classification is uniform at every odd prime power and degenerates only in the $2$-adic world.

### No Cantor set at any finite level

One hope going in was that the boundary of the tree — the set of infinite words in three letters, a Cantor set of Hausdorff dimension $\log 3/\log 2$ in its natural metric — might embed $p$-adically and inherit a fractal dimension. It cannot, and the obstruction is a counting argument of one line. At depth $d$ the tree has $3^d$ vertices, all lying on the null cone, which modulo $p$ has only $p^2$ points. As soon as $3^d > p^2$, two distinct words must produce the same triple modulo $p$. (Even without the cone, $m^3 < 3^d$ already forces a collision modulo $m$.) At $p=5$ and depth $4$, for instance, the $81$ words produce only $12$ distinct residues.

So no fixed finite level holds a faithful copy of the boundary, and the "box dimension" of the reduction is at most $2$, not $\log3/\log2$ times anything. What does survive is the inverse-limit statement: each generator is periodic to any precision, so the system is well-defined and non-trivial on the full $p$-adic integers, even though every finite truncation of it is a small finite permutation.

### What the shadow shows

Take a step back. The Berggren tree in its real incarnation is a hyperbolic object: a free group of Lorentz isometries, one boost and two parabolics, tiling a hyperbolic triangle and generating chaos on the boundary circle. Its reduction modulo $p$ keeps all the *labels* of that structure and destroys all the *dynamics*. The parabolics stay parabolic — nilpotent, with $p$-power order and one fixed null line each. The boost stays recognisably a boost, but only for half the primes: for $p \equiv \pm1 \pmod 8$ it has two null eigendirections and behaves like a scaling; for $p\equiv\pm3\pmod 8$ its eigendirections leave the field and it becomes a rotation of order dividing $p+1$. Meanwhile the mixing evaporates: orbits are short, the system is never ergodic, and the whole apparatus grinds to a halt at $p=2$, where the residue is nothing but the parity of a Pythagorean triple.

That mixture of preservation and collapse is characteristic of arithmetic dynamics, and it suggests the right questions to ask next. How many cycles are there exactly, as a function of $p$ and of $p\bmod 8$? Are there infinitely many exceptional primes like $13$ and $31$, where the period refuses to grow at the first extra digit? How many points does the null cone have modulo $p^k$ — the pattern $p^2$, $p^4+p^3-p^2$, $\dots$ comes from stratifying by the valuation of the light-cone coordinate.

The tree of right triangles is over two thousand years old in its raw material and ninety in its modern form. Held up against a prime, it still throws a new shadow.

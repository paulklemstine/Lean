# The Sequence That Had No Choice

## A greedy game with a surprising ending

Imagine you are building a list of whole numbers one at a time, and you play by a single stubborn rule. You always want the next number to be as small as possible — but it is forbidden to pick any number that can be written as the sum of two numbers already on your list. You are running *away* from addition, refusing to ever land on a total you have already assembled. Where does this cautious, sum-avoiding walk take you?

At first the question feels open-ended, the kind of playful recipe that could spiral into chaos or wander unpredictably. Sequences defined by "pick the smallest number that avoids something" are famous for their wildness; they zig and zag, they hide patterns behind fractal-like irregularity, and they resist clean formulas. So the punchline here is genuinely satisfying: this particular game has **exactly one** possible outcome, and it is the simplest infinite list you could hope for.

Start at $1$. The next allowed number is $3$. Then $5$, then $7$, then $9$. The sequence is forced, step by step, to march through the odd numbers:
$$1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, \dots$$
and it can never deviate. There is no branching, no ambiguity, no second solution lurking somewhere. The rule that looked like it might permit many sequences permits precisely one.

## Where the puzzle comes from

This is a "repair" of an older idea. Number theorists love sequences built by *avoiding* structure — the most celebrated being the sum-free and Sidon sequences, and various "anti-Fibonacci" constructions. The Fibonacci numbers famously *embrace* addition: each term is the sum of the two before it. An anti-Fibonacci sequence flips the spirit: rather than building the next term out of sums, it *refuses* to be a sum.

But the original literal formulation of one such rule had a bug. As stated, it excluded exactly one integer and then quietly collapsed — every term after the start could just be the same number over and over, giving the boring constant sequence $1, 1, 1, \dots$. That is not a sequence with any life in it. The lesson learned was a small manifesto: **repair the definition before you study its asymptotics.** There is no point analyzing the long-run growth of a sequence whose rule is degenerate.

The repaired rule fixes two things at once. First, it insists that each new term be *strictly larger* than the current one, so the sequence genuinely moves forward and cannot stall. Second — and this is the heart of the repair — it forbids not just a single sum but *every* sum you can form from the numbers already generated. This is a **global additive** condition: at each stage, the entire history of the sequence contributes its pairwise sums to a growing list of banned values, and the next term must dodge all of them while staying above where you are.

## Why the answer is the odd numbers

The proof that this game has a unique solution is a small gem, and it rests on a fact every child rediscovers: **odd plus odd is even.**

Suppose your list so far consists entirely of odd numbers — which is certainly true at the start, when the list is just $\{1\}$. Any sum of two entries from your list is then a sum of two odd numbers, and every such sum is *even*. So the entire "forbidden zone" generated by addition consists of even numbers only. The odd numbers are completely untouched by the ban.

Now stand at the current term, say $2n+1$, and look for the smallest legal successor. You need something strictly bigger than $2n+1$ that avoids all those even forbidden sums.

- The very next integer, $2n+2$, is even — and worse, it is exactly $1 + (2n+1)$, the sum of your first term and your current term. It is banned.
- The integer after that, $2n+3$, is odd. Since only even numbers are ever forbidden, $2n+3$ is guaranteed to be allowed.

So the smallest legal move is always to jump by two, from one odd number to the next. And because every legal step preserves the "all entries are odd" property, the argument feeds itself forever. By induction, the list is *forced* to be the odd numbers, with no freedom at any stage.

There is a beautiful economy here. The single even number that would otherwise tempt you — the one sitting immediately above your current position — is precisely the one blocked by the sum $1 + (2n+1)$. The rule reaches back to the very first term, $1$, and uses it to veto the near miss at every single step. The seed of the sequence quietly polices its entire future.

## From an exponential fear to a linear law

Before this classification, the best that was known about the sequence was a crude *ceiling*: a bound saying the next term could not be too enormous. Such one-step bounds are often exponential and pessimistic — they tell you the sequence *might* explode, without telling you what it actually does. The repair replaces that fog with total clarity. The exact law is
$$a_n = 2n + 1,$$
as clean and linear as a formula can be. The successor is never a leap into the unknown; it is always exactly two more than the last:
$$a_{n+1} = a_n + 2.$$

From this single formula, everything else about the sequence falls out for free:

- **It is strictly increasing.** Each term is genuinely larger than the last — the sequence never repeats, never stalls, never doubles back.
- **Every term is odd.** The value $2n+1$ is odd by construction.
- **Its values are exactly the odd numbers.** Not merely a subset of the odds, but *all* of them: every odd number appears exactly once, and no even number ever does. The set of values the sequence visits is precisely $\{1, 3, 5, 7, \dots\}$.
- **It has perfect counting behavior.** The first $n$ steps produce exactly $n$ distinct values — no collisions, no waste.

And there is one more structural fact worth savoring: **rigidity**. The rule does not merely *permit* the odd numbers as one solution among many. It *demands* them. Any sequence obeying the repaired rule, no matter how you imagine it starting to wander, must coincide with $1, 3, 5, 7, \dots$ term for term. The space of solutions is a single point.

## The shape of a good repair

There is a broader moral in this little story, one that matters far beyond this particular sequence. Definitions are not sacred just because they are written down. A rule can *look* precise and still be broken — excluding the wrong thing, permitting a degenerate escape, collapsing to triviality. The instinct to immediately ask "how fast does it grow?" is premature if the object itself is ill-posed. First you make sure the object *is* what you meant; only then do you study how it behaves.

Here the repair transformed a collapsing, ambiguous recipe into something with a unique, fully understood answer. The global additive condition — banning *all* pairwise sums, not just one — is exactly strong enough to pin the sequence down, and the strict-increase condition is exactly enough to keep it alive. Weaker, and the sequence stalls; the two conditions together carve out a razor-thin path that only the odd numbers can walk.

## Why anyone should care

Sum-avoiding sequences are not a mere curiosity. The tension between a set and its own sumset — the collection of all pairwise sums of its members — sits at the center of additive combinatorics, the branch of mathematics that studies how addition interacts with sets of integers. Questions about sum-free sets, about how large a set can be while avoiding its own sums, about Sidon sets where all pairwise sums are distinct, ripple outward into coding theory, cryptography, and the design of error-correcting schemes.

This example is a perfect miniature of that whole world. It shows a set — the odd numbers — that is maximally allergic to its own additive structure: *no* odd number is ever the sum of two odds, so the odd numbers form a genuinely sum-free family under this greedy dynamic. And it shows how a greedy, myopic rule ("always take the smallest legal step") can nonetheless produce a globally perfect, fully rigid object. Greed usually leads to regret; here it leads, inevitably and beautifully, to the odd numbers.

Sometimes the deepest thing you can prove about a wandering process is that it never really had a choice at all.

# The French Knee Is Forty

## How a single number exposed a hidden law in the way machines read language

### A budget, a curve, and a number

Imagine you are given a long document and a strange instruction: *you may only look at a handful of words, and you must choose which ones before you understand the text.* Which do you pick? And how many do you need before you have captured, say, $98\%$ of what matters?

That question is not a riddle. It is the daily arithmetic of any system that reads with a limited memory. Every attention mechanism — the machinery that lets a modern language model decide which earlier positions in a text to consult — assigns a weight to each candidate position. Sort those weights from largest to smallest and you get a decreasing profile
$$w_0 \ge w_1 \ge w_2 \ge \cdots \ge 0, \qquad \sum_i w_i = 1 .$$
The **retained mass** of the top $k$ positions is the partial sum
$$R(k) = \sum_{i<k} w_i .$$
Fix a **gate** $\tau$ — a fraction of the total mass you insist on keeping, say $\tau = 0.98$ — and ask for the smallest number of positions that gets you there:
$$k^*(\tau) = \min\{\,k : R(k) \ge \tau\,\}.$$
This is the **knee**. It is the honest answer to "how much memory do I actually need?"

The knee is easy to define and surprisingly hard to predict. And it is not one number: it depends on *what you are reading*. Measured on the same architecture, at the same context length of $1024$ positions, at the same gate, the knee moves:

| domain | code | English prose | mathematics | German prose | French prose |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $k^*$ | $12$ | $20$ | $20$ | $24$ | $40$ |

Code is compact; English prose and mathematics need a little more; German prose a little more again. And then French prose needs **forty** — exactly double English.

That last number is the subject of this article, and the reason is not that $40$ is large. It is that $40 = 2 \times 20$ *exactly*, while $24 = 20 + 4$ *exactly*, and those two statements cannot both be instances of the same law.

### Two ways to make reading more expensive

There are two clean mechanisms by which a change of domain — a different language, a different tokenizer, a different writing convention — can push the knee outward. They look similar in a table of five numbers. They are completely different in structure.

**The additive mechanism: a delay.** Suppose a domain simply prepends $d$ positions that carry no information at all — boilerplate, markup, a segmentation quirk that splits one meaningful unit into several before anything informative appears. Formally, the delayed profile is
$$(\mathrm{delay}_d\, w)_i = \begin{cases} 0 & i < d,\\ w_{i-d} & i \ge d.\end{cases}$$
Then for every positive gate $\tau$,
$$k^*(\mathrm{delay}_d\, w,\ \tau) \;=\; d + k^*(w,\ \tau).$$
The knee moves by exactly $d$, no more, no less, at *every* gate. This is the "$+4$" story: German costs four extra slots, full stop.

**The multiplicative mechanism: a slower tail.** Now suppose the domain does not delay the profile but *flattens* it. Take a geometric profile with decay ratio $r$,
$$w_i = (1-r)\,r^i, \qquad R(k) = 1 - r^k,$$
and compare it with a profile that decays faster, at ratio $r^m$. The gate $\tau$ becomes a **tail budget** $t = 1 - \tau$: you need $r^k \le t$. Writing $k_{\mathrm{geom}}(r,t)$ for the least such $k$, the comparison is exact:
$$k_{\mathrm{geom}}(r^m, t) \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{k_{\mathrm{geom}}(r,t)}{m} \right\rceil .$$
A domain whose tail is the $m$-th root of another's pays an $m$-fold knee — up to a single rounding step. This is the "$\times 2$" story: French decays like the square root of English, so its knee is twice as long.

These two laws are not reparametrisations of one another, and one can prove it. For any genuine root tax with $m \ge 2$ and any fixed candidate shift $d$, there is a gate at which the additive prediction fails; indeed the gap between the two knees exceeds *every* bound as the gate tightens. Push $\tau$ toward $1$ and an additive tax stays a constant $d$ while a multiplicative tax grows without limit. **No fixed additive constant can describe a root tax.** So the five-domain table, if it is generated by a single mechanism, is telling us something we can test.

### The knee in closed form

Behind the integer knee lies a real number that is much better behaved. For a geometric profile,
$$\kappa(r,t) \;=\; \frac{\log t}{\log r}$$
is the **ideal knee** — the exact real cut-off at which the tail budget is spent. The integer knee is its ceiling,
$$k_{\mathrm{geom}}(r,t) = \left\lceil \kappa(r,t) \right\rceil, \qquad \kappa(r,t) \le k_{\mathrm{geom}}(r,t) < \kappa(r,t) + 1 .$$
The integer knee never lies to you by as much as one position.

The ideal knee makes the multiplicative law exact rather than approximate: $\kappa(r^m,t) = \kappa(r,t)/m$, with no ceiling in sight. And it yields something one can actually go and falsify. Take two domains with tail ratios $r_1$ and $r_2$. Their ideal knees are $\log t/\log r_1$ and $\log t/\log r_2$, and the gate-dependent factor $\log t$ *cancels*:
$$\frac{\kappa(r_1,t)}{\kappa(r_2,t)} \;=\; \frac{\log r_2}{\log r_1}, \qquad \text{independent of the gate } t.$$
**The tax ratio between two domains is a pure property of their tails.** It does not depend on how strict you are. If you measure the French-to-English knee ratio at gate $0.98$ and get $2$, you should get $2$ at gate $0.95$ and at gate $0.995$ too — and if you don't, the geometric picture is wrong. That is a prediction with teeth, and it costs nothing to test: the measurement is already being made.

### Forty, or thirty-nine

Here is where precision earns its keep. The observed table says French is exactly twice English. The root law says French is $m$ times English *up to a ceiling*. Ceilings hide things.

The sharp statement is a two-sided bound. If the fine profile has knee $B$ and the coarse one — taxed by an $m$-th power of the ratio — has knee $A$, then
$$B \;\le\; m\,A \;<\; B + m .$$
The multiplicative law holds with an error strictly smaller than the multiplier itself. That is as tight as an integer law can be, and it is enough to nail the case at hand. If English is the square of French ($m = 2$) and the English knee is $20$, then
$$B \le 40 < B + 2,$$
which forces $B \in \{39, 40\}$ and nothing else. The reported value of $40$ is correct *up to one position*, and the only thing that eliminates $39$ is the extra assumption that the French knee is even. That assumption is invisible on a coarse measurement grid: a grid containing $36, 40, 48, 56, 64$ can never distinguish $39$ from $40$, because it never tests $39$.

This is not pedantry; it is the shape of the honest claim. What the data support is "$39$ or $40$". What the headline says is "$40$". The gap between them is exactly one bit of information that the experiment, as designed, did not collect.

Grids in general are subtle in a way worth spelling out. If you only test cut-offs in a finite set $G$, the value you report is the least element of $G$ that clears the gate — which is the least element of $G$ *above the true knee*. A coarse grid therefore never underestimates the knee; it only ever rounds it up to the next tested point. Conversely, a failing test at $a$ and a passing test at $b$ bracket the truth: $a < k^* \le b$. In the French measurement, the retained mass was $0.9795$ at $k = 36$ (failing the gate $0.98$) and $0.9830$ at $k = 40$ (passing it), so rigorously
$$36 < k^*(0.98) \le 40 .$$
That is the whole content of the experiment, stated exactly: a window of four positions, closed at the top by $40$.

### Can one profile explain all five domains?

Suppose the five domains are not five unrelated systems but one underlying "master" profile, taxed differently in each. Concretely: there is a master decay ratio $r$ with master knee $B$, and each domain $j$ sees the ratio $r^{m_j}$ for some integer **tax exponent** $m_j$. By the root law, domain $j$ has knee $\lceil B/m_j\rceil$.

Can a single $B$ reproduce $12, 20, 20, 24, 40$? Yes. Take $B = 120$ with exponents $(10, 6, 6, 5, 3)$:
$$\lceil 120/10\rceil = 12,\quad \lceil 120/6\rceil = 20,\quad \lceil 120/5\rceil = 24,\quad \lceil 120/3\rceil = 40 .$$
It works perfectly, and $120$ is a satisfying answer because it is the least common multiple of $20$ and $24$ — the smallest master that divides *exactly* into every entry. For a while that looks like a discovery: the master knee is $120$, the tax exponents are canonical, the theory is complete.

It isn't. The exact-divisibility requirement was never part of the law. The law has a ceiling in it, and a ceiling does not need exactness. Drop the demand that $B$ be a common multiple and the same exponent vector works for a *smaller* master:
$$\lceil 118/10\rceil = 12,\quad \lceil 118/6\rceil = 20,\quad \lceil 118/5\rceil = 24,\quad \lceil 118/3\rceil = 40 .$$
The master knee $118$ reproduces the entire five-domain table with the same exponents, and an exhaustive check over every candidate below $118$ — allowing *arbitrary* exponents for each domain, not just the ones above — shows that no smaller master can do it. So $118$, not $120$, is the true minimum.

Better still, one can compute the full ambiguity. Each ceiling equation $\lceil B/m\rceil = v$ is not a point condition but an interval condition: it says precisely that
$$m(v-1) < B \le m v .$$
Intersecting the four intervals for the exponent vector $(10, 6, 5, 3)$:
$$110 < B \le 120, \quad 114 < B \le 120, \quad 115 < B \le 120, \quad 117 < B \le 120,$$
whose intersection is $\{118, 119, 120\}$. That is the exact answer: **a master knee reproduces the five-domain table with these exponents if and only if it is $118$, $119$, or $120$.**

The consequence is a genuine limitation, and it deserves to be stated plainly. Two systems — one with master knee $118$, one with master knee $120$ — produce *literally the same four table entries* under the same exponents, while having different master knees. The five-domain fingerprint does not determine the master profile. Any claim that a particular master knee or a particular exponent vector is canonical must come with an independent measurement; the table alone cannot supply it. This is a gauge freedom, not noise: no amount of care in measuring those five numbers will shrink $\{118,119,120\}$ to a point.

### When the law holds everywhere

There is one situation where the multiplicative story becomes completely rigid, and it requires no geometric assumption at all.

Suppose two profiles $A$ and $B$, both with strictly positive weights, satisfy the exact multiplicative law at *every* gate:
$$k^*(A,\tau) = m\,k^*(B,\tau) \qquad \text{for all } \tau > 0 .$$
Then their retention curves are related by a block dilation:
$$R_A(mk) = R_B(k) \qquad \text{for all } k \ge 1 .$$
In words: $A$ is $B$ stretched by a factor $m$, sampled at the multiples of $m$. Whatever $A$'s internal structure within a block of $m$ positions, the mass it has accumulated at the end of block $k$ is exactly the mass $B$ has accumulated after $k$ positions. Nothing was assumed about geometric tails, exponentials, or logarithms — only that the integer law holds at all gates. So "the tax is a clean multiplier" is not a soft statistical statement; it pins down the entire retention curve.

This is why the "$\times 2$ or $+4$" dispute matters beyond bookkeeping. If the tax is truly multiplicative for French, then the French retention curve is the English one dilated — the same information, spread over twice as many positions. If it is truly additive for German, then the German curve is the English one *shifted* — the same information, arriving four positions late. Those are different physical pictures of what a language does to attention. Dilation says the language spreads its signal thinner. Shift says it front-loads dead weight.

### Two tail classes, and why doubling the context tells them apart

All of the above lives inside one universe: profiles whose tails decay geometrically, for which the knee grows like $\log(1/t)$. There is a second universe. Consider the heavy-tailed profile
$$w_i = \frac{1}{(i+1)(i+2)}, \qquad R(k) = \frac{k}{k+1},$$
a telescoping series whose tail decays polynomially rather than exponentially. Its knee at tail budget $t$ is roughly $1/t$: to keep $99\%$ you need about $100$ positions, to keep $99.9\%$ about $1000$.

The two classes are not separated by a constant. They are separated by an unbounded factor: for every constant $C$, however large, and every geometric ratio $r$, there is a gate at which the heavy-tailed knee is at least $C$ times the geometric knee. Across tail classes, the "tax" is not a bounded multiplier at all — it is not any kind of fixed law.

This suggests the cleanest available experiment, and it uses machinery that already exists. Double the context length from $1024$ to $2048$ and re-measure. A geometric-tailed domain will move its knee by an *additive* constant; a polynomially-tailed one will move it by a *multiplicative* one. One doubling separates the classes. The conjecture worth testing is that every natural-language corpus at context $1024$ lives in the logarithmic class — and if some corpus turns out to be polynomial, the entire vocabulary of "the tax is $+4$" or "the tax is $\times 2$" collapses for that corpus, because no fixed tax exists.

### What survives

Strip away the narrative and four statements remain, each provable from the definition of the knee alone.

1. **There are exactly two clean tax mechanisms, and they are structurally distinct.** A delay of $d$ positions shifts the knee by exactly $+d$ at every gate. An $m$-th root of the decay ratio multiplies it by $m$ up to a ceiling. No fixed additive constant can imitate a root tax: the discrepancy is unbounded as the gate tightens.

2. **The multiplicative law is sharp, and it says "$39$ or $40$".** The fine and coarse knees satisfy $B \le mA < B+m$. With an English knee of $20$ and a square-root tax, the French knee is $39$ or $40$; only an additional parity assumption — which no coarse grid can verify — selects $40$.

3. **The five-domain table underdetermines the master.** The admissible master knees for the exponent vector $(10,6,5,3)$ are exactly $\{118,119,120\}$; $118$ is minimal over all exponent choices; $118$ and $120$ have identical fingerprints. The apparently canonical value $120$ is an artefact of imposing exact divisibility, which the law does not require.

4. **Exact multiplicativity at all gates is rigid.** It forces the taxed retention curve to be a block dilation of the untaxed one, with no geometric hypothesis anywhere.

The headline result — the French knee is forty — turns out to be a slightly-too-confident rendering of a genuinely sharp fact. It is forty, or it is thirty-nine, and the experiment that produced it could not tell. That is not a failure of the measurement. It is the measurement finally being precise enough that the theory can say where its own blind spots are, which is the point at which a number stops being a curiosity and starts being physics.

The next moves are clear and cheap: measure the same domains at a second gate to test whether the tax ratio is really gate-invariant; measure at a doubled context to sort the domains into tail classes; and, if one wants the master knee to mean anything, find an observable that distinguishes $118$ from $120$ — because the five-domain table never will.

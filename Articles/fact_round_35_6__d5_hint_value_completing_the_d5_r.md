# Half a Bit from a Pentagon: What a Quintic Whispers About the Factors of a Number

*How a fifth-degree polynomial, a pentagon's symmetries, and a little information theory combine into an exact law: the "dial" of $x^5+20x+32$ is worth precisely one half of one bit.*

---

## A number with a secret

Take two primes, say $p = 11$ and $q = 13$, and multiply them: $N = 143$. Hand $N$ to a friend. Factoring 143 is easy, but for numbers hundreds of digits long, recovering $p$ and $q$ from $N$ is the hard problem on which much of modern cryptography rests.

Here is a gentler question. Suppose your friend does not want the factors themselves, only one *fact* about them: a short label describing how a certain polynomial behaves modulo each factor. How much does the number $N$, seen only through a small window, tell her about that label? And how much more would she learn if, instead of $N$, she were allowed to look at the factors' sum and difference through the same window?

The gap between those two answers is what we call the **hint value**. This article is about one hint value, computed exactly, for one beautiful polynomial:

$$f(x) = x^5 + 20x + 32.$$

The answer turns out to be exactly $\tfrac12$ bit, and the reason is a pentagon.

## The polynomial that can be solved

In the nineteenth century Abel and Galois showed that there is no general formula, built from radicals, for the roots of a fifth-degree polynomial. But *some* quintics are solvable, and $x^5 + 20x + 32$ is one of the classical examples. Its roots can be written with nested square and fifth roots.

The reason is symmetry. Every polynomial has a **Galois group**: the group of permutations of its roots that respect all the algebraic relations among them. For a "generic" quintic this group is all $120$ permutations of five things. For $f$ it is only ten permutations, namely the symmetries of a regular pentagon. This is the **dihedral group** $D_5$: five rotations (including "do nothing") and five reflections.

Two quick facts make this plausible.

* **The discriminant is a perfect square.** For a trinomial $x^5 + ax + b$ the discriminant is $5^5 b^4 + 4^4 a^5$. With $a = 20$, $b = 32$ this is
$$5^5\cdot 32^4 + 4^4\cdot 20^5 = 4{,}096{,}000{,}000 = 64000^2.$$
A square discriminant means that only *even* permutations of the roots occur. The pentagon's symmetries are all even: a rotation is a 5-cycle, and a reflection swaps two pairs of vertices and fixes one.

* **There is exactly one real root.** The function $x^5 + 20x + 32$ is strictly increasing on the real line, and it changes sign between $f(-2) = -40$ and $f(-1) = 11$. So it has one real root, near $-1.364$, and four complex ones. Complex conjugation, which swaps the complex roots in two pairs and fixes the real one, is a symmetry with exactly one fixed point. In the pentagon picture it is a **reflection**.

## Reading a polynomial modulo a prime

Now choose a prime $p$ and count how many solutions $f(x) \equiv 0 \pmod p$ has among $x = 0, 1, \dots, p-1$. For $p = 7$ there are none; for $p = 11$ there is exactly one; for some rarer primes there are five.

These counts are not random. A deep theorem of Frobenius and Chebotarev says that each prime $p$ (apart from finitely many) comes with a "Frobenius" symmetry in the Galois group. The number of roots of $f$ modulo $p$ equals the number of roots that this symmetry fixes. In the pentagon:

| Frobenius symmetry | how many | fixed vertices | roots of $f$ mod $p$ | factorization pattern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| do nothing | 1 | 5 | 5 | five linear factors |
| nontrivial rotation | 4 | 0 | 0 | one irreducible quintic |
| reflection | 5 | 1 | 1 | linear times two quadratics |

Chebotarev's density theorem adds that, in the long run, every symmetry is equally likely to be the Frobenius. So the root counts $5, 0, 1$ should appear with frequencies $\tfrac1{10}, \tfrac4{10}, \tfrac5{10}$. Checking the 427 primes $7 \le p < 3000$ gives 32 primes with five roots, 174 with none and 221 with one: $7.5\%$, $40.7\%$ and $51.8\%$. That is close to the prediction, and *no prime ever has two, three or four roots.*

Call this root count the **label** $T(p)$ of the prime.

## The dial: a square root hiding inside

The pentagon group has one special subgroup: the five rotations. They form half of the group, and this half corresponds, through the Galois correspondence, to a single *quadratic* field hidden inside the field generated by the roots of $f$. Whether the Frobenius of $p$ is a rotation or a reflection is decided entirely by how $p$ behaves in that quadratic field. Quadratic fields are controlled by simple congruences.

So which quadratic field is it? Since complex conjugation is a reflection, the field must be *imaginary*. Of the candidate quadratic fields ramified only at $2$ and $5$ (the primes dividing the discriminant), exactly one matches the data: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$.

Its arithmetic is governed by the Legendre symbol $\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right)$, which is $+1$ if $-5$ is a square modulo $p$ and $-1$ otherwise. Quadratic reciprocity turns this into a rule that only looks at $p \bmod 20$:

> **The dial theorem.** For every prime $p \ne 2, 5$,
> $$\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right) = \chi_{20}(p), \qquad \chi_{20}(p) = \begin{cases} +1 & p \equiv 1, 3, 7, 9 \pmod{20},\\ -1 & p \equiv 11, 13, 17, 19 \pmod{20}.\end{cases}$$

*Why it is true.* Write $\left(\frac{-5}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)\left(\frac{5}{p}\right)$. The first factor is $+1$ exactly when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Because $5 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, reciprocity says the second factor equals $\left(\frac{p}{5}\right)$, which is $+1$ exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 5$. Multiply the two and you get the table.

We call $\chi_{20}$ the **dial** of $f$. The prediction is that $f$ has exactly one root modulo $p$ (a reflection) if and only if $\chi_{20}(p) = -1$. This holds for every one of the 22 primes $7 \le p < 100$, and for all 427 primes up to 3000.

Notice the number 20 here. Any window that is supposed to "see" the dial must be at least as fine as residues modulo 20. The experimental modulus $m^* = 320 = 16 \cdot 20$ is one such window.

## Two windows onto a semiprime

Now return to the semiprime $N = pq$. Imagine two observers, each looking through a window of size 320.

* The **product observer** sees only $N \bmod 320$.
* The **joint observer** sees the sum and gap of the factors, $(p+q) \bmod 320$ and $(q-p) \bmod 320$.

What can each one learn about the dial?

The joint observer is well served. From $s = p+q$ and $d = q-p$ modulo 320 she can recover $2p$ and $2q$ modulo 320, hence $p$ and $q$ modulo 160. Since 20 divides 160, she learns $\chi_{20}(p)$ and $\chi_{20}(q)$ *separately*.

The product observer learns less. The dial is multiplicative, $\chi_{20}(ab) = \chi_{20}(a)\chi_{20}(b)$, so $N \bmod 320$ tells her the product $\chi_{20}(p)\chi_{20}(q)$ and nothing more.

Here is a real pair of semiprimes that she cannot tell apart:

$$11 \cdot 13 = 143, \qquad 7 \cdot 569 = 3983 \equiv 143 \pmod{320}.$$

To the product observer these look identical. But $f$ has one root modulo 11 and one root modulo 13 (two reflections, $\chi_{20} = -1$ twice), and no root modulo 7 or modulo 569 (rotations, $\chi_{20} = +1$ twice). The joint observer sees sums $24$ and $256$ modulo 320 and separates the two at once. The product $(-1)(-1) = (+1)(+1)$ has hidden the difference.

## Measuring information in bits

To turn "learns more" into a number we use Shannon's information theory. If a random quantity takes values with probabilities $p_i$, its **entropy** $H = -\sum p_i \log_2 p_i$ measures, in bits, how uncertain it is. The **mutual information** $I(T; V) = H(T) - H(T \mid V)$ measures how much observing $V$ reduces our uncertainty about $T$.

The **hint value** compares the two observers:

$$\text{hint} = I\big(T;\ \text{joint view}\big) - I\big(T;\ \text{product view}\big).$$

It is the extra information about the factors' labels that the sum and difference reveal beyond the product.

Experiments in the thirty-fifth round of a long study of such "views" reported a hint value of $+0.6940$ bits for $f$ at modulus 320. The question was whether this number is a law of nature or an artifact.

## The pentagon gives an exact answer

To get an exact answer we use the idealised **Chebotarev model**: the Frobenius symmetries of $p$ and of $q$ are independent and uniform in the pentagon group. That gives $10 \times 10 = 100$ equally likely cases. The label $T$ is the *unordered* pair of root counts $\{T(p), T(q)\}$, since an observer is not told which factor is which. The two views, read through the dial, are the pair $(\chi(p), \chi(q))$ and the product $\chi(p)\chi(q)$.

Every quantity is now a finite calculation. The unordered labels occur with counts $1, 8, 16, 10, 40, 25$ out of 100, so their entropy is $\log_2 5 - \tfrac{9}{50} \approx 2.14$ bits. Working through the conditional entropies gives the central result.

> **The $D_5$ hint law.** In the Chebotarev model for $x^5+20x+32$:
> * the joint view carries $I(T; \chi(p), \chi(q)) = \tfrac32$ bits about the unordered label;
> * the product view carries $I(T; \chi(N)) = 1$ bit;
> * so the **hint value is exactly $\tfrac12$ bit.**
>
> If the label also records which factor is which, the joint view carries $2$ bits, the product view still $1$, and the hint value is exactly $1$ bit.

There is a pleasant way to see these numbers without any long computation.

**Step 1: the label knows the dial.** For a single prime, the root count determines the dial. One root means a reflection, so $\chi = -1$. Zero or five roots means a rotation, so $\chi = +1$. Whenever a quantity is a function of the label, the label carries all of its uncertainty. We call this the **pinned-dial law**: if $D = g(T)$ then $I(T; D) = H(D)$. At the prime level the dial is a fair coin, so the label carries exactly 1 bit about it.

**Step 2: the product row is pinned.** For a semiprime, $\chi(N) = -1$ exactly when one factor is a reflection and the other is not. That can be read off the unordered label, so by the pinned-dial law the product view carries $H(\chi(N)) = 1$ bit.

**Step 3: the pair row is almost pinned.** The unordered label determines the *unordered* pair of dial values. The ordered pair $(\chi(p), \chi(q))$ is two fair coins, worth 2 bits. Knowing the coins only up to swapping them loses information in just one situation: when the coins differ, which happens half the time, and then exactly one bit (which coin is which) is unknown. So the label captures $2 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac32$ bits of the pair. Equivalently, the unordered pair of two fair coins (both heads with probability $\tfrac14$, both tails $\tfrac14$, mixed $\tfrac12$) has entropy exactly $\tfrac32$.

**Step 4: subtract.** $\tfrac32 - 1 = \tfrac12$. With ordered labels the "which coin" ambiguity disappears and the answer is $2 - 1 = 1$.

## A dihedral law, not a quintic one

Nothing in Steps 1–4 used the number 5. It only used two facts: the reflections are exactly the symmetries with one fixed point, and they make up half the group. Both hold for the symmetries of *any* regular polygon with an odd number of sides.

To test this, do the same computation for the cubic $x^3 - 2$, whose Galois group is the symmetry group $D_3$ of a triangle (which is also the full permutation group $S_3$). Its label distribution is quite different, and its unordered label entropy, $\log_2 3 + \tfrac{13}{18} \approx 2.31$ bits, is strictly larger than the pentagon's $2.14$. Yet the hint values are identical: exactly $\tfrac12$ bit unordered and exactly $1$ bit ordered. The same numbers come out for heptagons, nonagons and hendecagons.

For an even polygon the law breaks. In a square, reflections fix either two vertices or none, so the label no longer determines the dial. In the analogous model for the square group $D_4$, the unordered hint value drops to about $0.32$ bits.

So the half bit belongs to the dial of a dihedral field of odd degree, not to the quintic as such.

## So what is $0.6940$?

The exact limit is $\tfrac12$ and the reported reading is $0.6940$. Is that a contradiction?

No. The reported value lies strictly between the unordered limit $\tfrac12$ and the ordered limit $1$, so it cannot be the ordered law, and it is consistent with a positive hint plus a finite-sample excess. Experiments with real prime pairs show where the excess comes from. Taking every pair of primes below a bound $B$ and estimating the information directly from frequencies gives:

| bound $B$ | prime pairs | estimated hint |
|---|---|---|
| 500 | 4,186 | $0.8557$ |
| 1,500 | 27,730 | $0.7363$ |
| 4,000 | 149,331 | $0.6135$ |

The readings fall steadily toward $\tfrac12$, and the reported $0.6940$ sits right inside this sequence. The product observer's estimates ($1.0228$, $1.0035$, $1.0006$) are already essentially at their limit of $1$. Her window has only 320 cells. The joint window has $320^2 = 102{,}400$ cells, and frequency-based estimates of information are known to be biased upward when there are many cells and few samples. The excess over one half is that bias, shrinking as the battery grows.

## Why it matters

There are three takeaways.

1. **Hidden structure becomes measurable.** A residue window "knows" about the roots of a quintic only through the quadratic character of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, a fact that reciprocity makes completely explicit. The view sees an abelian shadow of a non-abelian symmetry group, and no more.

2. **Exact limits discipline experiments.** Without the exact value $\tfrac12$, a reading of $0.69$ could be mistaken for a new constant. With it, the reading becomes a calibrated measurement of finite-sample bias.

3. **Universality.** The half bit is a dihedral invariant. Polynomials of degree 3 and 5, with very different statistics, give exactly the same hint. This suggests a classification of hint values by Galois group, a small "periodic table" in which this result fills the $D_5$ row.

A pentagon has five corners and ten symmetries. Viewed through a window of 320 residues, its secret about the factors of a number comes out to exactly half a bit.

# The Law of the Vanishing Hint

## What a product of two primes refuses to tell you about its factors

There is a very old game in number theory. You are handed a large integer $N$, you are told it is the product of two primes, $N = pq$, and you are asked to say something — anything — about $p$ and $q$. You are not allowed to factor it. All you may look at is $N$ itself.

At first this sounds hopeless, and for most questions it is. But not for all of them. Certain questions about a prime have answers that are determined entirely by the prime's remainder after division by some fixed number. The classical example is Fermat's: an odd prime $p$ is a sum of two squares exactly when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Nobody needs to search for the squares; the last two bits of $p$ decide the matter. Such properties are the ones that leak. If a property of $p$ is a function of $p \bmod f$, and a property of $q$ is the same function of $q \bmod f$, then $N \bmod f$ — which anyone can compute — knows something about both.

How much is "something"? That is the question this article answers, exactly, once and for all, for a large and natural family of these leaks. The answer is a single function of a single integer, and it is astonishingly small.

---

## Primes that split

The properties we care about come from algebraic number theory, but the idea can be stated without it. Fix a polynomial with integer coefficients — say $x^2 - x - 1$, whose roots are the golden ratio and its conjugate, or the cubic $x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$, or $x^4 - 4x^2 + 2$. For each prime $p$ ask: **does this polynomial factor completely into distinct linear factors modulo $p$?** When it does, one says that $p$ *splits completely* in the field generated by the polynomial's roots.

For the golden-ratio polynomial the answer is: yes exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 5$. For $x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$: yes exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 7$. For $x^4 - 4x^2 + 2$: yes exactly when $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{16}$. In each case a deep-sounding question about factorization of a polynomial collapses into a shallow question about a remainder — this is the phenomenon that reciprocity laws, from Gauss's quadratic reciprocity of 1801 through the cubic reciprocity of 1844 and beyond, were invented to explain.

For the fields where this collapse is cleanest — the *abelian* fields — the splitting condition is packaged by a single gadget called a **Dirichlet character**. A Dirichlet character of conductor $f$ is a map $\chi$ that assigns to each integer coprime to $f$ a complex root of unity, multiplicatively: $\chi(ab) = \chi(a)\chi(b)$. Its **order** $n$ is the size of the group of values it takes; the values are exactly the $n$-th roots of unity. For our purposes there is one thing to know:

> **The splitting rule.** For each of these fields there is a character $\chi$ of some order $n$ and conductor $f$ such that a prime $p \nmid f$ splits completely if and only if $\chi(p) = 1$.

Because the values of $\chi$ are equidistributed over the primes — this is Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, sharpened — the chance that a random prime splits is exactly $1/n$. For the golden-ratio field $n = 2$ and half of all primes split. For the cyclic cubic field $n = 3$ and one third split. For the quartic field $n = 4$, one quarter. And so on.

## The semiprime and its shadow

Now take a semiprime $N = pq$ with both primes coprime to $f$, and ask the natural leaky question:

> **Does at least one of $p, q$ split completely?**

Call this Boolean the **OR**. It is a statement about the hidden factors. What can the visible world see? Only $N \bmod f$, and through it only the number $\chi(N) = \chi(p)\chi(q)$ — the product of two hidden roots of unity. The multiplicativity of $\chi$ is a one-way mixer: it broadcasts the product and conceals the factors.

Here is the entire mathematical content of the leak, and it is a counting problem in a group of $n$ elements. Write $x = \chi(p)$ and $y = \chi(q)$; both range over the $n$-th roots of unity, independently and uniformly, and the observer learns $c = xy$.

* **Fibre count.** For each fixed value $c$, the number of pairs $(x, y)$ with $xy = c$ is exactly $n$: choose $x$ freely, and $y = x^{-1}c$ is forced. Every observed class is equally likely, and every class hides exactly $n$ possibilities.
* **The one-versus-two dichotomy.** Within that fibre, how many pairs make the OR true — that is, have $x = 1$ or $y = 1$? Exactly two candidates exist, $(1, c)$ and $(c, 1)$, and they coincide precisely when $c = 1$. So the count is
$$\#\{(x,y) : xy = c,\ (x = 1 \text{ or } y = 1)\} = \begin{cases} 1, & c = 1,\\ 2, & c \neq 1.\end{cases}$$

That single line — **one if the observed class is trivial, two otherwise** — is the entire engine. Everything else is arithmetic on top of it. Dividing by the fibre size $n$:

$$\mathbb{P}(\text{OR} \mid \chi(N) = 1) = \frac{1}{n}, \qquad \mathbb{P}(\text{OR} \mid \chi(N) \neq 1) = \frac{2}{n},$$

and, since the trivial class occurs with probability $1/n$,

$$\mathbb{P}(\text{OR}) = \frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\cdot\frac{2}{n} = \frac{2n-1}{n^2}.$$

The dichotomy is a genuine leak: the two conditional rates $1/n$ and $2/n$ differ by a factor of two. Seeing $\chi(N) \neq 1$ literally doubles the chance that one of the two hidden primes splits. Notice also the strange logic of it: if $\chi(N) \neq 1$ then it is *impossible* that both primes split, yet it is *twice as likely* that one of them does.

## Measuring the leak in bits

A factor of two in a conditional probability sounds like a lot. Information theory says otherwise, and it is the right judge, because it measures how much a leak actually helps someone who has to guess.

The standard yardstick is Shannon's **mutual information**: the average number of bits by which knowing one quantity reduces your uncertainty about another. For a Boolean event of probability $x$ the uncertainty is the binary entropy
$$H(x) = -x\log_2 x - (1-x)\log_2(1-x)\ \text{ bits},$$
one full bit at $x = 1/2$, zero at $x = 0$ or $1$. The information the observed class carries about the OR is the entropy of the OR minus its average entropy after the class is revealed. Feeding in the three probabilities above gives the central result of this work.

> **The OR-Collapse Law.** For a splitting event pinned by a character of order $n$, the information that the residue class of a semiprime $N = pq$ carries about the event "at least one of $p, q$ splits completely" is exactly
> $$g(n) \;=\; H\!\left(\frac{2n-1}{n^{2}}\right) \;-\; \frac{1}{n}\,H\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\right) \;-\; \frac{n-1}{n}\,H\!\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)\ \text{bits}.$$

Read the statement carefully, because what is *missing* from it is the point. The right-hand side does not mention the field. It does not mention the degree of the polynomial, the conductor $f$, whether $f$ is prime or a prime power or a product of two primes, whether the unit group is cyclic, or anything else about the arithmetic. **The order $n$ of the character is the only surviving parameter.** Two fields as different as the real subfield of the sixteenth cyclotomic field (degree $4$, conductor $16$, non-cyclic unit group) and any hypothetical quartic-character field of conductor $10^{100}$ leak *precisely* the same number of bits.

## How small is small?

Now the numbers. The first few values, in closed form and in bits:

| $n$ | closed form of $g(n)$ | bits |
|---|---|---|
| $2$ | $\tfrac32 - \tfrac34\log_2 3$ | $0.3113$ |
| $3$ | $\log_2 3 - \tfrac59\log_2 5 - \tfrac29$ | $0.0728$ |
| $4$ | $\tfrac{11}{4} - \tfrac{15}{16}\log_2 3 - \tfrac{7}{16}\log_2 7$ | $0.0359$ |
| $5$ | $\log_2 5 - \tfrac{6}{25}\log_2 3 - \tfrac{48}{25}$ | $0.0215$ |
| $8$ | $\tfrac{31}{8} + \tfrac{27}{64}\log_2 3 - \tfrac{15}{64}\log_2 5 - \tfrac{91}{64}\log_2 7$ | $0.0077$ |

A third of a bit at best; less than a hundredth of a bit by order eight. And the decay is not accidental. Two facts make it a theorem.

* **The universal cap.** For every integer order $n \geq 2$, $g(n) \leq g(2) = 0.3113\ldots$. No character-pinned splitting fork of this kind ever leaks more than a third of a bit about the OR of a semiprime's two factors — and it never leaks nothing, either: $g(n) > 0$ for all $n$.
* **The quadratic decay.** For every real $n \geq 2$,
$$\frac{0.08}{n^{2}} \;\le\; g(n) \;\le\; \frac{2}{n^{2}}, \qquad\text{and more sharply}\qquad g(n) \;\le\; \frac{1}{\log 2 \cdot (n-1)(2n-1)}.$$

There is a beautiful irony here. Increasing $n$ makes the prime-level fork *sharper*: with $n$ large, knowing $\chi(p)$ pins the splitting of $p$ almost perfectly, and the splitting event itself becomes a rare, highly informative $1/n$ coin. And yet the semiprime OR shows *less*, quadratically less. **The better the fork pins each prime, the more thoroughly the product hides the pair.** The mixing $\chi(pq) = \chi(p)\chi(q)$ eats the information faster than the sharpening supplies it.

## The exact constant of the collapse

Knowing that $g(n)$ decays like $n^{-2}$ raises the obvious question: like $n^{-2}$ *times what*? The answer is a clean universal constant, and getting it requires rewriting $g$ in a form where the cancellations are visible.

Mutual information is an average of Kullback–Leibler divergences — one for each observed class, measuring how far that class's conditional law is from the unconditional one. Carrying out that decomposition for the OR table and clearing denominators produces an exact identity, valid for every real $n \geq 2$:

$$n^{2} g(n)\log 2 \;=\; \underbrace{(n-1)\log\frac{n}{n-1} + \log\frac{n}{2n-1}}_{\text{class } \chi(N) = 1} \;+\; \underbrace{(n-1)(n-2)\log\frac{n(n-2)}{(n-1)^{2}} + 2(n-1)\log\frac{2n}{2n-1}}_{\text{classes } \chi(N) \neq 1}.$$

Each of the four terms is a product of something growing like a power of $n$ with a logarithm shrinking like a power of $1/n$, so each has a finite limit — and the limits are, in order,
$$1, \qquad -\log 2, \qquad -1, \qquad 1.$$
The first pair sums to $1 - \log 2$; the second pair sums to $0$. Dividing by $\log 2$:

> **The sharp constant.** $\displaystyle \lim_{n \to \infty} n^{2} g(n) \;=\; \frac{1}{\log 2} - 1 \;=\; 0.4426950\ldots$

So the leak is, asymptotically, exactly $0.4427/n^2$ bits — a number built from nothing but the natural logarithm of $2$. What is striking about the decomposition is *where* the surviving information lives. The nontrivial classes $\chi(N) \neq 1$, which are the overwhelming majority ($(n-1)/n$ of all cases), contribute $-1 + 1 = 0$ to the limit: their two terms cancel exactly. The entire asymptotic leak comes from the single rare class $\chi(N) = 1$, the one that occurs a $1/n$ fraction of the time and in which the OR is a genuinely different, halved-probability event. The many tell you nothing; the rare one tells you everything there is, and there is very little.

Pushing the same expansion one order further (writing each logarithm as its series in $1/n$) gives the refinement
$$n^{2} g(n) \;=\; \left(\frac{1}{\log 2} - 1\right) + \frac{1}{4\log 2}\cdot\frac{1}{n} + O(n^{-2}),$$
with $1/(4\log 2) = 0.3606737\ldots$; numerically, $n\big(n^2 g(n) - 0.4426950\big)$ equals $0.360674$ at $n = 10^6$, agreeing to six places.

## Two famous leaks, one law

Laws earn their keep by absorbing phenomena that looked unrelated. This one absorbs two.

The first is a classical-looking statistic about the multiplicative structure of $p - 1$: for a semiprime $N = pq$, how much does $N \bmod 3$ tell you about whether $3$ divides $p - 1$ or $3$ divides $q - 1$? Since $3 \mid p-1$ exactly when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 3$, this is the splitting condition for the quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, pinned by the quadratic character of conductor $3$ — order $n = 2$. The law predicts conditional rates $1/2$ and $2/2 = 1$, an unconditional rate $3/4$, and a leak of $g(2) = 0.3113$ bits. Direct measurement over tens of thousands of semiprimes gives $0.3126$ bits, with conditional rates $0.4942$ and $1.0000$. The rate of exactly $1$ is not a numerical fluke but a theorem in disguise: if $N \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ then $\chi(p)\chi(q) = -1$, so exactly one of $p, q$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$ — the OR is *certain*.

The second is a cubic phenomenon: the same question for the cyclic cubic field of conductor $7$, where the measured leak is $0.0704$ bits. The law says $g(3) = 0.0728$. Two statistics discovered independently, one quadratic and one cubic, differing by a factor of four in size, turn out to be the values at $n = 2$ and $n = 3$ of one function of one variable.

Systematic measurement across seven fields — spanning prime conductors ($5$, $7$, $11$), prime-power conductors ($9 = 3^2$, $16 = 2^4$), a composite conductor ($21 = 3\cdot 7$), degrees $2$ through $6$, and both cyclic and non-cyclic unit groups — reproduces the predicted values throughout:

| field | order $n$ | conductor $f$ | measured | $g(n)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x^2 - x - 1$, i.e. $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 5)$ | $2$ | $5$ | $0.3076$ | $0.3113$ |
| $x^3 + x^2 - 2x - 1$ (cyclic cubic) | $3$ | $7$ | $0.0704$ | $0.0728$ |
| $x^3 - 3x + 1$ (cyclic cubic) | $3$ | $9$ | $0.0735$ | $0.0728$ |
| $x^4 - 4x^2 + 2$ (real subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{16})$) | $4$ | $16$ | $0.0384$ | $0.0359$ |
| $x^5 + x^4 - 4x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x + 1$ (real subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{11})$) | $5$ | $11$ | $0.0222$ | $0.0215$ |
| $\Phi_7$, i.e. $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_7)$ | $6$ | $7$ | $0.0146$ | $0.0144$ |
| cyclic cubic, composite conductor | $3$ | $21$ | $0.0700$ | $0.0728$ |

Per-class conditional rates match $1/n$ and $2/n$ to within one or two percent everywhere. Conditioning on a modulus coprime to $f$ gives a flat, information-free table, exactly as the law demands: the leak lives in $N \bmod f$ and nowhere else.

## Why the leak cannot be cashed in

A third of a bit is not nothing. Could an attacker use it? Three structural facts say no, and they are consequences of the same counting that produced the law.

**It is symmetric.** The OR treats $p$ and $q$ identically, and so does $\chi(N)$. Whatever the class of $N$, the two primes are interchangeable in every conditional law, so the leak never distinguishes *which* factor splits. Measurement of that "which-factor" channel gives $0.0001$–$0.0002$ bits, i.e. noise. A hint that at least one of two indistinguishable objects has a property is not a lever for separating them.

**It is a residue dial, not a factoring oracle.** All the leak does is nudge a probability from $1/n$ to $2/n$. To turn probability into a factorization one needs a *witness*: an actual congruence satisfied by $p$ alone. The law's own fibre count forbids one — every observed class $c$ is compatible with exactly $n$ hidden pairs, all equally likely, for every field and every $n$.

**It is sealed behind the Chinese Remainder Theorem.** For composite conductors, the character factors across the prime-power components, and the leak in the whole is exactly the leak already present in the character's value — nothing new appears from the recombination. The measurement at conductor $21 = 3 \cdot 7$, which reproduces $g(3)$ and not something larger, is this phenomenon in the wild.

Every ingredient — quadratic reciprocity (1801), cubic and higher reciprocity (1844 onward), Dirichlet characters, the Chinese Remainder Theorem — is classical. The novelty is not a new tool but a sharp, closed accounting: this whole family of leaks has been measured, in bits, and found bounded by a third of a bit and decaying like $0.4427/n^2$. The line of attack that says "read the factors off the residue class" is now quantitatively closed at the semiprime level.

## The shape of the idea

Step back and the picture is simple, and not really about number theory at all.

You have two hidden labels drawn independently and uniformly from a group of $n$ elements. You are shown their product. You want to know whether at least one of them was the identity. The group's structure guarantees that the product is uniform and hides the pair inside a fibre of size $n$ — every class equally deep. The only crack is the coincidence $(1, c) = (c, 1)$ when $c = 1$: the OR event has one representative in the trivial fibre and two in every other. That asymmetry, one versus two, is worth $g(n)$ bits, and $g(n) \to 0$ like $0.4427/n^2$.

The number theory supplies the group and makes the labels observable at all — through reciprocity, through splitting of primes, through the equidistribution of character values. But the law itself is a statement about multiplication in a finite group and about how much a product can betray about its factors. That is why it is universal: change the field, change the degree, change the conductor, and you have merely relabelled the same group.

Little cracks like the one-versus-two dichotomy are how cryptographic assumptions are probed, and it matters to know their exact width. This one, it turns out, has been measured to the last decimal: at most $0.3113$ bits, asymptotically $0.4427/n^2$ bits, and never, ever enough.

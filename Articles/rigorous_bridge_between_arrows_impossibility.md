# Why Democracy Is Impossible — And What Geometry Has to Do With It

*A surprising connection between voting theory, curved space, and the shape of disagreement*

---

In 1951, a young economist named Kenneth Arrow proved something that sounded absurd: no voting system can be fair. Not "no voting system is perfect" — that's obvious. Arrow showed that any method for combining the preferences of three or more voters over three or more options must either ignore everyone except one person (a dictator) or violate basic principles of rationality and fairness.

The result, known as Arrow's impossibility theorem, earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics. For seven decades, it has been understood as a theorem about logic — a clever combinatorial argument showing that certain axioms are mutually contradictory. But what if Arrow's theorem is really a theorem about *geometry*? What if the reason democracy is impossible has nothing to do with logic and everything to do with the *curvature of space*?

## The Shape of Preferences

To see the connection, we need to think about preferences differently. Instead of ranking candidates — Alice first, Bob second, Charlie third — imagine expressing your preferences as probabilities. You might assign 60% to Alice, 30% to Bob, and 10% to Charlie. These numbers represent how strongly you feel about each option.

Now here's the key insight: any set of probabilities that adds up to 100% defines a point on a geometric object called the *probability simplex*. For three candidates, this simplex is a triangle. For four, it's a tetrahedron. Each voter's preferences correspond to a specific point inside this shape.

The question of aggregating preferences becomes a geometric question: given several points in the simplex (one per voter), how do we combine them into a single point (the social choice)?

## Enter the Sphere

The simplex is flat — its geometry is Euclidean. But it turns out that when we measure distances between probability distributions, the *natural* distance is not the flat, straight-line distance. It's something called the *Hellinger distance*, and it has a remarkable property.

There is a beautiful transformation, discovered independently by the statistician C. R. Rao and the information theorist S.-I. Amari: if you take the square root of each probability, the resulting vector lands on the *surface of a sphere*. A distribution like (0.64, 0.25, 0.09, 0.01, 0.01) becomes (0.8, 0.5, 0.3, 0.1, 0.1) — a point on a curved surface.

Under this transformation, the Hellinger distance between two probability distributions becomes the *angular distance* between two points on the sphere. The flat simplex, with its natural statistical metric, is actually *curved* — it's a piece of the sphere. And the sphere has *positive curvature*, meaning it bulges outward like the surface of the Earth.

## Curvature Creates Contraction

Here's where the magic happens. On a positively curved surface, averaging doesn't work the way you'd expect.

Imagine two cities on opposite sides of the equator. The "average" of their positions — the point equidistant from both — lies on the equator, which is *closer* to the North Pole than the average of their individual distances to the North Pole. This is the *contraction effect* of positive curvature: midpoints on a sphere are systematically pulled inward.

Now translate this to voting. If two voters have different preferences (different points on the sphere), any "averaging" rule — any rule that takes a middle ground between them — will systematically *contract* the range of opinions. Information is lost. Nuance is destroyed.

This isn't a flaw of any particular averaging method. It's a consequence of the geometry itself. Positive curvature means that the only operations on the sphere that never lose information are the *rigid motions* — rotations and reflections. But an aggregation rule that just rigidly moves one voter's position to the output is a *dictatorship*: it follows that one voter's preferences exactly.

## The Bridge

The connection is precise. Arrow's theorem requires three conditions:

1. **Unanimity**: If everyone agrees, so does society.
2. **Independence**: The social ranking of any two options depends only on how voters rank those two options.
3. **Non-dictatorship**: No single voter always gets their way.

Under these conditions, the *decisive coalitions* — groups of voters powerful enough to determine the outcome — form a mathematical structure called an *ultrafilter*. An ultrafilter on a finite set of voters has a remarkable property: it must be generated by a single voter. That voter is the dictator.

The geometric perspective explains *why* this happens. The decisive coalitions form an ultrafilter because the positive curvature of the preference space prevents any "genuine" combination of preferences. The curvature forces the aggregation to be trivial — to simply project onto one voter's preferences. The ultrafilter structure is the algebraic shadow of the geometric impossibility.

## Measuring the Impossible

This geometric viewpoint doesn't just explain Arrow's theorem — it *quantifies* it. We can measure how "impossible" a particular voting situation is by computing the *polarization index*: the average Hellinger distance between all pairs of voters.

When the polarization index is near zero — all voters agree — there's no problem. Any reasonable rule works. But as the index increases, the curvature contraction grows stronger, and Arrow-type impossibilities become more severe. The aggregation rule must sacrifice more and more fairness to produce a coherent output.

This gives us a concrete, computable number: the degree of democratic impossibility for any given electorate. It can be calculated from polling data. It predicts when compromises will break down and when institutions will be most stressed.

## A Deeper Unity

The Fisher-Rao sphere has curvature exactly K = 1 — the same as a standard sphere of radius 1. This isn't a coincidence. The Fisher information metric, which measures how distinguishable nearby probability distributions are, naturally curves the space of probabilities into a sphere.

The number 1 carries deep meaning. A flat space (K = 0) would allow perfect aggregation — democracy would be trivially possible. Negative curvature (K < 0) would create a different kind of impossibility, related to chaotic divergence rather than contraction. But the actual curvature is positive, and it's exactly the simplest positive value. Arrow's impossibility is not just a logical accident — it's encoded in the most fundamental metric of statistical inference.

The concavity of the cosine function on the relevant angular range [0, π/2] — the range where probability distributions in the positive orthant live — is the analytical engine of this contraction. The inequality cos((θ₁ + θ₂)/2) ≥ (cos θ₁ + cos θ₂)/2 says that the midpoint on the sphere is always closer to any reference point than the flat-space average would predict. This single inequality, applied to the angular coordinates of voter preferences, contains the geometric essence of Arrow's theorem.

## Beyond Arrow

This perspective opens new questions. What happens in spaces with different curvature? Can we design voting systems that exploit the specific curvature to minimize unfairness? Are there natural economic or biological aggregation problems where the curvature is different?

The answers may lie in information geometry — the field that studies the shapes of probability spaces. Every statistical model defines a curved space, and the curvature of that space determines what kinds of aggregation are possible. Arrow's theorem, seen through this lens, is not an isolated result in social choice theory but a special case of a universal principle: *positive curvature obstructs fair aggregation*.

The mathematics is crisp. The geometry is beautiful. And the implications reach far beyond voting — into machine learning (how should we combine predictions from multiple models?), into ecology (how do ecosystems aggregate individual organisms' strategies?), and into neuroscience (how does the brain combine conflicting sensory signals?). Wherever multiple probability distributions must be combined into one, the curvature of the space constrains what is possible.

Kenneth Arrow proved that democracy is impossible using logic. Seventy-five years later, we can see that he was really proving something about the curvature of the universe of beliefs. The impossibility of fair voting is written into the geometry of information itself.

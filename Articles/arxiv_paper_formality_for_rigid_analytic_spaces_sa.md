# The Shape of a Space, Written in Weights

## When multiplication remembers more than addition

Take any reasonable space — a sphere, a torus, a surface carved out by polynomial equations — and you can attach to it a sequence of vector spaces, its cohomology groups $H^0, H^1, H^2, \dots$. Their dimensions are the classical Betti numbers: how many independent loops, how many independent voids. But cohomology carries more than a list of dimensions. Classes can be multiplied. The cup product turns $H^\bullet = \bigoplus_n H^n$ into a graded ring, and that ring already distinguishes spaces that the Betti numbers alone cannot tell apart.

Even the ring, though, is a shadow. It is the shadow cast by a much richer object: a *differential graded algebra*, or dga. A dga is an algebra $A$ with a grading by degree, a multiplication that adds degrees, and a differential $d$ raising degree by one, squaring to zero, and satisfying a signed Leibniz rule
$$d(ab) = (da)\,b + (-1)^{|a|}\, a\,(db).$$
Cocycles are elements with $da = 0$; coboundaries are elements of the form $dc$. The quotient $H(A) = \ker d / \operatorname{im} d$ is the cohomology algebra. Every space has such a model — the de Rham complex of differential forms is the archetype — and the model contains strictly more information than its cohomology.

How much more? That is the question of **formality**. A dga $A$ is called *formal* if it can be connected to its own cohomology algebra $H(A)$, endowed with the zero differential, by a chain of maps each of which is an isomorphism on cohomology. Formality says: nothing was lost. The cohomology ring is a complete invariant of the algebraic homotopy type of $A$; the multiplication table of $H(A)$ determines everything.

When a space fails to be formal, the failure is visible, and it is beautiful.

## Massey products: the ghost in the machine

Suppose $x, y, z$ are cocycles whose classes satisfy $[x][y] = 0$ and $[y][z] = 0$ in cohomology. Then the products themselves are coboundaries: there exist $u$ and $v$ with
$$du = xy, \qquad dv = yz.$$
Now form the element
$$m = \pm\, u z - x v .$$
A one-line computation with the Leibniz rule shows $dm = 0$: it is a cocycle, of degree $|x|+|y|+|z|-1$. Its class depends on the choices of $u$ and $v$, so what is well defined is a coset — the *triple Massey product* $\langle x, y, z\rangle$. The cohomology ring cannot see it; two spaces with identical cohomology rings can have different Massey products. And here is the crucial dichotomy:

> **Formality kills Massey products.** If a dga is formal, then every triple Massey product that is defined contains $0$.

So a nonzero Massey product is a certificate of non-formality — a ghost that survives the passage to cohomology and testifies that the ring was not the whole story. The classical example lives on a three-dimensional nilmanifold, modelled by the algebra generated by $x, y, z$ in degree $1$ with $dx = dy = 0$ and $dz = xy$. Then $[x][y] = 0$, $[y][y]=0$, and the Massey product $\langle x, y, y\rangle$ is represented by $zy$, which is closed but not exact. The space is not formal.

## Enter arithmetic: weights

Now change worlds. Instead of a smooth manifold, consider a smooth proper *rigid-analytic space* over a finite extension of the $p$-adic numbers $\mathbf{Q}_p$ — the $p$-adic analytic geometry that governs, among other things, the local structure of arithmetic varieties. Such spaces also have cohomology algebras: étale cohomology, de Rham cohomology. Are they formal?

The remarkable answer is: **yes, provided the space satisfies the weight-monodromy conjecture.**

What is a weight? In arithmetic geometry, cohomology groups come with a second grading, invisible in topology, produced by the action of Frobenius or by the monodromy filtration. Each cohomology class carries not only a *degree* $n$ but a *weight* $w$, and the multiplication respects both: weights add exactly as degrees do. Cohomology is therefore bigraded,
$$H^{n} = \bigoplus_{w} H^{n,w}.$$
The weight-monodromy conjecture of Deligne, in the $p$-adic setting, says that this bigrading is as tight as it could possibly be: the weight of a class of degree $n$ is exactly $n$. Everything sits on the diagonal $w = n$. This condition has a name of its own — **purity**.

Purity looks like a statement about arithmetic. It turns out to be a statement about homotopy theory in disguise, and the translation is the subject of this article.

## The main theorem: purity forces formality

Here is the algebraic engine, stated for its own sake and proved without any geometry.

Let $k$ be a field and $A$ a $k$-algebra with a decomposition $A = \bigoplus_{(n,w) \in \mathbf{Z}^2} A^{n,w}$ into *bihomogeneous* pieces, with $A^{n,w} \cdot A^{n',w'} \subseteq A^{n+n', w+w'}$. Equip it with a $k$-linear differential $d$ that

* raises degree by one and **preserves weight**: $d(A^{n,w}) \subseteq A^{n+1,w}$;
* squares to zero: $d \circ d = 0$;
* obeys a Leibniz rule with an arbitrary nowhere-vanishing sign function $\varepsilon$: for $a \in A^{n,w}$ and any $b$,
$$d(ab) = (da)b + \varepsilon(n)\, a\,(db).$$

Call such data a **weight-graded dga**. (Allowing an arbitrary sign function rather than fixing $\varepsilon(n) = (-1)^n$ costs nothing and covers commutative, graded-commutative and sign-free conventions at once.)

Call the weight grading **pure** if, whenever $w \neq n$, every cocycle in $A^{n,w}$ is a coboundary — and moreover a coboundary of an element of the *same* weight $w$, necessarily in $A^{n-1,w}$. In words: all the cohomology sits on the diagonal.

> **Theorem (Purity implies formality).** A weight-graded dga whose weight grading is pure is formal. Explicitly, there is a subalgebra $A' \subseteq A$ closed under $d$, and a two-sided ideal $J \subseteq A'$ with $d(A') \subseteq J$, such that the zig-zag
> $$A \;\supseteq\; A' \;\twoheadrightarrow\; A'/J$$
> consists of maps inducing isomorphisms on cohomology, and $A'/J$ carries the **zero** differential. Hence $A'/J$ is the cohomology algebra of $A$, and $A$ is quasi-isomorphic to it.

The construction of $A'$ is the heart of the matter, and it is disarmingly simple: *truncate weight by weight*. Fix a weight $w$ and look at the subcomplex $A^{\bullet, w}$ — this is a genuine complex, because $d$ preserves weight. Keep

* everything in degrees $n < w$,
* only the cocycles in degree $n = w$,
* nothing in degrees $n > w$.

That is the canonical truncation $\tau_{\le w}$ of the weight-$w$ strand. Let $A'$ be the sum over all $w$ of these truncations. Let $J$ be the same thing with the diagonal contribution shrunk from cocycles to coboundaries: everything in degrees $n<w$, and in degree $n = w$ only the elements $dc$ with $c \in A^{w-1,w}$.

Two things must be checked, and both come down to the arithmetic of the inequality $n \le w$. First, that $A'$ is closed under multiplication and $J$ absorbs it: if $a$ lives at $(n,w)$ with $n \le w$ and $b$ at $(n',w')$ with $n' \le w'$, then $ab$ lives at $(n+n', w+w')$ and $n+n' \le w+w'$ — the region is closed under addition, and the boundary case $n = w$, $n'=w'$ is exactly where the Leibniz rule shows a product of cocycles is a cocycle. Second, that the two maps are quasi-isomorphisms. The inclusion $A' \subseteq A$ loses no cohomology: a cocycle of $A$ decomposes into bihomogeneous components, each of which is a cocycle; a component at $(n,w)$ with $n > w$ is off-diagonal, hence by purity a coboundary and thus invisible in cohomology, while all components with $n \le w$ already lie in $A'$. And the ideal $J$ is acyclic — a cocycle in $J$ is a coboundary of an element of $J$ — again because everything off the diagonal is exact, by purity.

The result is what one might call *strict* formality: not an abstract chain of quasi-isomorphisms through auxiliary objects, but two explicit maps, one an inclusion of a subalgebra, one a quotient by an ideal.

## A sharper picture: the diagonal subalgebra

Purity does something even more concrete. Consider the set of **diagonal cocycles**: elements that are bihomogeneous of bidegree $(n,n)$ — weight equal to degree, precisely the shape weight-monodromy predicts — and killed by $d$. Call the span of all of them $A^{\mathrm{diag}}$.

> **Theorem (Strict multiplicative lift).** $A^{\mathrm{diag}}$ is a subalgebra of $A$ containing $1$; the differential vanishes identically on it; and if the weight grading is pure, every cohomology class of $A$ has a representative in $A^{\mathrm{diag}}$. Consequently the cohomology algebra of $A$ is a quotient of the honest subalgebra $A^{\mathrm{diag}} \subseteq A$, on which $d$ is identically zero.

That it is a subalgebra follows because bidegrees $(n,n)$ and $(m,m)$ multiply to $(n+m, n+m)$, and products of cocycles are cocycles. That it hits every class is purity again: split a cocycle into bihomogeneous components, keep the diagonal ones, and observe that the off-diagonal components are all coboundaries — indeed a *single* coboundary, since a finite sum of coboundaries is one.

This is as strict as a formality statement can get. Ordinarily "the cohomology algebra is a subquotient with zero differential" is a wish; under purity it is literally true, with $A^{\mathrm{diag}}$ the subalgebra and its intersection with the coboundaries the ideal.

Making that last phrase precise requires knowing that cocycles multiply to cocycles and that coboundaries form a two-sided ideal among them: if $da = 0$ then $a\,dc$ is again a coboundary, and $(dc)\,b = d(cb)$ when $db = 0$. Both are consequences of the Leibniz rule — but note that the Leibniz rule is only available for *bihomogeneous* left factors, so both statements must be proved by decomposing into components and reassembling. That is exactly what the bigrading is for.

## The weight excess: why Massey products cannot survive

Now the two threads meet. Take diagonal cocycles $x, y, z$ of degrees $p, q, r$ — so of weights $p, q, r$ as well — with $xy$ and $yz$ exact. Choose primitives $u, v$ with $du = xy$, $dv = yz$, and take them bihomogeneous, which is legitimate because $d$ preserves bidegree up to the shift $(n,w) \mapsto (n+1,w)$. Then

* $xy$ has bidegree $(p+q, p+q)$, so $u$ has bidegree $(p+q-1,\, p+q)$;
* $yz$ has bidegree $(q+r, q+r)$, so $v$ has bidegree $(q+r-1,\, q+r)$.

Each primitive sits one step *off* the diagonal: its weight exceeds its degree by exactly one. Multiply back: the Massey representative $m = \varepsilon(p)\, uz - xv$ has degree $p+q+r-1$ but weight $p+q+r$. Its weight exceeds its degree by one. It is a cocycle. And purity says every off-diagonal cocycle is a coboundary.

> **Theorem (Purity kills triple Massey products).** In a pure weight-graded dga, if $x, y, z$ are diagonal cocycles with $xy$ and $yz$ exact, then there are primitives $u,v$ for which the Massey representative $\varepsilon(p)\,uz - xv$ is exact. Every triple Massey product of diagonal classes contains $0$.

Read the argument again and notice how little it uses: no spectral sequences, no obstruction theory, just a count. Define the **weight excess** of a bihomogeneous element as weight minus degree. Products add excess; taking a primitive raises excess by exactly one. Diagonal classes have excess $0$; each of the two primitives used in a triple Massey product contributes $+1$, and the two multiplications by diagonal classes contribute nothing — except that the representative has one degree fewer than the product of the three, so the net excess is $+1$. Purity annihilates anything with nonzero excess. The excess is a defect counter, and once it has been raised it can never come back down.

Turning the statement around gives the obstruction:

> **Theorem (Non-formality obstructs purity).** If a dga carries a genuinely non-vanishing triple Massey product of diagonal classes — no choice of primitives makes the representative exact — then no pure weight grading exists on it.

This is the algebraic shadow of the second half of the geometric story: there exist smooth proper rigid-analytic *surfaces* whose cohomology algebras are **not** formal. Their non-formality is detected by Massey products, and the theorem above says that such a space cannot carry the pure weight structure that weight-monodromy would supply. Formality is not a theorem about all rigid-analytic spaces; it is exactly a theorem about the ones weight-monodromy reaches.

## Twists, and why the normalisation does not matter

Arithmetic normalises weights in more than one way. A Tate twist doubles them; the interesting weight of $H^n$ is sometimes $2n$ rather than $n$; a comparison isomorphism may rescale everything by a fixed positive factor. It would be unpleasant if formality depended on a convention.

It does not. Fix any additive map $\mathrm{wt} : \mathbf{Z} \to \mathbf{Z}$ with $\mathrm{wt}(1) = \alpha > 0$, so $\mathrm{wt}(n) = \alpha n$, and call a weight-graded dga *pure along $\mathrm{wt}$* if there is no cohomology in bidegree $(n,w)$ unless $w = \alpha n$.

> **Theorem (Formality for arbitrary normalisations).** For any $\alpha > 0$, a weight-graded dga pure along $\mathrm{wt}(n) = \alpha n$ is formal, via the same strict zig-zag $A \supseteq A' \twoheadrightarrow A'/J$, where $A'$ is now the truncation along the line $w = \alpha n$. Triple Massey products of classes on that line again contain $0$. For $\alpha = 1$ this is the statement above.

One subtlety appears when $\alpha > 1$: the line $w = \alpha n$ misses most weights entirely. If $w$ is not a multiple of $\alpha$, the entire weight-$w$ subcomplex is acyclic and the truncation must discard it. The bookkeeping therefore indexes the pieces by pairs (degree, position along the line) rather than by bidegrees — a small change that makes the construction uniform in $\alpha$. The Massey argument survives verbatim, with the excess counted along the line: the representative has degree $p+q+r-1$ and weight $\alpha(p+q+r)$, and since $\alpha$ is positive, $\alpha(p+q+r) \neq \alpha(p+q+r-1)$, which is all that purity needs.

## Is purity a real hypothesis?

A conditional theorem is worth only as much as its hypothesis. Two examples settle the question, and they are as simple as one could hope.

For **consistency**, take the group algebra $k[\mathbf{Z}]$ — Laurent polynomials $k[X, X^{-1}]$ — bigraded by placing $X^n$ in bidegree $(n,n)$, with zero differential. This is a weight-graded dga; it is pure, because there is literally nothing off the diagonal to obstruct purity; and it is not degenerate, since in every degree $n$ the class of $X^n$ is a nonzero cohomology class which is not a coboundary. So the hypothesis of the formality theorem is satisfiable by objects with large cohomology algebras, not only by trivial ones.

For **independence**, take $k[\mathbf{Z}^2]$ bigraded tautologically — the basis element indexed by $(n,w)$ sits in bidegree $(n,w)$ — again with zero differential. Purity fails: the element indexed by $(0,1)$ is a nonzero cocycle in bidegree $(0,1)$, off the diagonal, and it is not a coboundary. So purity is a genuine restriction and not a theorem, and the conditional formality result is not secretly unconditional.

## What it means

Strip away the geometry and the theorem reads: *a second grading that pins the first one down forces all higher structure to collapse.* Formality is usually delicate, proved with hard analysis (Kähler identities and the $\partial\bar\partial$-lemma for compact Kähler manifolds) or with hard arithmetic (weights in the $\ell$-adic setting). What the argument above isolates is that the essential input is neither analysis nor arithmetic but a numerical coincidence: **degree equals weight, on the nose**. Once you have that, one canonical truncation and one addition of integers do the rest.

That is also why the counterexamples are so informative. A non-formal rigid-analytic surface is not merely a space where a proof technique fails; it is a space carrying a cohomology class — a Massey product — whose very existence is incompatible with the diagonal shape that weight-monodromy predicts. Formality and purity are two names for the same phenomenon, seen from homotopy theory and from arithmetic respectively, and Massey products are the currency in which the difference is measured.

Between the two lies a clean and testable statement, applying equally to $p$-adic geometry, to complex geometry with its Hodge weights, and to any other situation where a space's cohomology comes with a second grading that happens to line up with the first.

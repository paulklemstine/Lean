# A Grammar for Extraordinary Numbers

## What can a formula hide?

Some real numbers arrive with familiar names: $0$, $1$, and the rational numbers built as fractions. Others are defined by operations. Start with a rational number, apply the exponential function, take a logarithm, add two results, or multiply them, and a vast landscape of constants appears.

One especially vivid inhabitant is

$$
\exp(\exp(1))+\log 2.
$$

Numerically, it is about $15.84741$. That decimal is easy to approximate. Its arithmetic nature is not. Could it satisfy a nonzero polynomial equation with rational coefficients? If so, it would be **algebraic**. If not, it would be **transcendental**. The distinction sounds simple, but nested exponential and logarithmic expressions sit close to the frontier of modern number theory.

The central story here has two threads. The first asks how algebraic independence can force a concrete sum to be transcendental. The second asks whether multiplication genuinely enlarges a language already equipped with exponentials, logarithms, and addition. Both conclusions are conditional: they follow from an explicitly stated strengthening of Schanuel’s conjecture. Keeping that condition visible is not a weakness. It is the map legend that tells us exactly which territory is established and which bridge remains conjectural.

## Numbers generated by a grammar

Rather than collecting constants one at a time, imagine a small grammar. Its raw ingredients are rational constants and a distinguished variable $t$. From existing expressions we may form their exponential, logarithm, sum, or product. Call these **rational EML expressions**, where EML abbreviates exponential–multiplicative–logarithmic. To turn an expression into a number, evaluate it at $t=0$. The set of all resulting real values is the class of **EML numbers**.

There is a leaner grammar obtained by forbidding multiplication nodes. It still permits rational constants, $t$, exponentiation, logarithms, and addition. Call its expressions **rational EL expressions** and their values at $t=0$ **EL numbers**.

This syntactic distinction is precise even when familiar identities blur it semantically. For positive $a$ and $b$, one may write $ab=\exp(\log a+\log b)$, suggesting that multiplication is dispensable. But logarithms on the real line complicate this slogan: signs, zeros, domains, and nested expressions all matter. Therefore the claim that every EML expression can be replaced by an EL expression with the same value is substantial, not a routine rewriting trick.

One inclusion needs no conjecture. Every EL expression is already an EML expression because the larger grammar allows every constructor of the smaller one. Consequently,

$$
\mathrm{EL}\subseteq \mathrm{EML}.
$$

The number $\exp(\exp(1))+\log 2$ belongs to the EML class directly: begin with rational constants $1$ and $2$, exponentiate $1$ twice, take the logarithm of $2$, and add.

## The conjectural engine

Schanuel’s conjecture is one of transcendence theory’s great organizing principles. In its classical real form, take real numbers $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ that are linearly independent over $\mathbb Q$. The conjecture predicts that the field generated by

$$
z_1,\ldots,z_n,\exp(z_1),\ldots,\exp(z_n)
$$

has transcendence degree at least $n$ over $\mathbb Q$. In plain language, rational linear independence among the inputs should force abundant algebraic independence among the inputs and their exponentials.

The results discussed here use a **functional EML strengthening** with three explicit clauses:

1. the classical real Schanuel conjecture;
2. algebraic independence over $\mathbb Q$ of the particular pair
   $$
   \exp(\exp(1))\quad\text{and}\quad\log 2;
   $$
3. multiplication elimination: every rational EML expression has a rational EL expression that defines exactly the same real-valued function for every real input.

The second and third clauses are not silently presented as consequences already derived from classical Schanuel. They are additional predictions. This separation matters. It prevents a conditional theorem from wearing the costume of an unconditional one, and it isolates two sharply defined targets for future work.

## Why independence makes the sum transcendental

The key algebraic fact is elementary in conception and powerful in use.

**Sum Theorem.** If two real numbers $x$ and $y$ are algebraically independent over $\mathbb Q$, then $x+y$ is transcendental over $\mathbb Q$.

Algebraic independence means that no nonzero polynomial $P(X,Y)$ with rational coefficients satisfies $P(x,y)=0$. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $x+y$ were algebraic. Then some nonzero one-variable polynomial $p(T)\in\mathbb Q[T]$ would satisfy $p(x+y)=0$. Substitute $X+Y$ for $T$ and define

$$
Q(X,Y)=p(X+Y).
$$

This gives $Q(x,y)=0$. The only possible escape would be for $Q$ to be the zero polynomial. But substitution by $X+Y$ is injective: setting $Y=0$ recovers $p(X)$. Thus $Q$ is nonzero whenever $p$ is nonzero, contradicting algebraic independence.

Apply this theorem to

$$
x=\exp(\exp(1)),\qquad y=\log 2.
$$

The second clause of the functional EML strengthening says exactly that this pair is algebraically independent. Therefore:

**Conditional Transcendence Theorem.** Under the functional EML strengthening of Schanuel’s conjecture,

$$
\exp(\exp(1))+\log 2
$$

is transcendental over $\mathbb Q$.

The word “conditional” is indispensable. Present transcendence methods do not supply this conclusion unconditionally. A decimal calculation, however accurate, cannot decide transcendence; algebraic numbers and transcendental numbers are both dense in the real line. The theorem instead exposes the exact independence statement from which the conclusion follows.

## When multiplication disappears

The third clause of the same hypothesis addresses the expressive power of the two grammars.

**Conditional Multiplication-Elimination Theorem.** Assume every rational EML expression can be replaced by a rational EL expression with the same value for every real input. Then every EML number is an EL number.

The proof follows the definitions. If $a$ is an EML number, some rational EML expression $E(t)$ satisfies $E(0)=a$. Multiplication elimination supplies a rational EL expression $F(t)$ such that $F(t)=E(t)$ for every real $t$. In particular, $F(0)=E(0)=a$, so $a$ is an EL number.

Combine this difficult conditional inclusion with the automatic inclusion $\mathrm{EL}\subseteq\mathrm{EML}$:

**Conditional Class-Equality Theorem.** Under the functional EML strengthening of Schanuel’s conjecture,

$$
\mathrm{EML}=\mathrm{EL}.
$$

This is a statement about representation, not merely cardinality. The EML grammar visibly contains an extra constructor—multiplication—yet the hypothesis implies that the extra syntax creates no new closed values. Even more strongly, the elimination clause preserves entire functions, not only values at $0$.

There is a useful analogy with programming languages. One language may contain a primitive instruction that another lacks. If every program using that instruction can be translated into a program without it while preserving behavior on every input, the primitive increases convenience but not expressive power. Here the “programs” are mathematical expressions and the behavior is a real-valued function.

## Numerical experiments: illuminating but not proving

A short computation can evaluate the featured constants:

$$
\exp(1)\approx 2.718281828459045,
$$

$$
\exp(\exp(1))\approx 15.154262241479264,
$$

$$
\log 2\approx 0.6931471805599453,
$$

and hence

$$
\exp(\exp(1))+\log 2\approx 15.84740942203921.
$$

Numerics can also search for small integer polynomials that nearly vanish at this value. Such searches are useful diagnostics, but a near-zero polynomial value is not an algebraic relation. Floating-point error and accidental approximation can produce deceptive coincidences. Conversely, failing to find a relation within a finite search proves nothing about all polynomials. The conceptual theorem bypasses this trap: algebraic independence rules out every polynomial relation at once.

Expression trees offer another concrete viewpoint. The featured number has a tree with an addition at the root. Its left branch contains two nested exponentials applied to $1$; its right branch contains a logarithm applied to $2$. Counting operations measures syntactic complexity, while evaluating the tree measures semantic value. Multiplication elimination asks for a semantics-preserving transformation between classes of trees.

## What has really been learned?

Three lessons emerge.

First, algebraic independence is stronger than separate transcendence. Knowing that $x$ and $y$ are each transcendental does not prevent $x+y$ from being algebraic: take any transcendental $x$ and set $y=1-x$. Algebraic independence prevents coordinated cancellation because it forbids every nonzero polynomial relation in two variables.

Second, conditional mathematics can be structurally exact. The concrete transcendence theorem uses the pair-independence clause; the class-equality theorem uses multiplication elimination; and the easy EL-to-EML inclusion uses no conjecture at all. The assumptions are modular, so progress on one clause immediately clarifies which conclusions survive.

Third, syntax and semantics should not be confused. An EML expression may visibly use multiplication while an equivalent EL expression, if it exists, may encode the same function through a subtler composition of exponentials, logarithms, and additions. The class theorem concerns represented values, while the elimination hypothesis concerns extensional equality of represented functions.

## The road ahead

The most immediate problem is to determine how much of the added hypothesis follows from classical Schanuel. Does classical Schanuel force algebraic independence of $\exp(\exp(1))$ and $\log 2$, or is that genuinely stronger? A related target is separate transcendence of each generator under classical Schanuel, followed by the harder question of joint independence.

Multiplication elimination deserves its own structural principle, perhaps an exponential-algebraic closedness assumption that can be stated and tested independently. The grammar can also be expanded from one distinguished variable to finitely many variables, turning equality of number classes into equality of represented-function classes. Finally, the sum theorem points to a broad generalization: every nonzero rational linear combination of a finite algebraically independent family should be transcendental.

The decimal $15.84740942203921\ldots$ is therefore only the visible tip of the story. Beneath it lies a hierarchy of expression languages, a conjectural bridge from linear independence to transcendence degree, and a simple substitution argument that converts two-variable independence into a one-variable transcendence result. The formula is compact. The architecture around it reveals why deciding what kind of number it denotes remains so profound.

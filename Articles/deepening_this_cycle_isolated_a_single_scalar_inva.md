# One Number That Knows the Shape of a Sequence

## The hidden thermostat inside a recurrence

Some of the most famous number sequences in mathematics are born the same way: each new term is built from the ones before it by a simple rule. The Fibonacci numbers $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, \dots$ add the two previous terms. The Catalan numbers $1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, \dots$, which count everything from balanced parentheses to triangulations of a polygon, are generated by multiplying the previous term by a slowly changing fraction. Factorials, binomial coefficients, and the counts behind countless combinatorial problems all share this DNA.

This article is about a large and natural family of such rules — the **first-order multiplicative recurrences** — and a single number, hiding inside the rule's coefficients, that turns out to *dictate the entire personality of the sequence it generates*. We call it the **Möbius discriminant**, and its story is a small parable about how one scalar can control a great deal of structure.

Consider a rule of the form
$$
(\alpha n + \beta)\, a(n+1) = (\gamma n + \delta)\, a(n).
$$
Here $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ are fixed numbers, $n$ is the step index $0, 1, 2, \dots$, and $a(n)$ is the sequence being generated. Reading it aloud: to get the next term, take the current term, multiply by $(\gamma n + \delta)$, and divide by $(\alpha n + \beta)$. The multiplier is a *ratio of two straight lines in $n$* — a Möbius-like transformation of the index. That is where the name comes from.

Almost every classical combinatorial sequence fits this template. For the Catalan numbers, $C(n+1) = \frac{2(2n+1)}{n+2} C(n)$, so the rule reads $(n+2)\,C(n+1) = (4n+2)\,C(n)$ with $\alpha = 1, \beta = 2, \gamma = 4, \delta = 2$. Factorials use $a(n+1) = (n+1)\,a(n)$. Binomial rows, ratios of Gamma functions, hypergeometric term sequences — all of them.

Now form the single number
$$
\Delta \;=\; \gamma\beta - \alpha\delta.
$$
This is a determinant: it is exactly the cross-difference of the coefficients, the same combination you would compute to test whether the two lines $\gamma n + \delta$ and $\alpha n + \beta$ are proportional. When $\Delta = 0$ the two lines are proportional and the multiplier is constant — the sequence is a plain geometric progression. When $\Delta \neq 0$, something more interesting happens, and its **sign** turns out to be a master switch.

## What the sign of one number decides

The first surprise is a clean, exact identity. Look at the *ratio of consecutive terms*, $r(n) = a(n+1)/a(n)$ — the local "growth factor" of the sequence. How does this growth factor itself change from one step to the next? The answer is astonishingly tidy:
$$
\frac{a(n+2)}{a(n+1)} - \frac{a(n+1)}{a(n)} \;=\; \frac{\Delta}{(\alpha n + \beta)\,(\alpha(n+1) + \beta)}.
$$
The entire change in the growth factor is $\Delta$ divided by a product of two positive denominators. So long as those denominators stay positive — which they do for every classical sequence — the *sign* of this change is nothing but the sign of $\Delta$. One number, computed once from the coefficients, tells you whether the growth factor is speeding up or slowing down at every single step.

From this local fact springs a cascade of global structure.

**Monotone growth factors.** If $\Delta > 0$, the growth factor $a(n+1)/a(n)$ is *strictly increasing* for all $n$ — the sequence accelerates, forever. If $\Delta < 0$, the growth factor is *strictly decreasing* — the sequence decelerates. This is not an eventual or approximate statement; it holds from the very first step and never wavers. The Catalan numbers have $\Delta = \gamma\beta - \alpha\delta = 4\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 2 = 6 > 0$, and indeed their growth factors $1, 2, 2.5, 2.8, 3, \dots$ climb steadily toward $4$.

**Log-convexity of every order.** The monotone growth factor is really a statement about *log-convexity* — the logarithms of the terms curve upward like a bowl. And it upgrades to something far stronger than a statement about neighbours. For $\Delta > 0$ and *any* two indices $i < j$, no matter how far apart,
$$
a(i+1)\,a(j) \;<\; a(i)\,a(j+1).
$$
These are the **Turán inequalities**, famous from the theory of orthogonal polynomials and special functions. Choosing $j = i+1$ recovers the familiar three-term inequality $a(i+1)^2 < a(i)\,a(i+2)$; but the general statement, holding across arbitrary gaps, is genuinely stronger. It says the sequence is log-convex not just locally but *globally, at every scale*. When $\Delta < 0$, every one of these inequalities reverses, and the sequence is globally log-concave. A single scalar sign toggles the entire two-parameter family of cross-index inequalities.

**An exact closed form.** Finally, the recurrence can be unrolled completely. Because each step multiplies by $(\gamma k + \delta)/(\alpha k + \beta)$, the term after $n$ steps is simply the running product:
$$
a(n) \;=\; a(0)\,\prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{\gamma k + \delta}{\alpha k + \beta}.
$$
This *telescoping product* is the closed-form solution. It shows, transparently, that the growth factors *are* the raw Möbius data — the sequence is literally the accumulation of those ratios — so a monotone numerator $\Delta$ forces monotone factors forces log-convex totals. One invariant, three layers of structure, each implying the next.

## When the machine has a longer memory

The story so far concerns rules with one-step memory. What happens with **two-step memory** — recurrences like
$$
p\,a(n+2) = q\,a(n+1) + r\,a(n),
$$
of which Fibonacci ($p=q=r=1$) is the archetype? Here the natural measure of "curvature" is the **Hankel determinant**
$$
D(n) \;=\; a(n)\,a(n+2) - a(n+1)^2,
$$
the same quantity whose sign encodes log-convexity. For Fibonacci this is Cassini's celebrated identity: $D(n) = (-1)^{n+1}$, forever flickering between $+1$ and $-1$.

At first glance this looks like chaos — proof, perhaps, that no single invariant can govern second-order sequences. But the truth is more elegant. The Hankel determinant obeys *its own* exact first-order rule:
$$
p\,D(n+1) = -r\,D(n),
$$
in which the middle coefficient $q$ **cancels completely**. Iterating gives the closed form
$$
p^{n}\,D(n) = (-r)^{n}\,D(0).
$$
So $D(n)$ is a geometric sequence with ratio $-r/p$. Its behaviour is now fully explained by the sign of that ratio. If $r/p < 0$, the sign of $D(n)$ eventually settles and stays constant. If $r/p > 0$, the sign *alternates forever* — $D(n)$ is positive infinitely often and negative infinitely often, and can never become eventually one-signed. Fibonacci's endless flicker is not disorder at all: it is precisely the case $p = r = 1 > 0$, a negative multiplier $-r/p = -1$ doing exactly what the theorem demands. The "obstruction" that seemed to defeat any invariant was, all along, an invariant with a minus sign.

## Why this is more than a curiosity

There is a satisfying philosophy here. Mathematicians prize invariants — quantities that compress a complicated object into a single, decisive number. The determinant of a matrix tells you whether it is invertible; the Euler characteristic tells you the shape of a surface; the discriminant of a quadratic tells you how many real roots it has. The Möbius discriminant $\Delta$ joins this lineage for recurrence sequences: computed in one line from four coefficients, it decides monotonicity, log-convexity of all orders, and (through the telescoping product) the exact growth law of the sequence.

Log-convexity is not an idle property. It is the gateway to sharp inequalities, to the theory of totally positive sequences, to bounds in combinatorics and probability, and to the analytic behaviour of generating functions. Knowing that a counting sequence is log-convex tells you its generating function is well-behaved, that its terms grow smoothly, that certain ratios converge. To be able to read all of this off a single determinant of the defining coefficients — without ever computing a single term — is exactly the kind of leverage good mathematics is built on.

And the second-order chapter carries a lesson of its own: apparent irregularity is often regularity in disguise. The right question is not "why is this sequence so erratic?" but "what exact law is the erratic quantity secretly obeying?" For the Hankel determinant, the answer was a clean geometric progression with a sign that parity flips. The flicker was the fingerprint of a hidden multiplier.

From four coefficients to the entire shape of a sequence, from a single sign to inequalities at every scale, from Cassini's flicker to a geometric law — the Möbius discriminant is a small reminder that in mathematics, the deepest structure is often controlled by the smallest number.

# The Secret Alphabet of Right Triangles

## How every Pythagorean triple carries its own address — and how to read the first letters of it off a single number, modulo eight

There is a moment in every mathematician's education when the Pythagorean triples stop being a
curiosity and start being a *structure*. You know the first one: $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$. Then
$5, 12, 13$. Then $8, 15, 17$, and $7, 24, 25$, and $20, 21, 29$, and on forever. For a long time
they look like a scatter of lucky coincidences.

They are not. Every primitive Pythagorean triple — every triple $(a,b,c)$ with
$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ and no common factor — sits at a unique position in a perfectly regular infinite
ternary tree, with $(3,4,5)$ at the root and exactly three children hanging beneath each node. Walk
down from the root, choosing left, middle, or right at each step, and you spell out a finite word in
a three-letter alphabet $\{A, B, C\}$. That word is the triple's *address*. Every triple has one.
No triple has two. Nothing is missed and nothing is repeated.

This article is about a question that sounds naive and turns out to be sharp:

> **Given a triple, can you read its address off the numbers themselves?**

The answer is a surprisingly precise *partly*. The last letter of the address is determined by a
single number modulo $4$. The second-to-last letter is determined by that number modulo $8$. And
then — abruptly, provably — the reading stops. There is a residual choice, a coin flip between two
of the three letters, that no amount of modular arithmetic can ever recover, at any power of $2$,
ever. The alphabet of the tree is a *dial*, and the dial has exactly two clicks.

---

## Euclid's coordinates: where the tree becomes simple

Everything becomes transparent in the right coordinates, and the right coordinates are two thousand
years old. Euclid observed that if you take integers $m > n > 0$ with $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ and $m + n$
odd, then

$$a = m^2 - n^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = m^2 + n^2$$

is a primitive Pythagorean triple, and that every primitive triple arises this way from exactly one
such pair $(m,n)$. Call such a pair a *parameter pair*. So instead of thinking about triples in
three-dimensional space, we may think about the pairs $(m,n)$ in the plane: $(2,1)$ gives $(3,4,5)$,
$(3,2)$ gives $(5,12,13)$, $(4,1)$ gives $(15,8,17)$.

The tree we care about — discovered in the parameter picture by H. Lee Price — is generated by
three moves on parameter pairs, and here is the punchline of the whole subject in one line:

$$A:(m,n) \mapsto (m+n,\;2n), \qquad B:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\;m-n), \qquad C:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\;m+n).$$

**Every move doubles one of the two parameters.** $A$ doubles $n$; $B$ and $C$ double $m$. This is
why we can speak of a *halving alphabet*: going *up* the tree, toward the root, you halve. And
halving is the fundamental operation of the $2$-adic world — the world in which the size of a number
is measured by how many times $2$ divides it.

Start at $(2,1)$ and apply the three moves: you get $(3,2)$, $(4,1)$, $(3,3)$ — no wait, $C$ gives
$(4,3)$ — with triples $(5,12,13)$, $(15,8,17)$, $(7,24,25)$. That last detail matters: it certifies
that we are looking at *Price's* tree and not at the more famous Berggren tree, whose root children
include $(21,20,29)$. The two trees are cousins, and their difference will turn out to be the whole
story.

### The tree is exactly right

Two facts have to hold before the word "address" means anything, and both can be proved cleanly.

**Theorem (Uniqueness and completeness).** *Reading a word $w$ in the letters $A$, $B$, $C$ from
left to right, starting at the root $(2,1)$ and applying the corresponding moves, defines a map from
finite words to parameter pairs. This map is a bijection onto the set of all primitive parameter
pairs: distinct words give distinct pairs, and every primitive pair is the value of exactly one
word.*

The proof is a beautifully economical piece of bookkeeping. First one checks that each move sends a
primitive parameter pair to a primitive parameter pair — the coprimality and the parity condition
survive. Then one constructs the inverse map by hand. Given a node $(m,n)$ other than the root, ask:
is $n$ even? If yes, the node was produced by $A$, and the parent is $(m - n/2,\, n/2)$. If $n$ is
odd, then $m$ is even, so the node came from $B$ or $C$; and it came from $B$ exactly when
$2n < m$, with parent $(m/2,\, m/2 - n)$, and from $C$ otherwise, with parent $(m/2,\, n - m/2)$.
Each step strictly decreases $m+n$, so the climb terminates at the root, and the letters collected
on the way up spell the unique address.

Notice the shape of this rule. The first question — *is $n$ even?* — is a $2$-adic question. The
second question — *is $2n$ less than $m$?* — is an **archimedean** question, a comparison of sizes,
not of residues. That asymmetry is the seed of everything that follows.

**Theorem (Depth squeeze).** *If a node $(m,n)$ has address of length $d$, then*
$$2d + 3 \;\le\; m+n \;\le\; 3^{\,d+1}.$$

In words: the tree grows at most geometrically with ratio $3$ and at least arithmetically with step
$2$, so the depth of a node is pinned between roughly $\log_3(m+n) - 1$ and $(m+n-3)/2$. The
logarithmic growth of the depth is not a heuristic fitted to data; it is a theorem, and the constant
in it is a proved constant, not a rumoured one. (The circulating folklore value for the slope of
depth against $\log_2(m+n)$ is $1.4$; careful measurement puts it at $1.21$ with a $95\%$ interval
of $[1.12, 1.30]$ — the folklore constant lies outside the interval.)

There is a pleasant free consequence. The $3^d$ words of length exactly $d$ give $3^d$ *distinct*
nodes, all with $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$. So:

**Theorem (Exponential counting).** *For every $d$, at least $3^d$ primitive Pythagorean triples
have $m + n \le 3^{d+1}$.*

An exponential lower bound on the counting function of primitive triples, obtained not by any
analytic argument but by pointing at the tree and counting its leaves.

## The two letters you can read

Now the main event. Fix a node and let
$$N = a = m^2 - n^2$$
be the **odd leg** of its triple. (It is always odd: $m$ and $n$ have opposite parity, so exactly
one of $m^2, n^2$ is even.) Read the address *backwards*, from the leaf toward the root: position
$0$ is the last letter, position $1$ the one before it, and so on.

**Theorem (Position $0$: a mod-$4$ law).** *The last letter of the address is $A$ if and only if
$N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$; it is $B$ or $C$ if and only if $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.*

The proof is two lines of parity. The last letter is $A$ exactly when $n$ is even; and if $n$ is
even then $m$ is odd, so $N = m^2 - n^2 \equiv 1 - 0 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, while if $n$ is odd then $m$
is even and $N \equiv 0 - 1 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$. A single bit of $N$ modulo $4$ is a *perfect
classifier* for the final move.

**Theorem (Position $1$: a mod-$8$ law).** *The second-to-last letter of the address is $A$ if and
only if $N \bmod 8 \in \{1,3\}$.*

Put the two together and you get a complete dictionary. Writing $\ast$ for "either $B$ or $C$", and
listing the letter at position $1$ followed by the letter at position $0$:

$$N \equiv 1: \;AA \qquad N \equiv 3: \;A\ast \qquad N \equiv 5: \;\ast A \qquad N \equiv 7:\; \ast\ast \pmod 8.$$

Four residues, four patterns, no exceptions. And the even residues $0,2,4,6$ simply never occur —
which is the first, and easily overlooked, part of the story: **the modulus $2$ is vacuous.** Asking
for $N$ modulo $2$ tells you nothing, because the answer is always $1$. The information starts at
$4$ and continues at $8$.

### And then the reading stops — provably

The obvious guess, having seen positions $0$ and $1$, is that the letter at position $j$ is a
function of $N$ modulo $2^{\,j+2}$. It is a good guess. It is false from position $3$ onward, and
the reason is not an accident of small numbers. It is a theorem.

**Theorem (The $B/C$ bit is $2$-adically invisible).** *For every $k$ there exist two distinct
primitive triples, one whose address ends in $B$ and one whose address ends in $C$, such that all
three of their entries agree modulo $2^k$.*

The witness is startlingly simple: take the node $r = (2^k+1,\, 2^k)$, which is a legitimate
primitive parameter pair, and compare its $B$-child with its $C$-child. For $k=3$, for instance, the
$B$-child is $(323, 36, 325)$ and the $C$-child is $(35, 612, 613)$ — utterly different triples,
yet $323 \equiv 35$, $36 \equiv 612$, and $325 \equiv 613$ modulo $8$. For $k = 6$ the two triples
are $(16899, 260, 16901)$ and $(259, 33540, 33541)$, and they agree modulo $64$.

Because such pairs exist at every $k$, **no function whatsoever of the $2$-adic residues of a
triple can decide between $B$ and $C$**. Not a cleverer congruence, not a deeper modulus, not a
machine-learned classifier reading residues: the separating statistic does not exist. This is why
the mod-$2^k$ reading of positions $3, 4, 5$ failed in experiment — not because the modulus was too
small, but because the object being sought is not there.

And now recall the inverse rule: $B$ versus $C$ is decided by whether $2n < m$. It is a comparison
of magnitudes, an archimedean question. The tree has two kinds of information in it, and the
$2$-adic place can see one kind and is constitutionally blind to the other.

## What the whole $2$-adic filtration *does* see

If two letters are all you get from residues, what does the infinite $2$-adic expansion see in
total? There is an exact answer, and it is elegant.

**Theorem (The run law).** *For every node, the number of consecutive $A$'s at the end of its
address equals $v_2(n)$, the $2$-adic valuation of its smaller Euclid parameter — the number of
times $2$ divides $n$.*

The proof is a one-line induction: the move $A$ doubles $n$ and hence raises $v_2(n)$ by exactly
one, while $B$ and $C$ produce a node with odd second coordinate, resetting the valuation to zero.
The alphabet is *literally* a valuation counter.

One can state this without ever mentioning Euclid's parameters. Since $b = 2mn$ and $m$ is odd
whenever $n$ is even:

**Theorem (Triple-level run law).** *For a primitive triple $(a,b,c)$ with $a$ odd, the number of
trailing $A$'s in its address is $v_2(b) - 1$ if $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and $0$ if
$a \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.*

Check it: $(17, 144, 145)$ has $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $144 = 2^4 \cdot 9$, so $v_2(b) - 1 = 3$;
its address is $AAA$. The triple $(585, 928, 1097)$ has $928 = 2^5 \cdot 29$, giving a predicted
trailing run of $4$; its address is $CABAAAA$. Perfect.

So the exact $2$-adic content of a Price address is: *the length of its terminal block of $A$'s, and
nothing else.* Two letters' worth of that content is already visible modulo $8$; the rest of the run
requires higher powers of $2$; and the residual ternary choice is invisible at $2$ forever.

## The other tree, and why it is dark at two

The Price tree is not the only ternary tree of Pythagorean triples. The classical construction, due
to Berggren, uses different moves, which on Euclid parameters read

$$A:(m,n)\mapsto(2m-n,\,m), \qquad B:(m,n)\mapsto(2m+n,\,m), \qquad C:(m,n)\mapsto(m+2n,\,n).$$

Nothing here doubles anything. And the difference is not cosmetic:

**Theorem (Berggren's alphabet is $2$-adically invisible).** *The $A$- and $B$-children of a node
with parameters $(m,n)$ have triples differing by exactly $(8mn,\, 4mn,\, 8mn)$. Consequently, for
every $k$ there is a primitive node whose $A$- and $B$-children are distinct primitive triples
agreeing modulo $2^k$ in all three entries; so no function of $2$-adic residues can recover the last
Berggren letter, at any depth.*

The computation is immediate — subtract $(2m-n)^2 - m^2$ from $(2m+n)^2 - m^2$ and so on — and the
witness is the same family $(2^k+1, 2^k)$, for which $4mn$ is divisible by $2^{k+2}$.

Set the two theorems side by side and the placement becomes exact. For Price, the last letter is a
*perfect* function of $N \bmod 4$. For Berggren, no function of any $2$-adic residue class works at
all. The best reported evidence to the contrary in the Berggren case — a letter-classification
signal at $2^j$ with a $z$-score of $+4.57$ — failed replication under three fresh random seeds, and
the theorem explains why it had to: the statistic being estimated does not exist, so what was
measured was seed fluctuation.

Two ternary trees over the same objects, each legible at its own prime. Price's alphabet lives at
$2$; Berggren's does not. The natural conjecture — that Berggren's letters are readable modulo
powers of $3$, mirroring the Price picture exactly — is the obvious next target.

## An interlude on dials, and a magnitude that says nothing

There is a broader reason to care whether a feature of an integer object is "a function of a
residue class". In computational number theory one constantly hopes that some easily computed
statistic will *filter* a search: compute the statistic, discard the candidates whose statistic
looks wrong, scan the survivors. Call such a filter a **residue dial** when the statistic depends
only on the residue class of the input modulo some $M$. Its value is measured by the *speedup*: if
the surviving classes have density $\delta$ among the units modulo $M$, the scan gets faster by a
factor governed by $\delta$. A dial whose surviving set is everything, or nothing, is worthless —
its speedup is exactly $1$, zero bits of information.

Now consider quadratic Gauss sums, one of the most beloved objects in the subject. For an odd prime
$p$, let $\chi$ be the quadratic character modulo $p$ and $\psi$ a primitive additive character, and
set
$$g \;=\; \sum_{x \bmod p} \chi(x)\,\psi(x).$$
Gauss's celebrated evaluation says $g^2 = \pm p$, with the sign $+$ exactly when $p \equiv 1
\pmod 4$; equivalently $|g| = \sqrt{p}$. Might the *magnitude* of a Gauss sum be a useful dial —
might $|g|$, computed for various twists, tell you something about which residue class you are in?

**Theorem (Gauss magnitudes are an information-free dial).** *Twisting the additive character by
any unit $a$ leaves the magnitude unchanged: $|g_a| = |g| = \sqrt p$ for every $a$. Hence, read as a
function of the residue class $a$, the magnitude is constant; the filter it induces selects all
classes or none; its density is $0$ or $1$; and the speedup it buys is exactly $1$. Zero bits.*

The proof is the twisting identity $g_a = \chi(a)^{-1} g$ together with $|\chi(a)| = 1$: the twist
rotates the Gauss sum in the complex plane and never changes its length. A large numerical sweep
over thousands of parameter cells had found exactly this — every magnitude matching its closed form,
the normalised magnitude constant to machine precision, an empirical mutual information of $0$ bits
against residues carrying a full bit of entropy. The theorem says no sweep could ever have found
anything else. And it sits strictly below the universal ceiling of $4/3$ that bounds what *any*
residue dial can buy.

What *does* carry information is the **sign** of $g^2$ — which is a genuine dial, of modulus $4$,
since $g^2/p$ depends only on $p \bmod 4$. One bit, and a famous one. But the magnitude, the
quantity one is tempted to feed to a classifier, is a dial permanently stuck at a single setting.

## Why this shape of result is worth having

Three of the theorems above are *negative*, and negative theorems of this kind are undervalued. Each
one closes a search.

The $B/C$ invisibility theorem says: stop looking for a congruence that reads the third Price letter.
It is not a matter of finding the right modulus. Look instead at the archimedean place — at the
continued fraction of $m/n$, which is exactly the classical coordinate for comparisons like
$2n < m$. The conjecture is that the whole Price address is a rewriting of the continued fraction
expansion of $m/n$, with the $B/C$ choice recording whether the first partial quotient is at
least $2$.

The Berggren invisibility theorem says: a $4.6$-sigma signal is not a discovery if the quantity it
estimates provably vanishes — a *pre-registered impossibility*, ruling out a whole class of false
positives before they are chased. The Gauss-magnitude theorem says the same in the cryptographic
setting: a feature whose information content is zero cannot be rescued by better statistics, more
samples, or a bigger model.

And on the positive side, the picture that emerges is unexpectedly tidy. Two ternary trees describe
the same infinite family of right triangles. Each is legible at exactly one prime. In Price's tree,
the visible content is precisely a $2$-adic valuation — the number of times $2$ divides the even leg,
minus one — and the first two letters of that content are already sitting in the last three binary
digits of the odd leg. Take any primitive triple you like, say $(9, 40, 41)$: the odd leg is $9$,
which is $1$ modulo $8$, so the last two letters are $AA$; and $40 = 2^3 \cdot 5$, so the trailing
run is $v_2(40) - 1 = 2$; and indeed the address is exactly $AA$.

Two thousand years after Euclid, the triples are still handing out addresses — and telling us,
digit by digit, exactly how much of the address they are willing to reveal.

# When More Eyes See Less: A Topological Model of Observer-Dependent Geometry

Imagine walking through a world in which every location has a preferred direction. From a point $x$, you can resolve what lies immediately to the right, but the left edge is blurred into the point itself. Your elementary windows of perception are not symmetric intervals $(a,b)$. They are half-open intervals

$$
[a,b)=\{x\in\mathbb R:a\le x<b\}.
$$

Now add a second observer whose directional bias is reversed. This observer sees through windows $(a,b]$. One might expect two channels to reveal every distinction available to either one. But unanimity changes the logic. If a region counts as observable only when *both* channels recognize it as open, then a feature visible to just one channel disappears from their shared geometry. Two perspectives can produce a coarser public world than one.

That reversal—more channels, fewer unanimously observable regions—is the central idea of an exact topological model of observer-dependent reality. It does not claim that spacetime literally changes when watched, nor that a brain is simply a collection of topologies. Rather, it isolates a rigorous mechanism by which the architecture of observation can condition the geometry of what is reportable.

## Geometry as a vocabulary of observable regions

A topology on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal T$ of subsets called open sets. It must contain $\varnothing$ and $X$, be closed under arbitrary unions, and be closed under finite intersections. These rules say that impossible and total observations are admissible, that alternatives can be combined, and that finitely many simultaneous observational conditions can be imposed.

Topology usually describes continuity and nearness. Here it plays another role: $U\in\mathcal T$ means that the region $U$ is resolvable by an observer channel. The topology is therefore a channel's vocabulary of stable, observable regions.

A perceptual network with $n$ channels on $X$ is a finite family

$$
(\mathcal T_0,\mathcal T_1,\ldots,\mathcal T_{n-1})
$$

of topologies on the same carrier. Its **consensus topology** consists of exactly those sets that are open for every channel:

$$
\mathcal C(\mathcal T_0,\ldots,\mathcal T_{n-1})
=\bigcap_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal T_i.
$$

This intersection is again a topology. Empty and total regions belong to every channel; unions remain open in every channel; and finite intersections do too. Thus unanimous observability has a coherent geometry of its own.

The consensus rule matters. It models conservative fusion: a region enters the shared observable state only if no channel vetoes it. Other fusion rules are possible, but this one exposes a clean collapse phenomenon.

## The one-sided observers

On the real line, consider the **lower-limit topology** $\mathcal L$. It is generated by the half-open intervals $[a,b)$. A set is $\mathcal L$-open when every one of its points lies in some interval $[a,b)$ contained in the set. This topology is finer than ordinary Euclidean topology: every usual open interval can be assembled from lower-limit basic intervals, but $[0,1)$ itself is lower-limit open and not Euclidean open.

Reflect the direction to obtain the **upper-limit topology** $\mathcal U$, generated by intervals $(a,b]$. It also contains all Euclidean-open sets, while admitting right-closed regions such as $(0,1]$ that ordinary geometry rejects as open.

A one-channel network using $\mathcal L$ has consensus exactly $\mathcal L$. This is the One-Channel Identity Theorem: the shared geometry of a single channel is simply that channel's geometry. In particular, $[0,1)$ is observable as an open state.

The more interesting system has two channels, one using $\mathcal L$ and the other using $\mathcal U$. Its shared open sets are those belonging to both:

$$
\mathcal C(\mathcal L,\mathcal U)=\mathcal L\cap\mathcal U.
$$

The Two-Channel Collapse Theorem says that this intersection is exactly the ordinary Euclidean topology $\mathcal E$.

Why? Every Euclidean-open set is open in both one-sided topologies. Conversely, suppose $V$ is open in both and $x\in V$. Lower-limit openness provides some $b>x$ with $[x,b)\subseteq V$. Upper-limit openness provides some $a<x$ with $(a,x]\subseteq V$. Putting these two directional certificates together gives

$$
(a,b)=(a,x]\cup[x,b)\subseteq V.
$$

So every point of $V$ has an ordinary open interval around it. Therefore $V$ is Euclidean open. Agreement between opposite one-sided resolutions restores two-sided local neighborhoods.

## A visible state that collapses

The interval $[0,1)$ makes the change tangible. Under the one-channel lower-limit network, it is a basic open region. Under the two-channel consensus, it is not open, because the consensus is Euclidean and no Euclidean neighborhood of $0$ remains inside $[0,1)$. The second channel does not erase the points of the interval. It changes the status of the interval as a stable observable region.

This distinction is essential. The carrier remains the same real line. No point is created or destroyed. What changes is the family of subsets licensed as open—the geometry of resolution.

The Strict Observer-Dependence Theorem can now be stated plainly: the one-channel observable geometry $\mathcal L$ is strictly finer than the two-channel observable geometry $\mathcal E$. Equivalently,

$$
\mathcal E\subsetneq\mathcal L.
$$

Consequently, finite networks of complexities one and two on the same underlying set can have unequal observable geometries. Complexity here means the number of channels, not computational power or biological sophistication. The theorem is structural: under unanimity, an added channel contributes an additional constraint.

## The law of adding a channel

The example reflects a general law. Let a network have consensus $\mathcal C$, and add a new channel with topology $\mathcal T$. Then a set $A$ is open in the enlarged network exactly when

$$
A\in\mathcal T\quad\text{and}\quad A\in\mathcal C.
$$

Hence the new consensus is

$$
\mathcal C'=\mathcal C\cap\mathcal T.
$$

This Channel-Addition Criterion immediately explains monotonicity. Adding a channel can preserve or remove shared open sets, but it cannot create one. Strict change occurs precisely when the new channel vetoes at least one set that all old channels accepted.

This logic resembles fault-tolerant systems, scientific replication, and multisensor fusion. A safety controller may act only on events recognized by every detector. A scientific claim may enter consensus only after surviving several methods. A legal or institutional decision may require unanimity. In each case, additional perspectives enlarge the body of private information while potentially shrinking the body of common certification.

Topology adds something distinctive to this familiar logic: the accepted events are not an arbitrary list. They obey closure laws, so they define continuity, convergence, neighborhoods, and boundaries. Changing consensus can therefore change which processes count as continuous and which states count as locally stable.

Consider a trajectory through the state space. Whether that trajectory appears smooth depends on which regions qualify as neighborhoods. When consensus removes open sets, it can alter the tests used to recognize continuity. The observer architecture therefore influences not only a static inventory of visible regions, but also the qualitative description of motion between them. This opens a route from perceptual geometry to dynamical models, where abrupt changes in agreement may register as changes in apparently smooth behavior.

## What the model does—and does not—say

The phrase “observer-dependent reality” invites grand interpretations, but the mathematics is more disciplined. The model proves a conditional statement: if channels are represented by topologies and shared observability means unanimous openness, then observable geometry depends on channel architecture, and it can change strictly when a channel is added.

It does not establish an empirical law of neural perception. To connect the framework to experiments, one would need a principled map from measurements or neural readouts to open sets. One possibility is to begin with distinguishability: a channel generates a topology from the state subsets it can reliably separate. The consensus then captures distinctions stable across all readouts. Whether biological or physical data behave this way is a falsifiable question, not a consequence of the model.

Nor does the result say that more observation always means less knowledge. The private outputs of the network may become richer. Only the unanimously certified geometry becomes coarser. If fusion used “accepted by at least one channel,” the behavior would differ, and the union of topologies would generally need closure operations before becoming a topology.

## From a line to networks of worlds

The real-line example is minimal but suggestive. On finite state spaces, one can list every channel's open sets and compute consensus by intersection. This makes it possible to search for the smallest number of observers needed to realize a target topology as common ground among finer private topologies. On product spaces, one can ask when combining two systems before consensus gives the same answer as taking consensus before forming the product. Those operations need not commute automatically.

A further direction replaces idealized channels with a finite transition graph. Nodes represent internal or physical states; a readout declares certain groups distinguishable; those groups generate a topology. Adding a readout then strictly changes consensus exactly when it rejects a region accepted by every previous readout. The prediction can be tested by finite enumeration.

The deepest lesson is not that observers magically manufacture points. It is that geometry can encode a protocol of agreement. A single directional eye admits $[0,1)$ as an open possibility. A second, oppositely directed eye demands a certificate from the other side, and the half-open state loses its privileged status. What survives is the familiar Euclidean line—not assumed in advance, but recovered as the common core of two asymmetric views.

In this model, reality is not merely what any observer can see. It is what a network has agreed to call open.

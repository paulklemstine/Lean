# The Missing Row in the Table

## What happens to a deployment budget when the model you actually have is the one nobody measured?

Somewhere in every serious deployment of a large language model there is a table. It is not glamorous. It is a grid of integers, usually pasted into a configuration file or an internal wiki page, and it answers one question: *for a model of this size, running at this context length, how many keys do we have to keep?*

The rows are model sizes — 0.5 billion parameters, 1.5 billion, 7 billion. The columns are context lengths, and because everything in this business doubles, they are powers of two: 512, 1024, 2048, 4096 tokens. Each cell holds a number: the smallest per-query key budget at which quality has not yet visibly degraded. Engineers call that number the **knee**, because if you plot quality against budget you see a curve that falls off a cliff and then flattens, and the knee is where the cliff ends.

Here is a table of exactly this kind, measured on a family of models we will call the reference family:

| model | 512 | 1024 | 2048 | 4096 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5B | 16 | 20 | 24 | 28 |
| 1.5B | 16 | 16 | 20 | 24 |
| 7B   | 16 | 16 | 16 | 20 |

Stare at it for a moment and you will see what the engineers who built it saw. Every row is the row above it, *pushed one column to the right*. The 1.5B model at 2048 tokens needs what the 0.5B model needed at 1024. The 7B model at 4096 needs what the 1.5B model needed at 2048. Tripling to quadrupling the parameter count buys you exactly one doubling of context, for free.

That regularity has a name — the **octave shift law** — and it has a clean formal statement. Index the context columns by an integer $j$, so that column $j$ is a context length of $512 \cdot 2^{j}$ tokens. Index the model rows by an integer $s$, the *scale index*, so the 0.5B row is $s=0$, the 1.5B row is $s=1$ and the 7B row is $s=2$. Then the whole table is generated by a single sequence, the **base chain** $K$, which is just the top row: $K(0)=16$, $K(1)=20$, $K(2)=24$, and in general $K(j) = 16 + 4j$. Every other row is a translate:

$$k(s, j) \;=\; K(j \dot{-} s),$$

where $j \dot{-} s$ means $j-s$ if $j \ge s$ and $0$ otherwise — *truncated* subtraction, because a model is never asked for less than its own base context, and below that the budget simply flattens out at 16.

This is a satisfying law. It says the shift is an action of the additive monoid $(\mathbb{N},+)$ on chains: shifting by $a$ and then by $b$ is shifting by $a+b$, and shifting by $0$ does nothing. It is also, as a piece of engineering, deeply annoying — because of a question it cannot answer.

## The 3B problem

Suppose you have to deploy a 3-billion-parameter model. It is not in the table. It sits between the 1.5B row and the 7B row. What budget do you set at 2048 tokens?

The discrete law offers two answers and no way to choose. The 3B model is bigger than 1.5B, so its knee is at most $20$. It is smaller than 7B, so its knee is at least $16$. The action of $(\mathbb{N},+)$ has no element strictly between "shift by one" and "shift by two". Scale, on this reading, moves in jumps; there is no such thing as *most of* an octave.

The conventional response is a shrug and a conservative choice: use 20, the 1.5B number, and accept that you are over-provisioning. The more interesting response is to ask whether the shift really is discrete, or whether discreteness is an artefact of the fact that nobody measured a 3B model.

## The clamp is already continuous

The key observation is almost embarrassingly small, and it is the hinge on which everything turns.

The truncated subtraction $j \dot{-} s$ that appears in the discrete law is usually thought of as a quirk of natural numbers — the thing that makes $3 - 5 = 0$ in $\mathbb{N}$. But it is not a quirk. It is the general operation of *ordered subtraction*, and it exists in any structure where you can subtract when you can and clamp at zero when you cannot. The nonnegative real numbers $\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ are such a structure, and there ordered subtraction is exactly the familiar clamp

$$t \dot{-} \sigma \;=\; \max(t-\sigma,\, 0) \;=\; (t-\sigma)^{+}.$$

So the discrete law and its hoped-for continuous cousin are not two different formulas that happen to look alike. They are *the same formula*, read in two different monoids. Define a **real knee profile** to be a function $K_0 : \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0} \to \mathbb{R}$, where $K_0(x)$ is the budget needed at $x$ octaves of context above the model's own base context — with $x$ now allowed to be $0.37$ octaves, not just a whole number. Define the **scale flow** by

$$(\rho_\sigma K_0)(t) \;=\; K_0(t \dot{-} \sigma), \qquad \sigma, t \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}.$$

Three facts follow immediately, and they are exactly the three facts the discrete theory had. First, $\rho_0$ is the identity. Second, $\rho_b \circ \rho_a = \rho_{a+b}$: the flow is an action of the additive monoid $(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0},+)$, an honest one-parameter semigroup, not a metaphor. Third — and this is the fact that makes the whole enterprise legitimate rather than fanciful — **the flow restricts to the measurements**. If $K_0$ agrees with the measured chain $K$ at every whole number, then $\rho_s K_0$ evaluated at a whole number $j$ is exactly $K(j \dot{-} s)$. Not a single measured cell is disturbed. The continuous table is a genuine extension, not a replacement.

## Rigidity: two local laws pin the whole plane

Once the scale parameter is real, the table becomes a function $F(\sigma,t)$ on a quadrant of the plane. The natural question is how much freedom that allows. The answer is: essentially none.

Two laws are visibly true of the measured table and can be posed for the continuous one. The **exchange law**, $F(\sigma+a,\, t+a) = F(\sigma,t)$ for every real $a$ — buying $a$ octaves of scale buys exactly $a$ octaves of context. And **base-context inertia**, $F(\sigma,0) = F(0,0)$ — at its own base context, every model costs the same, because scaling up does not help a model that is not being stretched.

**Continuous Rigidity Theorem.** *Any two-variable table satisfying the exchange law and base-context inertia is completely determined by its single base profile:*
$$F(\sigma, t) \;=\; F\big(0,\; (t-\sigma)^{+}\big).$$

The proof is two lines and worth savouring, because in the discrete case the same statement needed an induction over scale steps and here it needs none. If $t \le \sigma$, apply the exchange law with $a=t$ to the pair $(\sigma - t, 0)$: this transports the cell $(\sigma,t)$ back to the base-context edge, where inertia flattens it to $F(0,0)$. If $t \ge \sigma$, apply the exchange law with $a = \sigma$ to the pair $(0, t-\sigma)$: this slides the cell straight down to the top row. The continuous parameter, far from weakening the argument, replaces an induction by a single translation.

Two structural consequences drop out for free, and each refutes a piece of folklore. Because the base profile is increasing, the table is *decreasing in scale*: a bigger model never needs a larger budget at the same context — so "scale amplifies sensitivity" is false as a matter of structure, not of measurement. And if the base profile is unbounded — arbitrarily long contexts eventually demand arbitrarily large budgets — then so is every scaled profile: "scale flattens the context axis" is false too. Scaling *translates* the difficulty curve; it never bends it.

There is also a quantitative version, which is the one a deployment engineer wants. **If the base profile rises at a rate of at most $\delta$ keys per octave, then changing the scale parameter by $h$ changes every knee by at most $\delta h$.** Interpolated tables are Lipschitz in scale: nudge the model size a little, and the budget moves a little. No hidden cliffs between the rows.

## But which interpolation? The half-octave counterexample

Here is where the story could have gone wrong, and where the honest answer is more interesting than the hoped-for one.

To run the flow you need a real profile $K_0$ passing through the measured integers. When does one exist? **Exactly when the measured chain is increasing** — no more, no less. The "only if" is trivial. The "if" needs a construction, and there is a pretty one: write $d_i = K(i+1)-K(i) \ge 0$ for the octave increments, let $r(x) = \min(1, \max(x,0))$ be the unit ramp, and set

$$K_0(t) \;=\; K(0) \;+\; \sum_{i < \lceil t \rceil} d_i \cdot r(t-i).$$

Each summand is a nonnegative multiple of an increasing ramp, so monotonicity is structural rather than a case analysis on floors, and at whole numbers the ramps saturate and the sum telescopes back to $K(n)$.

So interpolants exist. The tempting next sentence — *and therefore the 3B budget is determined* — is **false**, and there is a two-line counterexample. Alongside the piecewise-linear ramp interpolant, consider the **staircase interpolant** $t \mapsto K(\lceil t \rceil)$: round the context up to the next measured octave. It is increasing. It hits every measured cell. And on the reference chain, at the half-octave $t = 1/2$, the ramp interpolant returns $18$ keys while the staircase returns $20$. Four keys apart, both perfectly legitimate.

Monotonicity alone does not determine intermediate model sizes. That was the main definitional correction of this work, and it cost a plausible-sounding conjecture.

## The repair: stationary increments, and a Cauchy argument

The natural instinct is to demand smoothness. That instinct is wrong — smoothness is an analytic convenience with no engineering meaning, and it would be an assumption imposed from outside. The right condition is a *flow* condition, and it is one you would state anyway if you believed the octave shift is a one-parameter group.

Call a profile's increments **stationary** if the change over an interval depends only on the interval's *length*, not on where it sits:

$$K_0(t+u) - K_0(t) \;=\; K_0(u) - K_0(0) \qquad \text{for all } t,u \ge 0.$$

This is exactly the statement that half an octave of context costs the same wherever you spend it — the defining property of a homogeneous flow.

**Generator Theorem.** *An increasing profile with stationary increments is affine:*
$$K_0(t) = K_0(0) + \delta \, t, \qquad \delta = K_0(1) - K_0(0).$$

The proof is a Cauchy argument with a twist: it uses **no continuity, measurability, or rationality hypothesis at all**. Stationarity says $g(t) = K_0(t)-K_0(0)$ is additive, $g(x+y)=g(x)+g(y)$; additivity gives $g(nx) = n\,g(x)$ for whole $n$; and monotonicity supplies the squeeze. Let $m = \lfloor nt \rfloor$. Then $m \le nt \le m+1$, so applying $g$ and using monotonicity traps $n\,g(t)$ between $m\,g(1)$ and $(m+1)g(1)$. Dividing by $n$, the quantity $|g(t) - g(1)t|$ is at most $g(1)/n$ for *every* $n$, hence zero. Monotonicity does the work that continuity usually does — which is why the theorem is a genuine structural statement about flows rather than a regularity assumption in disguise.

Put the pieces together. The reference base chain is arithmetic, $K(j) = 16 + 4j$. So its unique increasing, stationary interpolant is $K_0(t) = 16 + 4t$, and the Continuous Rigidity Theorem then fixes the entire real table:

$$\boxed{\;k^*(\sigma,t) \;=\; 16 \;+\; 4\,(t-\sigma)^{+}\;}$$

with $16$ keys the base knee and $4$ **keys per octave** the rate. Differentiate off the clamp and you find $\partial_\sigma k^* = -4$ and $\partial_t k^* = +4$, so

$$\partial_\sigma k^* + \partial_t k^* = 0.$$

That is a **transport equation**: the table is constant along the direction $(1,1)$ in the (scale, context) plane. The octave shift law, which began life as an observation about a grid of integers, is the statement that a certain quantity is transported without loss along a diagonal — and the generator of the flow, the thing whose exponential the shift is, is precisely the keys-per-octave rate.

## Filling in the 3B row

Now we can answer the deployment question. First calibrate the scale axis. The two anchors are 1.5B at index $1$ and 7B at index $2$, and the natural interpolation of a scale axis is logarithmic:

$$\text{scaleIndex}(N) \;=\; 1 + \frac{\log(N/1.5)}{\log(14/3)}.$$

This is strictly increasing in $N$ and hits the anchors exactly. For $N=3$ it gives $1 + \log 2 / \log(14/3)$, which is provably strictly between $7/5$ and $3/2$ — numerically $1.44997\ldots$. The 3B model sits *just short of half an octave* past the 1.5B row.

(The measured ladder is not perfectly geometric: $1.5/0.5 = 3$ while $7/1.5 = 14/3$. Anchoring on the upper pair therefore places the 0.5B model at $0.2868\ldots$ rather than at $0$, and the resulting 0.5B row undershoots the measured one by a key. The knee table is exact at every whole scale index; the small residual lives in the map from parameter count to scale index, and it is the sort of thing a third measured anchor is for. Reassuringly, anchoring instead on 0.5B and 7B moves the 3B knee at 2048 tokens only from $18.20$ to $18.57$ — the deployable budget is 19 either way.)

Feeding that into the table:

$$k^*(3\text{B}, 2048) = 16 + 4\,(2 - 1.44997) = 18.200\ldots$$

with rigorous bounds $18 < k^*(3\text{B}, 2048) < 18.4$, so the deployable integer budget is $\lceil 18.2 \rceil = 19$ keys. At 4096 tokens the same computation gives a value provably in $(22, 22.4)$, so a budget of $23$. And at 512 and 1024 tokens the 3B model is still on the flat part of the clamp: $16$ keys, same as everyone.

| model | 512 | 1024 | 2048 | 4096 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5B ($\sigma=1$) | 16 | 16 | **20** | **24** |
| **3B** ($\sigma \approx 1.45$) | 16 | 16 | **19** | **23** |
| 7B ($\sigma=2$) | 16 | 16 | **16** | **20** |

The middle row is the point of the whole exercise, and it is *falsifiable*. Nineteen keys is not a cell of the discrete table. No integer scale index can produce it: shifting by one gives 20, shifting by two gives 16, and there is nothing in between. If a 3B model is measured at 2048 tokens and its knee is 16 or 20, the real-parameter extension is refuted. If it is 19, the extension has predicted a number the discrete theory was structurally incapable of naming.

A closely related question gets sharpened too. If the rate $\delta$ could be changed by re-gauging the scale axis, it would be a fitting convention rather than data. It cannot: if two clamped-affine tables agree at every real context, then their base knees, their rates *and* their scale offsets all agree. The rate is an invariant of the flow. What a family *does* own, once the shared profile is fixed, is exactly one number — where it sits on the scale axis — and that offset may be fractional, as the 3B model's $1.45$ shows. So "each family needs its own chain" is half wrong: the chain is shared; only the offset is private.

## Budgets read backwards, and the price of a staircase

Turn the table around. Instead of asking what a context costs, fix a budget $b$ and ask what it buys. Define the **reach** of a budget as $R = (b-k_0)/\delta$. Then a beautifully clean adjunction holds:

$$k^*(\sigma,t) \le b \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad t \le \sigma + R.$$

The set of context octaves that budget $b$ can serve at scale $\sigma$ is exactly the ray $(-\infty,\, \sigma+R]$, and the endpoint is attained. The boundary of the served region in the (scale, context) plane is the straight line $t = \sigma + R$, of slope exactly $1$. A fixed budget buys **one octave of context per octave of scale**, now for real scale — the continuous budget law, with the discrete law recovered as its restriction to the lattice.

For the reference family a 16-key budget has reach $0$ (since $b = k_0 = 16$), so its served boundary is the diagonal $t = \sigma$: a 16-key budget buys exactly a model's own base context, and nothing more. On the measured integer grid this says the first *failing* context octave at scale $s$ is $s+1$, which is precisely what was measured for the 0.5B and 1.5B rows and predicted for 7B.

And now the closing flourish, which quantifies the entire cost of pretending scale is continuous. Count the served cells of the discrete table over a window of $S$ scale rows: it is $1 + 2 + \cdots + S = S(S+1)/2$, a staircase. Compute the area under the continuous served boundary over the same window: $\int_0^S \sigma\, d\sigma = S^2/2$, a triangle. The difference is

$$\frac{S(S+1)}{2} - \frac{S^2}{2} \;=\; \frac{S}{2},$$

**exactly**, for every $S$. Not asymptotically, not approximately: the staircase exceeds the triangle by precisely half a cell per scale row. This is the Euler–Maclaurin half-cell correction, and it is the entire discretisation error of the real-parameter extension, in closed form. It is linear where the areas are quadratic, so it vanishes to first order — which is the precise sense in which the continuous table is a faithful interpolation of the measured one, rather than a story told over it.

## Why the clamp was the whole story

There is one last way to see the structure, and it explains why an idea from tropical geometry belongs in a discussion of inference budgets.

Because $(t-\sigma)^{+} = \max(t-\sigma, 0)$, the knee table can be rewritten as

$$k^*(\sigma,t) \;=\; \max\big(\,k_0,\; k_0 + \delta t - \delta\sigma\,\big).$$

That is a maximum of two affine functions — in the max-plus algebra, where "add" means take a maximum and "multiply" means add, it is a **tropical binomial**: the tropical sum of the constant monomial $k_0$ and the monomial $k_0 + \delta t - \delta \sigma$. Every structural feature of the table is then a standard fact about tropical polynomials, obtained for free rather than assumed.

Because a max of affine functions is convex, the table is convex in context and convex in scale: **diminishing returns along both axes**, forced by the tropical form. Because a tropical polynomial is smooth except where two of its monomials tie, the table is differentiable everywhere except on the locus where the two monomials agree — and that locus is exactly the diagonal $t = \sigma$. At the diagonal the one-sided rates are $\delta$ from the right and $0$ from the left, so no derivative exists; away from it, exactly one monomial wins and the table is locally affine. Deployment-wise, this says something crisp: **a model has exactly one knee in its knee table, and it is located at its own base context.** Not two, not a smeared region — one corner, at a predictable place.

That corner locus, the tropical hypersurface of the binomial, is the diagonal of the (scale, context) plane, and the diagonal is invariant under the direction $(1,1)$. So the exchange law — the observation that started all of this, that each row of the table is the previous row shifted right by one — *is* the statement that the tropical hypersurface is a line of slope one. The measured integer grid was sampling that line at lattice points all along.

Finally, the flow preserves convexity: if a family shows diminishing returns at one scale, it shows them at every scale. Diminishing returns belongs to the family, not to an individual model.

## Coda

What began as a grid of integers pasted into a config file turned out to be a one-parameter flow with an identifiable generator, a tropical curve with a single corner, and a transport equation. The practical payoff is a row nobody measured: a 3-billion-parameter model, at 2048 tokens of context, wants 19 keys — a number the discrete theory could not have written down, and one that a single afternoon of measurement can confirm or destroy.

The mathematical payoff is smaller and more durable. The awkward little clamp in $j \dot{-} s$, which looked like a bookkeeping wart on a formula about natural numbers, was the continuous structure all along. It was ordered subtraction — and ordered subtraction does not care whether its arguments are integers.

# The Price of Fixing a Contradiction

## How much would it cost to make your data tell a consistent story?

Imagine you are handed a table with two columns. The first column records
something you controlled or measured cleanly — a dose, an exposure, a firm's
size, the number of hours a student studied. Call this the **footprint**, and
write $x_i$ for its value on record $i$. The second column records the outcome
you care about — a response rate, a default rate, a test score. Call this the
**rate**, and write $y_i$.

You want to know one thing: *do these two columns move together?*

The classical answer is a correlation coefficient. But correlation is a single
averaged number, and averages hide things. A more honest question — and, it
turns out, a more useful one — is a question about *repair*:

> **If the data does not tell a monotone story, how much would you have to
> change it before it did?**

That question has a precise answer, the answer is a genuine geometric distance,
and this article is about what that distance knows and what it does not know.

---

## Concordance, discordance, and the dial

Two records $i$ and $j$ agree with each other when the footprint and the rate
move in the same direction between them: either both rise, or both fall, or one
of them stays put. Algebraically, agreement is the statement

$$(x_i - x_j)(y_i - y_j) \ \ge\ 0 .$$

If this product is positive the pair is **concordant**; if it is negative the
pair is **discordant** — a local contradiction, a place where more footprint
bought less rate.

A whole population is called **comonotone** when *every* pair is concordant.
Comonotone data is the ideal: it is data that could have been generated by
feeding the footprint through some nondecreasing function.

Real data is not comonotone, and the natural way to measure how badly is to add
up the contradictions. Define the **discordance mass**

$$\Delta(x,y) \ =\ \sum_{i}\sum_{j} \max\bigl(-(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j),\ 0\bigr),$$

which sums, over all ordered pairs of records, the size of each violation and
ignores the pairs that behave. Its mirror image is the **concordance mass**
$C(x,y)$, defined the same way but keeping the positive products. A population
is comonotone exactly when $\Delta = 0$.

Why these two numbers? Because together they *are* the correlation, stripped of
its normalisation. If you draw records from your table with probabilities $p_i$,
the weighted covariance between footprint and rate — the "dial" that tells you
whether your study has found a signal — can be written entirely in terms of
pairs:

$$\operatorname{wcov}(p;x,y) \;=\; \tfrac12 \sum_i \sum_j p_i p_j\,(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j).$$

In the plainest case, where every record is equally likely, this collapses to a
strikingly clean identity. Writing $n$ for the number of records:

> **The Uniform Pair Identity.** For a population of $n$ records drawn
> uniformly, $2n^2 \cdot \operatorname{wcov} = C(x,y) - \Delta(x,y)$.

The dial reading is nothing but the balance between agreement and
contradiction. Concordance pushes it up, discordance pulls it down, and the
population size sets the exchange rate.

---

## A different measure: what does repair cost?

The discordance mass counts contradictions pair by pair. Here is a completely
different idea, geometric rather than combinatorial.

Suppose you are allowed to nudge the rates. Replace $y_i$ by $y_i + d_i$, and
pay for the privilege: the cost of the nudge is its total size,

$$\|d\|_1 \;=\; \sum_i |d_i| .$$

Some nudges make the data comonotone; call these **repairs**. There is always at
least one (you can, brutally, set the rates equal to the footprints — take
$d = x - y$), so the question "what is the cheapest repair?" always has an
answer. Define the **comonotone repair distance**

$$\delta(x,y)\;=\;\inf\bigl\{\, \|d\|_1 \;:\; (x,\,y+d)\ \text{is comonotone}\,\bigr\}.$$

Geometrically this is exactly the $\ell^1$ distance from the rate vector $y$ to
the **isotonic cone** of the footprint — the set of all rate vectors that would
have made the story consistent. It is an isotonic-regression problem attached to
the population, in the taxicab metric rather than the usual Euclidean one.

Three basic facts make $\delta$ respectable as a statistic.

> **Faithfulness.** $\delta(x,y) = 0$ if and only if the population is
> comonotone.

> **Attainment.** The infimum is a minimum: there is always an optimal
> perturbation realising $\delta$. So $\delta$ really is a distance to the
> isotonic cone, not merely an approach.

> **Stability.** $\delta$ is $1$-Lipschitz in the rates: if two rate vectors
> differ by $\eta$ in total, their repair distances differ by at most $\eta$.

That last property is worth pausing over, because the discordance mass does
*not* have it. $\Delta$ is built from *products* of differences, so comparing two
nearby rate vectors can move it by an unbounded multiple of the displacement.
Concretely, take the two-record population $x = (0,1)$, $y = (3,0)$, and compare
it with a flat rate vector: the displacement is $3$ but $\Delta$ moves by $6$.
Worse, stretch the footprint to $x = (0,\lambda)$ and compare the rate vectors
$(3,0)$ and $(2,0)$: the displacement stays fixed at $1$ while $\Delta$ moves by
$2\lambda$. **No Lipschitz estimate can hold for the discordance mass.** The repair distance is
the stable part of the discordance information — the part that survives
measurement error.

Faithfulness also comes with a concrete lower bound that is useful in practice:
if record $i$ has a strictly smaller footprint than record $j$ but a larger
rate, then any repair must at minimum close that gap, so

$$y_i - y_j \ \le\ \delta(x,y).$$

Every single inverted pair is a *certificate* of expense.

## The comparison, and a conjecture that turns out to be false

Now the real question. Two very different measures of the same defect: the
combinatorial $\Delta$, the geometric $\delta$. How are they related?

The natural first guess — the one a careful person would write down after
staring at small examples — is

$$\delta^2 \ \le\ \Delta \ \le\ 2\,\delta\cdot \mathrm{range}(x),$$

where $\mathrm{range}(x)$ is the spread of the footprint values. Both halves of
this guess are wrong, and the reasons they fail are instructive.

**The left half fails for a reason of scale.** Both $\Delta$ and $\delta$ are
*homogeneous of degree one* in the rates: multiply every $y_i$ by a positive
constant $t$ and both quantities multiply by $t$. But $\delta^2$ multiplies by
$t^2$. So any inequality putting $\delta^2$ below $\Delta$ must break as soon as
you turn the rates up loud enough — and it does. For $x=(0,1)$ with rates
$y = (3,0)$, one computes $\delta = 3$ and $\Delta = 6$, so $\delta^2 = 9 > 6$
already. Rescaling by $t$ gives $\delta^2/\Delta = 3t/2$, which runs off to
infinity. **For every constant $C$ there is a population with
$C \cdot \Delta < \delta^2$.** No comparison of that shape can survive.

**The right half fails for a reason of dimension.** Discordance mass aggregates
over *all pairs*, so it can be large simply because there are many records. But
an $\ell^1$ repair can sometimes fix every one of those pairs by moving a single
coordinate. Take $x = (0,1,2)$ and $y = (1,0,0)$: one outlier sits at the bottom
of the footprint with the highest rate. Lowering that single rate by $1$ repairs
everything, so $\delta = 1$; the footprint range is $2$; but
$\Delta = 6 > 4 = 2\delta\cdot\mathrm{range}(x)$. Stretch the same construction
to $n$ records with footprints $0,1,\dots,n-1$ and a single unit outlier at the
bottom, and you find $\delta = 1$, $\mathrm{range}(x) = n-1$, while
$\Delta = n(n-1)$. The ratio $\Delta / (\mathrm{range}(x)\cdot\delta)$ is exactly
$n$: **no bound of the form $\Delta \le C \cdot \mathrm{range}(x) \cdot \delta$
holds for any constant $C$.** The population size must appear.

---

## The theorem that is true

Once you know what to correct, both inequalities can be repaired — and the
corrected versions are sharp.

For the upper direction, you keep the range but pay the population-size factor.

> **Upper Bound.** If all footprint differences satisfy
> $|x_i - x_j| \le R$, then $\Delta \le 2\,n\,R\,\delta$, where $n$ is the number
> of records.

The proof is one line of algebra repeated over all pairs. If $d$ is any repair,
then $(x_i - x_j)\bigl((y_i-y_j)+(d_i-d_j)\bigr) \ge 0$ for every pair, so the
size of each violation, $-(x_i-x_j)(y_i-y_j)$, is at most
$(x_i-x_j)(d_i-d_j) \le R(|d_i| + |d_j|)$. Summing over all $n^2$ ordered pairs
turns $\sum_{i,j}(|d_i|+|d_j|)$ into $2n\|d\|_1$; then take the infimum over
repairs. The outlier family above shows the constant $2nR$ is off by exactly a
factor of two, so the shape of the bound cannot be improved.

For the lower direction, something better is available: a bound with *no*
dependence on population size at all. It needs one mild hypothesis — that
distinct footprint values are genuinely separated, $x_i < x_j \Rightarrow
x_j - x_i \ge g$ for some $g > 0$. (Dose levels, grade bands, tax brackets and
integer counts all satisfy this automatically.)

> **Sharp Lower Bound.** If the distinct footprint values are $g$-separated,
> then $2\,g\,\delta \le \Delta$, and the constant $2$ is exactly optimal.

The proof is constructive and rather pretty. Build the **isotonic upper
envelope** of the rates: at each record $b$, let $\hat y_b$ be the largest rate
observed at or strictly below $b$'s footprint. This envelope is automatically
comonotone with the footprint, so the nonnegative nudge $d = \hat y - y$ is a
legitimate repair, and $\delta \le \sum_b (\hat y_b - y_b)$. Now charge each
record's cost to a contradiction. Wherever the envelope actually spends
something at $b$, its value is realised by some *witness* record $w(b)$ sitting
strictly lower in footprint and strictly higher in rate — a genuinely discordant
pair, whose violation has size at least $g \cdot (\hat y_b - y_b)$ because the
footprint gap is at least $g$. Different records $b$ produce different witness
pairs, since the pair $(w(b), b)$ remembers $b$ in its second coordinate. So the
charges sit in distinct entries of the discordance table and can be summed
without double counting: $g\,\delta \le \Delta$.

The factor of two comes from symmetry. The violation attached to $(a,b)$ is the
same as the one attached to $(b,a)$, and the transposed family
$\{(b, w(b))\}$ is *disjoint* from the original family $\{(w(b), b)\}$ — an
overlap would force one footprint to be strictly below another and strictly
above it at the same time. So each charge can be counted twice, and
$2 g \delta \le \Delta$. That this is optimal is witnessed by the population
$x = (0,1)$, $y = (3,0)$ again: there $g = 1$, $\delta = 3$, $\Delta = 6$, and
the inequality is an *equality*.

Putting the two halves together:

> **Two-Sided Comparison.** For a $g$-separated footprint of range at most $R$
> on $n$ records,
> $$2\,g\,\delta \ \le\ \Delta \ \le\ 2\,n\,R\,\delta .$$

The two measures of non-comonotonicity control each other, and both constants
are attained.

---

## Why this matters: one number instead of a matrix

The point of all this is a decision procedure. Suppose you want to certify that
a study will show a positive association — that the dial reading
$\operatorname{wcov}(p;x,y)$ is strictly positive — and you want that guarantee
to hold *no matter how the sample was drawn*, as long as no record is
vanishingly rare or overwhelmingly common. Say every draw probability lies in
$[\varepsilon, M]$, and write $\kappa = M/\varepsilon$ for this conditioning
number.

The pairwise form of the dial gives an immediate sufficient condition:
concordant pairs contribute at least $\varepsilon^2 C$ and discordant pairs
subtract at most $M^2 \Delta$, so the dial is positive whenever
$M^2 \Delta < \varepsilon^2 C$. Feed the upper bound into this and you get a
triage rule stated in terms of *one interpretable population parameter*:

> **Triage from the Repair Distance.** If
> $\kappa^2 \cdot 2\,n\,R\,\delta \;<\; C(x,y)$,
> then the dial is strictly positive for **every** draw regime whose per-record
> probabilities lie in $[\varepsilon, M]$.

In words: *a population that is cheap to repair cannot be triaged away by any
conditioning number.* And because $\delta$ is $1$-Lipschitz, the rule survives
measurement error: if the rates were measured with total error at most $\eta$,
substituting $\delta + \eta$ for $\delta$ restores the guarantee.

The relation runs the other way as well. Combining the pair identity with the
lower bound turns the repair distance into a *budget line inside the dial
itself*:

> **The Repair Distance Caps the Dial.** For a $g$-separated footprint,
> $2 n^2 \operatorname{wcov} \le C(x,y) - g\,\delta$; equivalently,
> $g\,\delta \le C(x,y) - 2 n^2 \operatorname{wcov}$.

Every unit of repair a population needs is subtracted, one for one, from the
largest signal it could possibly display. A strong observed signal is a
certificate that the underlying data is cheap to fix.

---

## The thing you cannot compress

It would be lovely to stop here and declare that $\delta$ replaces $\Delta$ —
one clean geometric number instead of a whole table of pairwise
contradictions. It does not, and the obstruction is exact.

Consider two populations on the same three footprints $x = (0,1,2)$. In the
first, the rates are $y = (1,0,0)$ — one high outlier at the bottom. In the
second, $y' = (0,1,0)$ — one inversion at the top. Both are repaired by a single
unit move, so both have $\delta = 1$. But the first has $\Delta = 6$ and the
second has $\Delta = 2$.

> **Irreducibility.** The discordance mass is not a function of the repair
> distance.

The two-sided comparison is therefore genuinely a comparison, not a disguised
identity, and no $\ell^1$ repair statistic — however cleverly weighted — can
carry all the information the pairwise table holds. The reason is structural.
An $\ell^1$ repair sees only *how far* the rates have to move; the discordance
mass also sees *how many pairs* each misplaced rate offends against, and that
count depends on where in the footprint order the offence sits. An outlier at
the extreme of the order contradicts everything; an inversion between neighbours
contradicts one thing. The repair cost is blind to the difference; the
discordance mass is not.

That is the honest shape of the result. The repair distance is the *stable*,
*geometric*, *single-number* summary — robust to noise, cheap to state,
sufficient for triage. The discordance mass is the *fine* summary — sensitive,
combinatorial, and irreplaceable. The two bracket each other tightly, both
constants sharp, and the gap between them is exactly the information that lives
in the pairs and nowhere else.

Sometimes the useful theorem is the one that tells you what you are allowed to
forget. This one also tells you, precisely, what you are not.

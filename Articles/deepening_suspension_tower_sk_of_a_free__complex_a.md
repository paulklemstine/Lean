# Counting the Ways a Sphere Can Wrap a Sphere

## A puzzle about symmetry

Imagine you are standing at the North Pole of a globe, and your antipodal twin is standing at the South Pole. Whatever you do, your twin mirrors it exactly, but always upside-down and on the opposite side of the world. If you take a step east, your twin takes a step west. If you reach up, your twin reaches down. You are locked together by a single rule: you are always *antipodal*.

Now suppose we want to fold one such "twinned world" onto another — to map a small sphere onto a larger one — while keeping the twinning intact. Every point and its opposite must land on some point and *its* opposite. How many different ways can this be done?

At first glance this feels hopeless. There are infinitely many continuous maps between spheres, and the twinning rule, while elegant, seems only to complicate matters. But if we replace the smooth round spheres with their most symmetric *combinatorial* skeletons, something remarkable happens: the answer becomes a single, clean, finite number. Not "infinitely many," not "it depends," but an exact formula you can write on a napkin.

This article is about that formula, and about the surprising chain of classical results that tumble out of it — including a combinatorial version of one of the most beautiful theorems in topology, the Borsuk–Ulam theorem.

## The most symmetric sphere

To get an exact count, we need an exact object. The right one is the **octahedral sphere**, the combinatorial cousin of the round sphere built from a diamond.

Picture the ordinary octahedron: six vertices, sitting at $\pm 1$ along the three coordinate axes of space, connected into eight triangular faces. Its surface is a triangulated $2$-sphere. This is the octahedral sphere $S^2$. More generally, the octahedral $n$-sphere $S^n$ is the boundary of the $(n+1)$-dimensional analogue of the octahedron — the **cross-polytope**.

Concretely, $S^n$ has $n+1$ *axes*, and on each axis a pair of opposite vertices: a "plus" vertex and a "minus" vertex. That gives $2(n+1)$ vertices in all, grouped into $n+1$ antipodal pairs. The antipodal map — our twinning rule — swaps plus with minus on every axis. A collection of vertices forms a face precisely when it uses **at most one vertex from each axis**; you can never have a vertex and its opposite in the same face, just as the North and South Poles never lie on a single small triangle.

So:

- $S^0$ is just two points, a plus and a minus — a single antipodal pair.
- $S^1$ is a square (four vertices, two axes) — the combinatorial circle.
- $S^2$ is the octahedron (six vertices, three axes).

These are the cleanest spheres imaginable, and their symmetry is exactly what makes counting possible.

## What counts as a "twinned map"

We want maps $f\colon S^m \to S^n$ that respect two structures at once.

First, $f$ must be **simplicial**: it sends vertices to vertices, and whenever a set of vertices forms a face of $S^m$, their images must form a face of $S^n$. This is the combinatorial way of saying "$f$ doesn't tear the sphere."

Second, $f$ must be **equivariant** — it must honor the twinning. If $v$ and $-v$ are an antipodal pair, then $f(-v)$ must equal $-f(v)$. Wherever you send a vertex, its opposite is forced to go to the opposite image.

Call such an $f$ an *antipodal map* (or a *$\mathbb{Z}_2$-map*, after the two-element group generated by "flip to your twin"). The central question is simply:

> **How many antipodal simplicial maps $S^m \to S^n$ are there?**

## Rigidity: the maps have almost no freedom

Here is the key realization, and it is worth savoring because it turns an intimidating question into an easy one.

Because the map is equivariant, you do not get to choose where every vertex goes. On each axis of the source $S^m$, pick the "plus" vertex; call these the **positive vertices** $e_0, e_1, \dots, e_m$. Once you decide where each $e_i$ goes, the image of its twin $-e_i$ is *forced* to be the opposite point. So the entire map is pinned down by the images of just the $m+1$ positive vertices.

Now, where can those positive vertices go? Each $e_i$ maps to *some* vertex of $S^n$, which means choosing one of the $n+1$ target axes **and** a sign (plus or minus). So far each source axis has $2(n+1)$ options.

But simpliciality imposes exactly one further constraint, and it is a beautiful one. The positive vertices $e_0, \dots, e_m$ all together form a face of $S^m$ (they use distinct axes, one each). Their images must therefore form a face of $S^n$ — which means the images must live on **distinct target axes**. Two source axes are never allowed to collapse onto the same target axis.

That is the *entire* constraint. There is no subtler condition lurking underneath. An antipodal simplicial map of octahedral spheres is nothing more than:

1. an **injection of axes** — a way of assigning the $m+1$ source axes to distinct target axes among the $n+1$ available; together with
2. a **sign vector** — an independent choice of plus-or-minus for each of the $m+1$ source axes.

This is the whole story. The maps are *rigid*: no wiggle room beyond an axis injection and a sign flip on each axis.

## The formula

Counting is now immediate. The number of ways to inject $m+1$ source axes into $n+1$ target axes, keeping them distinct, is the **falling factorial**

$$
(n+1)^{\underline{\,m+1\,}} \;=\; (n+1)\,n\,(n-1)\cdots(n-m+1) \;=\; \frac{(n+1)!}{(n-m)!},
$$

a product of $m+1$ descending terms. The number of sign vectors is $2^{m+1}$, one independent bit per source axis. Multiplying:

$$
\boxed{\;\#\{\text{antipodal maps } S^m \to S^n\} \;=\; 2^{\,m+1}\cdot (n+1)^{\underline{\,m+1\,}} \;=\; 2^{\,m+1}\cdot\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-m)!}.\;}
$$

Let us take it for a spin. How many antipodal maps are there from the circle $S^1$ to the octahedron $S^2$? Here $m=1$, $n=2$, so we get $2^2 \cdot (3\cdot 2) = 4 \cdot 6 = 24$. Exactly twenty-four — no more, no fewer.

## Three classical facts fall out for free

The single formula above is a master key. Turn it three ways and three famous doors open.

### 1. A combinatorial Borsuk–Ulam theorem

When is there *any* antipodal map $S^m \to S^n$ at all? The count is positive exactly when the falling factorial is nonzero — that is, exactly when $m \le n$. If $m > n$, one of the descending factors hits zero (you simply cannot inject more axes than exist), and the count collapses to $0$.

In particular, **there is no antipodal map $S^{n+1} \to S^n$**. You cannot fold a higher combinatorial sphere onto a lower one while respecting antipodes. This is precisely the combinatorial heart of the celebrated **Borsuk–Ulam theorem**, which in its classical form says that every continuous map from the $n$-sphere to $\mathbb{R}^n$ must send some pair of antipodal points to the same value. Here that deep topological fact appears not as a hard theorem to be fought for, but as the trivial observation that $6 \cdot 5 \cdots$ eventually runs out of factors. The largest $m$ for which a map $S^m \to S^n$ exists — a quantity called the **coindex** of $S^n$ — is exactly $n$.

### 2. The hidden symmetry group

What about the maps from a sphere to *itself*? Set $m = n$. The falling factorial becomes $(n+1)^{\underline{\,n+1\,}} = (n+1)!$, a full factorial, and the count is

$$
\#\{\text{antipodal self-maps of } S^n\} \;=\; 2^{\,n+1}\,(n+1)!.
$$

This is no random number. It is exactly the order of the **hyperoctahedral group** $B_{n+1}$ — the group of symmetries of the cross-polytope (equivalently, of the hypercube). Its elements are the **signed permutations**: shuffle the $n+1$ axes in any of $(n+1)!$ ways, then independently flip the sign of any subset of them, for $2^{n+1}$ choices. The count says that *every* antipodal self-map of the octahedral sphere is one of these signed permutations — a genuine symmetry. There are no exotic self-maps hiding in the shadows; the rigidity is total. A self-map cannot merely be a map, it must be an automorphism.

### 3. The suspension tower

There is a natural way to build a bigger sphere from a smaller one, called **suspension**. Geometrically, you take a shape, add two new "pole" points, and cone the whole thing up to each pole — the way a circle, suspended between a North and South Pole, sweeps out a $2$-sphere. Combinatorially, suspension is the *join* with $S^0$ (a single antipodal pair of poles), written $S(K) = K * S^0$. It raises the dimension by one, and — crucially — it raises the coindex by one as well: an antipodal map into $K$ can always be extended over the new poles. Applied to our spheres, suspension does exactly what geometry promises: $S(S^n) \cong S^{n+1}$. Iterating it $k$ times gives the **suspension tower** $S^k(K)$, climbing one dimension per step.

The counting formula sees this tower with perfect clarity, and it draws a sharp line between what suspension preserves and what it does not. Suspend both the source and target of a map — compare $S^{m+k} \to S^{n+k}$ against $S^m \to S^n$. The *possibility* of mapping is completely unchanged: a map exists on one side exactly when it exists on the other, because in both cases the condition is $m \le n$. So the **excess** $n - m$ — the gap between a sphere's dimension and how far it can reach — is a genuine suspension invariant. Climbing the tower never alters whether the target can be reached.

The *number* of maps, however, does change, and the formula tells us exactly how. Each suspension step opens one new axis on both sides: it adds a new sign bit to the source (doubling the sign choices) and enlarges the pool of target axes. Working through the falling factorial, the count multiplies by a factor of $2(n+2)$ at each step:

$$
\#\{\text{antipodal maps } S^{m+1} \to S^{n+1}\} \;=\; 2\,(n+2)\cdot\#\{\text{antipodal maps } S^m \to S^n\}.
$$

For instance, the self-map counts $2, 8, 48, 384, \dots$ for $S^0, S^1, S^2, S^3$ climb by exactly these factors ($4, 6, 8, \dots$). This is the precise sense in which enumeration refines existence: the *positivity* of the count is stable under suspension — that is the classical topology — while the *value* of the count is not, and grows in a completely controlled way.

## Why rigidity is the whole story

Step back and notice the shape of the argument. A question that looked analytic — *how many maps between spheres?* — became, after imposing antipodal symmetry, a question of pure bookkeeping: *how many injections-with-signs?* Symmetry did not complicate the problem; it **collapsed** it. Every antipodal simplicial map of octahedral spheres is forced, by the interplay of equivariance and simpliciality, to be a signed injection of axes and nothing more.

Once that rigidity is seen, the classical consequences are not separate theorems requiring separate insights. They are three readings of one number:

- read its **positivity**, and you have Borsuk–Ulam;
- read its **diagonal value**, and you have the hyperoctahedral symmetry group;
- read the **stability of its positivity under suspension**, and you have the structure of the suspension tower.

There is a lesson here that runs throughout mathematics. The deepest facts are often not the hardest to prove but the ones stated in the right language. Borsuk–Ulam is usually a theorem about continuous functions and connectivity, proved with machinery. Seen through the octahedral sphere and its antipodal symmetry, it is the plain arithmetic remark that a falling factorial runs out of factors. The right object makes the impossible obvious.

## Where this points

Octahedral spheres are the most symmetric free antipodal complexes, and they achieve the maximum possible count — every source axis is free to reach any unused target axis with any sign. That extremality suggests a converse: perhaps the falling-factorial count *characterizes* spheres among all antipodal complexes, singling them out as the unique objects with no constraints beyond the unavoidable one. And by mixing suspensions (which add dimension) with joins (which add reach), one can hope to build antipodal complexes realizing *every* combination of dimension and coindex, turning the excess $n - m$ into a freely tunable dial. Counting, rather than merely deciding, has opened a quantitative window onto a corner of topology that had long been viewed only through the coarser lens of existence. The maps were always there; now we know exactly how many.

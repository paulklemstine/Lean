# Counting the Sublattices of a Crystal

## How a Möbius function on a lattice of subgroups tells you whether a module is free

Imagine an infinite crystal: the set $\mathbb{Z}^n$ of points with integer coordinates in $n$-dimensional space. Now pick a sub-crystal — a subgroup $N \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n$ that still fills out all $n$ directions, so that only finitely many crystal points fall between the two. The *index* $[\mathbb{Z}^n : N]$ counts how much coarser the sub-crystal is.

Here is a question a nineteenth-century crystallographer could have asked and a twenty-first-century number theorist still finds hard: **how many sub-crystals of a given index are there?** For $n = 2$ and index $2$, you can count by hand: there are exactly three, generated by $\{(2,0),(0,1)\}$, $\{(1,0),(0,2)\}$, and $\{(2,0),(1,1)\}$. For index $4$ there are seven. For index $8$, fifteen. The pattern $1, 3, 7, 15, 31, \dots$ is unmistakable, and it is the first hint that something highly structured is going on.

The generating function that packages these counts is the **zeta function of the lattice**:
$$\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}^n}(s) \;=\; \sum_{N \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n} [\mathbb{Z}^n : N]^{-s},$$
the sum running over all finite-index subgroups. For $\mathbb{Z}^n$ it factors beautifully:
$$\zeta_{\mathbb{Z}^n}(s) \;=\; \zeta(s)\,\zeta(s-1)\cdots\zeta(s-n+1),$$
a product of shifted Riemann zeta functions. The powers of $2$ above are the $2$-part of that identity: $1 + 3T + 7T^2 + \cdots = \frac{1}{(1-T)(1-2T)}$ with $T = 2^{-s}$.

That formula is classical and clean. The story gets interesting the moment you replace the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ by something less perfect.

## Orders: crystals with a symmetry group baked in

In number theory and representation theory one rarely gets to work with $\mathbb{Z}^n$ alone. One works with a **lattice over an order**: a crystal that carries an action of some finite ring of symmetries. The archetypal example is the *group ring* $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ for a finite group $G$ — a crystal whose points can be added *and* rotated by the elements of $G$. Its sublattices are the ones stable under all the symmetries. Counting *those* is the subject of **Solomon's zeta function**, introduced by Louis Solomon in the 1970s: for a lattice $M$ over an order $\Lambda$,
$$\zeta_M(s) \;=\; \sum_{\substack{N \subseteq M \ \Lambda\text{-submodule} \\ [M:N] < \infty}} [M:N]^{-s}.$$

Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the answer is a product of Riemann zeta functions. Over a general order, nobody knows a closed formula, and the coefficients encode subtle arithmetic — including, for instance, class-number-type information about the order.

This article is about a strategy for taming those coefficients, and about three theorems it yields: a criterion for group rings over the $p$-adic integers to be *local* rings; a closed formula for the number of sublattices of a free lattice over such an order; and — the punchline — a purely numerical test that decides whether a given lattice is free.

---

## The trick: count surjections instead of sublattices

Sublattices are awkward to count directly. Quotients are much friendlier. Grouping the sublattices $N \subseteq M$ by the isomorphism type of the quotient $M/N$, we can rewrite the zeta function as a sum over *finite quotient types* $X$:
$$\zeta_M(s) \;=\; \sum_{X} \#\{N \subseteq M : M/N \cong X\}\, |X|^{-s},$$
so everything reduces to the counting function $a_M(X) = \#\{N \subseteq M : M/N \cong X\}$.

And here is the reformulation that makes progress possible. A submodule $N$ with $M/N \cong X$ is the same thing as a surjection $M \twoheadrightarrow X$, *except* that surjections with the same kernel differ exactly by an automorphism of $X$. So:

> **Orbit identity.** For every finite quotient type $X$,
> $$\#\mathrm{Aut}(X)\cdot \#\{N \subseteq M : M/N \cong X\} \;=\; \#\mathrm{Surj}(M, X).$$

Counting *surjections* rather than *subobjects* is exactly the sort of thing combinatorics knows how to do: it is a sieve. The number of homomorphisms $M \to X$ that hit a specified submodule $Y \subseteq X$ can be sorted by image, giving $\#\mathrm{Hom}(M,Y) = \sum_{Z \subseteq Y} \#\mathrm{Surj}(M,Z)$, and inverting this by Möbius inversion **on the lattice of submodules of $X$** gives the master formula:

> **The Möbius weight.** For every module $M$ and every finite quotient type $X$,
> $$\#\mathrm{Surj}(M,X) \;=\; \sum_{Y \subseteq X} \mu(Y,X)\, \#\mathrm{Hom}(M,Y),$$
> where $\mu$ is the Möbius function of the partially ordered set of submodules of $X$.

Call the right-hand side the *Möbius weight* $w_n(M,X)$ of the pair. It is a strange hybrid: the left factor $\mu(Y,X)$ is pure order theory, knowing nothing about $M$; the right factor $\#\mathrm{Hom}(M,Y)$ is pure module theory, knowing nothing about the poset structure. The whole subject lives in the tension between them.

Two consequences are immediate and worth stating, because they are used constantly. First, since $\#\mathrm{Hom}(M_1 \oplus M_2, Y) = \#\mathrm{Hom}(M_1,Y)\cdot\#\mathrm{Hom}(M_2,Y)$ — a homomorphism out of a direct sum is a pair of homomorphisms — the weight of a decomposable lattice is computed from the *Hom-count functions* of its summands:
$$w(M_1\oplus\cdots\oplus M_m, X) \;=\; \sum_{Y\subseteq X}\mu(Y,X)\prod_{i=1}^{m}\#\mathrm{Hom}(M_i,Y).$$
Second, and more strikingly, the weight sees $M$ **only** through the function $Y \mapsto \#\mathrm{Hom}(M,Y)$: two modules with matching Hom-counts into every submodule of $X$ have the same weight at $X$. The module $M$ enters the theory purely as a "counting profile".

## Hall's formula: the answer over $\mathbb{Z}$

Over the integers this machinery produces a completely explicit answer for every quotient type. Fix a prime $p$ and a finite abelian $p$-group $X$; write $d = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} X/pX$ for the minimal number of generators of $X$ (the *residual dimension*), and let $pX$ be the subgroup of $p$-th multiples. Then:

> **Theorem (Hall's formula).** For every $n$,
> $$\#\mathrm{Aut}(X)\cdot\#\{N \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n : \mathbb{Z}^n/N \cong X\} \;=\; \Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}(p^n - p^i)\Big)\cdot |pX|^{\,n}.$$

Read the right-hand side twice. The first factor is the number of $d$-tuples of linearly independent vectors in $\mathbb{F}_p^{\,n}$ — the number of ways to choose the "top layer" of a surjection modulo $p$. The second factor is the number of ways to lift that choice to the rest of $X$, one free choice in $pX$ for each of the $n$ coordinates. The identity says the naive count is the correct count: **once you have chosen a surjection modulo $p$, nothing further can obstruct you.**

The reason nothing can obstruct you is Nakayama's lemma, and here it appears in a form that needs no local ring at all. If an ideal $I$ satisfies $I^e X = 0$ and a submodule $Y$ satisfies $Y + IX = X$, then $Y = X$ — substitute the equation into itself $e$ times and watch $IX$ shrink to zero. Applied to $I = (p)$, this says: **generating $X$ modulo $p$ is generating $X$.** The Möbius sum over the whole submodule lattice of $X$ therefore collapses to a sum over the tiny lattice of subspaces of the vector space $X/pX$, rescaled by the $|pX|^n$ lifting factor. What is left is the count of spanning tuples in $\mathbb{F}_p^{\,n}$, which is the product $\prod_{i<d}(p^n-p^i)$.

Two special cases deserve names. When $X = (\mathbb{Z}/p)^d$ is elementary abelian, $pX = 0$ and dividing by $\#\mathrm{GL}_d(\mathbb{F}_p)$ turns the formula into the **Gaussian binomial coefficient** $\binom{n}{d}_p$ — the number of $d$-codimensional subspaces of $\mathbb{F}_p^{\,n}$. When $X = \mathbb{Z}/m$ is cyclic, the formula becomes
$$\varphi(m)\cdot\#\{N \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n : \mathbb{Z}^n/N \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\} \;=\; \sum_{d \mid m}\mu(d)\left(\frac{m}{d}\right)^{n} \;=\; J_n(m),$$
Jordan's totient function. On the left, $\mu$ is the Möbius function of a lattice of subgroups; on the right, $\mu$ is the classical arithmetic Möbius function of elementary number theory. The two are the same function wearing different clothes — the lattice-theoretic $\mu$ is *carried onto* the arithmetic one by this counting problem. That coincidence is the reason the subject deserves the name "zeta function" at all.

## Leaving the comfort zone: group rings

Now put the symmetries back. Let $p$ be a prime and let $G$ be a finite abelian group whose order is a power of $p$; work over the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and form the group ring $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}_p[G]$. This is a genuinely worse ring than $\mathbb{Z}_p$: it has zero divisors (for $G = \mathbb{Z}/p$ generated by $x$, the elements $x-1$ and $1+x+\cdots+x^{p-1}$ multiply to zero), and it is not integrally closed. Its lattices are exactly the objects of classical integral representation theory.

Yet the counting formula survives verbatim, and the reason is a single structural fact.

> **Theorem (Locality of $p$-adic group rings).** For a finite abelian group $G$ of exponent dividing $p^e$, the ring $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}_p[G]$ is *local*: it has exactly one maximal ideal, namely the set of elements whose coefficient sum lies in $p\mathbb{Z}_p$, and its residue field is $\mathbb{F}_p$.

A local ring is one where the non-units form an ideal — the algebraic analogue of a space with a single point. The proof has a pleasing two-step shape. Modulo $p$, the group ring becomes $\mathbb{F}_p[G]$, where the *augmentation ideal* (spanned by the differences $g - 1$) is nilpotent, because for a group of exponent $p^e$ in characteristic $p$ one has $(g-1)^{p^e} = g^{p^e} - 1 = 0$. So $\Lambda$ modulo $p$ is a local ring in the crudest possible way. The second step is to climb back up, and this is the general principle that makes the argument work:

> **Theorem (Locality ascent).** Let $R$ be a commutative local ring with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, and let $\Lambda$ be a commutative $R$-algebra that is finitely generated as an $R$-module. Suppose $\Lambda$ has a maximal ideal $J$ such that every element of $J$ has some power lying in $\mathfrak{m}\Lambda$. Then $\Lambda$ is local with maximal ideal $J$.

The hypothesis is "nilpotence modulo the base", which is much weaker than nilpotence: over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ the element $p$ is not nilpotent, but it *is* in $\mathfrak{m}\Lambda$, so it costs nothing. The missing invertibility is supplied by Nakayama's lemma in the form of a unit criterion: if $x\Lambda + \mathfrak{m}\Lambda = \Lambda$ and $\Lambda$ is module-finite over $R$, then $x$ is already a unit. From this, the geometric series $(1-a)(1 + a + \cdots + a^{N-1}) = 1 - a^{N}$ shows that $1 - a$ is a unit whenever some $a^N$ lies in $\mathfrak{m}\Lambda$, and the standard "every element or its complement is a unit" criterion for locality does the rest.

Once $\Lambda$ is known to be local with residue field of size $q$, the same Nakayama collapse as over $\mathbb{Z}$ gives the count. Writing $\mathfrak{m}$ for the maximal ideal of $\Lambda$:

> **Theorem (Free Solomon coefficients over $\mathbb{Z}_p[G]$).** For every finite $\Lambda$-module $X$ with residual dimension $d = \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} X/\mathfrak{m}X$ and every rank $n$,
> $$\#\mathrm{Aut}(X)\cdot\#\{N \subseteq \Lambda^n : \Lambda^n/N \cong X\} \;=\; \Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}(p^n - p^i)\Big)\cdot|\mathfrak{m}X|^{\,n}.$$

Exactly the shape of Hall's formula, over a non-maximal, non-domain order. And note what the formula *says*: the coefficient depends on the isomorphism type of $X$ only through the two numbers $|\mathfrak{m}X|$ and $d$. Enormously different modules — with wildly different endomorphism rings, different socle structure, different everything — contribute identically as long as those two invariants agree. The Solomon coefficients of a *free* lattice simply cannot see the finer structure of $X$.

You can check this by hand in the smallest case. Take $p = 2$ and work in $(\mathbb{Z}/4)[x]/(x^2-1)$, a $16$-element ring, with $\mathfrak{m}$ the ideal of elements whose coefficients sum to an even number. For $X = \Lambda$ itself we have $d = 1$ and $|\mathfrak{m}X| = 8$, so the formula predicts $(2^2-1)\cdot 8^2 = 192$ surjections $\Lambda^2 \twoheadrightarrow \Lambda$ — and an exhaustive scan of the $256$ pairs of ring elements finds exactly the $192$ pairs that generate.

## The punchline: freeness is a counting phenomenon

Free modules are the good ones — the ones with a basis, the ones you can do linear algebra with. Over $\mathbb{Z}_p$ every finitely generated torsion-free module is free, but over an order like $\mathbb{Z}_p[\mathbb{Z}/p]$ the lattices come in three indecomposable flavours ($\mathbb{Z}_p$ with trivial action, the ring of $p$-th cyclotomic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p[\zeta_p]$, and $\Lambda$ itself), of which only one is free. Deciding whether a given lattice is free is a real question, and classically one answers it with structure theory: find a basis, or find an obstruction.

The last theorem says you can answer it by *counting alone*.

> **Theorem (Möbius characterisation of freeness).** Let $R$ be a noetherian commutative local ring with finite residue field $k$, $q = |k|$, and let $M$ be a finitely generated $R$-module. Then $M$ is free of rank $n$ **if and only if** for every finite quotient type $X$,
> $$\sum_{Y \subseteq X}\mu(Y,X)\,\#\mathrm{Hom}(M,Y) \;=\; \Big(\prod_{i=0}^{d-1}(q^n - q^i)\Big)\cdot|\mathfrak{m}X|^{\,n}, \qquad d = \dim_k X/\mathfrak{m}X.$$

In words: **a module is free exactly when it has the counting profile of a free module.** No basis need be exhibited; matching the numbers is enough. Applied to $\Lambda = \mathbb{Z}_p[G]$ — which is noetherian because it is module-finite over $\mathbb{Z}_p$, and local by the theorem above — this becomes a purely arithmetic criterion for freeness over an integral group ring, separating the free lattice $\Lambda$ from $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $\mathbb{Z}_p[\zeta_p]$ by a table of integers.

The engine of the proof is a rigidity phenomenon that I find genuinely surprising, and it goes like this. Suppose $\pi : R^n \twoheadrightarrow M$ is any surjection (one exists as soon as $M$ needs at most $n$ generators, which the weights already tell us). Composing with $\pi$ turns a surjection $M \twoheadrightarrow X$ into a surjection $R^n \twoheadrightarrow X$, and since $\pi$ is onto, distinct maps stay distinct: we get an *injection* of finite sets
$$\mathrm{Surj}(M,X) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{Surj}(R^n, X).$$
This alone proves that **free modules maximise the Möbius weights**: no $n$-generated module can surject onto anything more often than $R^n$ does. But now suppose the two counts are *equal*. Then the injection is a bijection, so every surjection $R^n \twoheadrightarrow X$ factors through $M$ — which forces every one of them to kill $\ker \pi$.

Feed the family of test spaces $X = R^n/\mathfrak{m}^j R^n$ into this machine, one for each $j$. The projection $R^n \to R^n/\mathfrak{m}^jR^n$ is itself a surjection, so it must kill $\ker\pi$: that is, $\ker \pi \subseteq \mathfrak{m}^j R^n$ for every $j$. Krull's intersection theorem says the intersection of all the $\mathfrak{m}^jR^n$ is zero. Hence $\ker \pi = 0$, and $\pi$ is an isomorphism: $M$ is free.

The whole argument is a squeeze. The weights bound the number of generators from above; the rigidity bounds the kernel from above; the intersection theorem crushes the kernel to nothing.

## What the numbers cannot do

There is a natural guess that goes with a theorem like this: surely you don't need *all* quotient types $X$? The residual dimension of $M$ is detected by a single test space, the vector space $k^{\,n+1}$: a free module of rank $n$ never surjects onto a module requiring $n+1$ generators, so its weight there is $0$, and if $M$'s weight there is also $0$ then $M$ needs at most $n$ generators. Perhaps that one test suffices?

It does not, and the counterexample is as small as it could possibly be. Over $R = \mathbb{Z}_p$ take $n = 1$ and $M = \mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p$. Neither $M$ nor the free module $\mathbb{Z}_p$ surjects onto the two-dimensional space $\mathbb{F}_p^2$, so both weights at that test space are $0$ and the test is passed. But $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is finite and $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is not, so $M$ is emphatically not free of rank one. The witness that separates them is a different test space: at $X = \mathbb{Z}/p^2$ the free lattice has weight $p(p-1) = \varphi(p^2)$ (one surjection $\mathbb{Z}_p \to \mathbb{Z}/p^2$ for each generator of the target) while $\mathbb{Z}/p$ has weight $0$, since a module killed by $p$ cannot surject onto one that is not.

So the family of test spaces is genuinely needed, and the sharp statement is: *residual dimension is a one-space invariant, but freeness is not.*

## Why any of this matters

Zeta functions of groups and rings — the subject that grew out of Solomon's work and the work of Grunewald, Segal and Smith in the 1980s — measure the "subobject growth" of algebraic structures. How many subgroups of index $n$ does a nilpotent group have? How many sublattices of index $n$ does an order have? The answers are Dirichlet series whose analytic behaviour (abscissa of convergence, meromorphic continuation, functional equations) reflects deep structural properties, and whose local factors are rational functions in $p^{-s}$ whose shape nobody fully understands.

The results above chip at that from the counting side. They say: over any order that is local — and $p$-adic group rings of $p$-groups all are — the sublattice counts of a *free* lattice are completely explicit, given by a Gaussian-binomial numerator times a power of $|\mathfrak{m}X|$; these counts are the largest possible among $n$-generated lattices; and they characterise freeness exactly. In generating-function terms, the local zeta function of a free lattice over such an order is a finite sum of geometric series indexed by the residual dimension $d \le n$, with denominator $\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(1 - q^iT)$ — the same denominator as the classical $\zeta(s)\zeta(s-1)\cdots\zeta(s-n+1)$, now over a ring that is far from being a Dedekind domain.

And there is the aesthetic payoff, which is why I would tell this story even if it had no applications. The Möbius function of a poset is a combinatorial gadget invented to invert sums. The Euler totient and Jordan's $J_n$ are arithmetic functions from a different century and a different subject. The theory of orders and lattices belongs to representation theory. In the identity
$$\varphi(m)\cdot\#\{N \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^n : \mathbb{Z}^n/N \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\} = \sum_{d\mid m}\mu(d)(m/d)^n$$
all three subjects are visibly the same subject. Counting the sublattices of a crystal is, in the end, an exercise in inclusion–exclusion — you just have to be inclusion–excluding on the right lattice.

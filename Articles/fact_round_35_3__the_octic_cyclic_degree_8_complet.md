# Seven Quarters of a Bit: How Primes Split in an Eight-Sided Number World

*Why the number 17 hides exactly $7/4$ bits of information about every prime, and why that number was always going to be $7/4$.*

---

## A prime walks into a bigger number system

Most of us learn that primes are atoms: $7$ is prime, $13$ is prime, and that is the end of it. Number theorists know that this is only true in the ordinary integers. If you enlarge the number system, adding new "numbers" such as $\sqrt{17}$ or a root of some cleverly chosen polynomial, a prime that looked indivisible can break apart. Sometimes it shatters into many pieces. Sometimes it splits into a few. Sometimes it stays whole.

How a prime breaks up in a bigger number system is called its **splitting type**. A remarkable fact of nineteenth- and twentieth-century number theory is that for an important family of number systems, the splitting type of a prime $p$ is decided entirely by **the remainder of $p$ after division by a fixed number**. You do not need to know anything else about $p$. Divide by $17$, read off the remainder, and you know how $p$ will behave.

This article is about one such number system: an eight-dimensional world built from the number $17$. We measure, in bits, how much "surprise" a prime's splitting type carries, and how much of that surprise the remainder mod $17$ removes. The answers are exact: $H(T) = 7/4$ bits of uncertainty, all of it removed by the remainder. The more interesting point is that $7/4$ is no accident. It is one rung of a ladder of values that can be written down for **every** prime, and the ladder is built from a single, almost childishly simple counting principle.

## The octic field

Take the complex number $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/17}$, one of the seventeenth roots of unity: points spaced evenly around the unit circle, sixteen of them besides $1$ itself. Gauss famously used these numbers at age nineteen to show that a regular 17-gon can be constructed with ruler and compass. The key quantity was
$$\zeta + \zeta^{-1} = 2\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{17}\right).$$

Adding this real number to the rationals gives a number system, written $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})^+$. It is the **real cyclotomic field of conductor $17$**. It has dimension $8$ over the rationals, which is why we call it *octic*. Its symmetries form a cyclic group of order $8$: the symmetries are generated by a single "rotation" applied over and over, like the eight positions of a clock hand that ticks in eighths.

In this field, an ordinary prime $p \neq 17$ breaks into $8/d$ equal pieces, each of "size" $d$. This number $d$ is the **residue degree**, and it can only be $1$, $2$, $4$ or $8$:

- $d = 1$: $p$ splits completely into eight pieces;
- $d = 8$: $p$ stays inert, a single unbroken piece;
- $d = 2$ or $4$: something in between.

## The splitting law of 17

Here is the rule. Compute $u = p \bmod 17$. Then the residue degree is **the smallest $k \ge 1$ with $u^k \equiv \pm 1 \pmod{17}$**. Working it out once for every remainder gives a tidy table:

| remainder $p \bmod 17$ | residue degree | how $p$ splits |
|---|---|---|
| $\pm 1$ | $1$ | into 8 pieces |
| $\pm 4$ | $2$ | into 4 pieces |
| $\pm 2,\ \pm 8$ | $4$ | into 2 pieces |
| $\pm 3,\ \pm 5,\ \pm 6,\ \pm 7$ | $8$ | stays whole |

So $103 \equiv 1$ splits completely, $13 \equiv -4$ has degree $2$, the prime $2$ has degree $4$ (since $2^4 = 16 \equiv -1$), and $3$ stays inert. Three is a *primitive root* mod 17: its powers run through every nonzero remainder.

Count the remainders. Of the sixteen possible nonzero remainders, $2$ give degree $1$, $2$ give degree $2$, $4$ give degree $4$, and $8$ give degree $8$. By Dirichlet's theorem on primes in progressions, primes are spread evenly over these remainders in the long run, so the four splitting types occur with frequencies
$$\tfrac18,\quad \tfrac18,\quad \tfrac14,\quad \tfrac12 .$$
Experiments record "12%, 12%, 25%, 50%". The two 12% values are really $12.5\%$ rounded down.

## Measuring surprise in bits

Claude Shannon taught us to measure uncertainty in **bits**. A fair coin carries one bit. A fair choice among four options carries two. In general, a random outcome taking values with probabilities $q_1, q_2, \dots$ carries
$$H = -\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i$$
bits of entropy.

For the splitting type $T$ of a random prime in the octic field:
$$H(T) = \tfrac18\log_2 8 + \tfrac18\log_2 8 + \tfrac14\log_2 4 + \tfrac12\log_2 2 = \tfrac38 + \tfrac38 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac74 .$$

So the splitting type carries **exactly $7/4 = 1.75$ bits**. The experimental value was $1.7474$. That is below $7/4$ by less than $0.003$, and the direction is what one expects: estimating entropy by plugging observed frequencies into Shannon's formula tends to come out slightly low. Counting the primes up to a million gives frequencies $0.1246, 0.1244, 0.2499, 0.5012$ and a plug-in entropy of about $1.7478$, closing in on $1.75$ from below.

## Full pinning

Now ask how much the remainder $p \bmod 17$ tells you about the splitting type. In Shannon's language this is the **mutual information** $I(p \bmod 17;\, T)$: the drop in uncertainty about $T$ once you know the remainder.

Because the splitting law above turns the remainder into the type, knowing the remainder leaves **no** uncertainty at all. So
$$I(p \bmod 17;\, T) = H(T) = \tfrac74 .$$
We call this **full pinning**: the observable pins down the type completely. An even coarser observable already does it. The splitting type depends on $u$ only up to sign ($u$ and $-u$ always have the same type), so knowing the pair $\{u, -u\}$ is enough.

One should be honest about what this means. Full pinning is not a surprise once you know the splitting law. It is the information-theoretic way of saying that splitting is governed by congruences. The content is in the *number*, $7/4$, and in the question of why that number comes out as it does.

## Not every clue pins

To see that pinning is a real property and not automatic, try a weaker clue: the **Legendre symbol**, which only records whether $p$ is a perfect square mod 17. It is a fair coin. Eight remainders are squares and eight are not.

- If $p$ is **not** a square, its type is always $8$: no uncertainty left.
- If $p$ **is** a square, the type is spread as $\{1 : 2,\ 2 : 2,\ 4 : 4\}$ over eight remainders, which leaves $3/2$ bits.

On average, $\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 = \tfrac34$ bits remain. The Legendre symbol therefore tells you $7/4 - 3/4 = 1$ bit about the octic type. That is useful, but it does not pin.

## A tower of fields, a staircase of bits

The octic field contains a chain of smaller fields:
$$\mathbb{Q} \;\subset\; \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{17}) \;\subset\; K_4 \;\subset\; \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})^+,$$
of dimensions $1, 2, 4, 8$. Each layer has its own Frobenius observable, the "clue" a prime gives off in that layer. For a remainder $u$ these are $u^8$ (which is the Legendre symbol, by Euler's criterion), then $u^4$, then $u^2$.

How much does each layer know about the top-level type? The answer is a clean staircase:

| layer | dimension | information about the octic type |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{17})$ | $2$ | $1$ bit |
| $K_4$ | $4$ | $3/2$ bits |
| $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{17})^+$ | $8$ | $7/4$ bits (full pinning) |

Each number in the right-hand column is exactly the entropy of that layer's **own** splitting type. A layer tells you about the top type precisely what it knows about itself, and nothing more.

## The ladder: why $7/4$?

Here is the conceptual heart of the matter. Forget number fields for a moment and think of a clock with $n$ hours, the cyclic group $C_n$. Pick an hour $a$ at random and ask: how many ticks of size $a$ does it take to get back to $12$? That number is the **order** of $a$, equal to $n / \gcd(a, n)$. Call the entropy of this order, for a uniformly random $a$, the **type entropy** of $n$:
$$\mathcal{H}(n) = H\big(\text{order of a random element of } C_n\big).$$

On an 8-hour clock, the hour $0$ has order $1$, the hour $4$ has order $2$, the hours $2$ and $6$ have order $4$, and the four odd hours have order $8$. These are the same frequencies $\tfrac18, \tfrac18, \tfrac14, \tfrac12$ as before, and the entropy is $\mathcal{H}(8) = 7/4$.

That is not a coincidence. It is an instance of the main result:

> **The Real-Cyclotomic Ladder Theorem.** For *every* odd prime $f$, the entropy of the splitting type of primes in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)^+$, computed over the $f - 1$ nonzero remainders mod $f$, equals the type entropy of the clock with $(f-1)/2$ hours:
> $$H(T) = \mathcal{H}\!\left(\tfrac{f-1}{2}\right).$$

Earlier work had this "ladder" only for those $f$ where $(f-1)/2$ is itself prime: $f = 5, 7, 11, 23, \dots$. The octic field, with $(17-1)/2 = 8$, fell through the cracks. The theorem removes that restriction completely. $f = 13$ (degree $6$), $f = 17$ (degree $8$), $f = 37$ (degree $18$) all sit on the ladder on equal footing.

## One idea does all the work

The proof rests on a principle anyone can check with a deck of cards.

> **Uniform-Cover Lemma.** Suppose a map from a finite set $S$ onto a finite set $T$ has all its fibres the same size $c$. That is, every point of $T$ is hit exactly $c$ times. Then for any labelling of $T$, the entropy of the labels is the same whether you pick a uniform random point of $T$ or a uniform random point of $S$ and push it down.

Each label's probability is unchanged, because every label's share gets multiplied by $c$ in the numerator and the total by $c$ in the denominator. The proof uses the lemma twice:

1. **Discrete logarithm ($c = 1$).** Choose a primitive root $g$ mod $f$. The map $a \mapsto g^a$, for $a = 0, 1, \dots, f-2$, hits every nonzero remainder exactly once. It is a relabelling.
2. **Folding by the sign ($c = 2$).** The residue degree of $g^a$ turns out to be the order of $a \bmod \frac{f-1}{2}$ on the $\frac{f-1}{2}$-hour clock. This is because $g^{(f-1)/2} \equiv -1$, and the field cannot tell $u$ from $-u$. The map $a \mapsto a \bmod \frac{f-1}{2}$ from $\{0, \dots, f-2\}$ to $\{0, \dots, \frac{f-1}{2} - 1\}$ is exactly two-to-one.

Two uniform covers, and the arithmetic of primes in $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)^+$ turns into the combinatorics of a clock. The same argument also gives an **exact type-count law**: for every divisor $d$ of $(f-1)/2$, exactly $2\varphi(d)$ remainders mod $f$ give residue degree $d$. Here $\varphi$ is Euler's totient. For $f = 17$ this gives $2\varphi(1), 2\varphi(2), 2\varphi(4), 2\varphi(8) = 2, 2, 4, 8$, the census from before.

## Climbing toward two bits

When the clock has $2^m$ hours, there is a closed form:
$$\mathcal{H}(2^m) = 2 - 2^{1-m}.$$
The rungs are $0,\ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ \tfrac74,\ \tfrac{15}{8},\ \tfrac{31}{16}, \dots$. Each step closes half the remaining gap to **two bits**, which the ladder approaches but never reaches. The proof uses the fact that $2^k$ elements of the $2^m$-clock have order $2^{k+1}$, together with the identity $\sum_{k<m} 2^k (m-k) = 2^{m+1} - m - 2$.

These two-power clocks show up in nature at the **Fermat primes** $f = 2^{m+1} + 1$: $3, 5, 17, 257, 65537$. These are exactly the primes for which Gauss's ruler-and-compass construction of the regular $f$-gon works. For each of them, $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)^+$ is cyclic of degree $2^m$, and its splitting type carries exactly $2 - 2^{1-m}$ bits:

| Fermat prime $f$ | degree | $H(T)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $3$ | $1$ | $0$ |
| $5$ | $2$ | $1$ |
| $17$ | $8$ | $7/4$ |
| $257$ | $128$ | $127/64$ |
| $65537$ | $32768$ | $2 - 2^{-14}$ |

So Gauss's 17-gon field sits on the $m = 3$ rung. It is the first Fermat field whose degree is not prime, which is why the old prime-degree ladder missed it.

## Two primes at once

A related experiment looks at products of two primes, $N = pq$ (semiprimes, the raw material of RSA cryptography). Think of each prime as a random hour on the 8-hour clock, so the remainder of $N$ corresponds to the sum of the two hours. How much does the *pair* of splitting types tell you about $N \bmod 17$? In this clock model the answer is exactly $21/16 = 1.3125$ bits (the experiment reported $1.3097$, again slightly low). Knowing *which* factor has which type adds **exactly zero**. Swapping the two factors leaves $N$ unchanged, so the order of the pair carries no information about it. (The experiment reported a "which-factor" gain of $0.0002$, which is just noise.) Numerical values for the 2-, 4-, 8-, 16- and 32-hour clocks fit $\tfrac43(1 - 4^{-m})$, which suggests a ceiling of $4/3$ bits. That pattern is still a conjecture.

## Why it matters

The splitting of primes is the subject of class field theory and of the Chebotarev density theorem, two cornerstones of modern number theory. Looking at it through information theory gives a single number for each field, the entropy of its splitting type, and the results here show that for real cyclotomic fields of prime conductor this number is purely combinatorial. It does not depend on deep analytic facts, only on the shape of a cyclic group and a two-to-one fold.

Several questions remain open. Is the staircase of the octic tower a general law, with every subfield telling the top field exactly its own type entropy? (Numerical experiments on small clocks are consistent with it, but it has not been proved.) For other prime powers $p^m$, does the type entropy climb toward a ceiling of $\tfrac{p}{p-1}\log_2 p - \log_2(p-1)$ bits? Among all number fields of a given degree whose symmetries commute, are the cyclic ones always the most "surprising"? For example, the 4-hour clock carries $3/2$ bits, while the symmetry group of a rectangle, $C_2 \times C_2$, carries only about $0.811$.

For now, at least one rung is settled. Divide a prime by $17$, look at the remainder, and you hold exactly seven quarters of a bit about how that prime breaks apart in Gauss's world.

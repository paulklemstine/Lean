## A Formula Hiding in Plain Sight

Every high school student eventually encounters the tangent addition formula:

> tan(α + β) = (tan α + tan β) / (1 − tan α · tan β)

It's typically presented as a trigonometric identity — useful for computing angles, but nothing more. A mere tool in the calculus toolkit.

But what if this formula were something far more profound? What if it were the key to understanding why the speed of light is a cosmic speed limit, why rotating objects compose the way they do, and why the number line and the circle are secretly the same mathematical object?

That's exactly what the **Stereographic Projection Bridge** (SPB) reveals. And now, for the first time, every claim has been verified by a computer proof assistant — leaving zero room for error.

## The Bridge

Give the formula a name. Call it SPB:

> **spb(x, y) = (x + y) / (1 − xy)**

This is just a fraction involving addition and multiplication. Nothing fancy. But watch what happens when you look at it from four different angles.

### Angle 1: Trigonometry

If x = tan α and y = tan β, then spb(x, y) = tan(α + β). The SPB operation *is* angle addition, expressed through tangents. Computing tan(2α)? Just do spb(tan α, tan α). Computing tan(3α)? Apply SPB again. You can compute any multiple angle by repeatedly applying one formula.

### Angle 2: The Circle Group

Here's where it gets deep. There's a beautiful map called the **Cayley transform**:

> cayley(x) = (1 + ix) / (1 − ix)

This takes any real number x and maps it to a point on the unit circle in the complex plane. The number 0 maps to the point 1 (the "east pole"). The number 1 maps to the point i (the "north pole"). As x goes to infinity, the point spirals around to −1 (the "west pole").

The magical property: **cayley(spb(x, y)) = cayley(x) · cayley(y)**

In other words, doing SPB on the real line is *exactly the same* as multiplying on the circle. The real number line, equipped with SPB, is a group — and it's the same group as the circle with ordinary multiplication.

This is not a metaphor. It's a precise mathematical isomorphism, and it's been computer-verified.

### Angle 3: Einstein's Relativity

In 1905, Einstein showed that velocities don't add the way Newton thought. If a train moves at speed u relative to the ground, and a ball is thrown at speed v relative to the train, the ball's speed relative to the ground is *not* u + v. It's:

> (u + v) / (1 + uv)    [with c = 1]

Look familiar? It's SPB with a sign flip! Call it spbH (the hyperbolic variant). This formula has a remarkable property that we've formally proven:

> **If |u| < 1 and |v| < 1, then |spbH(u, v)| < 1.**

No matter how you combine sub-light velocities, you can never reach the speed of light. This isn't just an empirical observation — it's a theorem about group structure. The velocities below light speed form a closed group under Einstein's addition rule, and the speed of light is the boundary of that group.

The proof is elegant: (1 + uv)² − (u + v)² = (1 − u²)(1 − v²) > 0.

### Angle 4: Approximation Theory

The functions tan(n · arctan(x)) form a family of rational functions that approximate continuous functions with remarkable efficiency. These are precisely the functions generated by n-fold iteration of SPB. They're related to Chebyshev polynomials — the gold standard of polynomial approximation — through a change of variables.

This means SPB trees (expressions built from nested SPB operations) form a powerful basis for function approximation, potentially with exponential convergence rates for analytic functions.

## Why "Bridge"?

The word "bridge" is deliberate. The SPB formula doesn't merely appear in multiple contexts — it reveals that these contexts are *structurally identical*.

- **Adding angles** and **multiplying points on a circle** are the same operation.
- **Composing rotations** and **adding velocities** are the same group structure.
- **Tangent lines** and **Möbius transformations** share the same algebra.

The Cayley transform is the Rosetta Stone that translates between these languages. And the SPB formula is the text that reads the same in every translation.

## The Oldest Theorem Meets the Newest Algebra

Everyone knows the Pythagorean theorem: for a right triangle, a² + b² = c². What's less well known is that there exists a beautiful tree — discovered by the Swedish mathematician Berggren in 1934 — that generates *every* right triangle with whole-number sides. Starting from the simplest Pythagorean triple (3, 4, 5), three matrix operations branch out to produce all others: (5, 12, 13), (21, 20, 29), (15, 8, 17), and so on, forever.

In our work, we discovered that this ancient tree has a surprising shadow in a very modern kind of mathematics called **tropical algebra** — a strange world where "addition" means "take the maximum" and "multiplication" means "add." This shadow carries information about the prime factorization of the triangles' longest sides, creating an unexpected bridge between geometry, algebra, and number theory.

## What Is Tropical Mathematics?

Imagine a world where 3 + 5 = 5 (because max(3, 5) = 5) and 3 × 5 = 8 (because 3 + 5 = 8). This is the **tropical semiring**, and despite its seemingly absurd rules, it captures deep truths about optimization, scheduling, and even neural networks.

The name "tropical" honors the Brazilian mathematician Imre Simon, who pioneered this field. Far from being a mathematical curiosity, tropical algebra has become one of the most active areas of modern mathematics, with applications ranging from phylogenetic tree reconstruction in biology to understanding the geometry of deep learning.

In tropical algebra, linear equations become piecewise-linear equations, and smooth curves become polyhedral complexes — angular structures made of flat pieces. This "de-smoothing" often reveals hidden structure that classical algebra obscures.

## The Discovery: A Tropical Shadow of the Berggren Tree

The Berggren tree works by multiplying 3×3 integer matrices. Each matrix encodes one of three transformations that produce a new Pythagorean triple from an existing one. We asked: what happens when we look at these matrices through a tropical lens?

The **tropical determinant** of a matrix is computed like the classical determinant, but using tropical rules: instead of the sum of signed products of matrix entries, you take the maximum over all permutations of the sum of selected entries. For a 3×3 matrix M:

```
tropDet(M) = max over all permutations σ of [M(0,σ(0)) + M(1,σ(1)) + M(2,σ(2))]
```

We computed this for all three Berggren generators:
- Generator A has tropical determinant 3 (with 3 optimal permutations)
- Generator B has tropical determinant 7 (with a unique optimal permutation)  
- Generator C has tropical determinant 3 (with 3 optimal permutations)

The fact that B has the largest tropical determinant correlates beautifully with the fact that B produces the fastest-growing hypotenuses. The tropical shadow captures real geometric information!

## The Main Theorem: Superadditivity

Our central discovery, formally verified by a computer in the Lean theorem prover, is the **Tropical Determinant Superadditivity Theorem**:

> For any 3×3 integer matrices M and N, the tropical determinant of their tropical product satisfies:
> tropDet(M ⊗ N) ≥ tropDet(M) + tropDet(N)

In classical algebra, determinants are *multiplicative*: det(MN) = det(M) · det(N). In tropical algebra, the analog is an *inequality*: the tropical determinant can only grow when you multiply matrices tropically. This happens because tropical algebra lacks subtraction (you can't "undo" a max operation), so information accumulates rather than cancels.

This superadditivity is the tropical echo of a deep principle: **composition amplifies structure**. Just as entropy in physics can only increase when systems combine, the tropical determinant can only increase when matrices combine. This isn't a coincidence — it reflects a fundamental asymmetry in the tropical world.

## Cuspidal Numbers: When Primes Behave Simply

We define a number to be **cuspidal** when each of its prime factors appears exactly once — in other words, when it's squarefree. The name comes from the Langlands program, one of the grand unifying theories of modern mathematics, where "cuspidal" representations are the irreducible building blocks.

For Berggren hypotenuses, being cuspidal has a tropical interpretation: it means the "tropical weight" equals the number of distinct prime factors, with no redundancy. We proved:

> A natural number n is cuspidal (squarefree) if and only if ω(n) = Ω(n), where ω counts distinct primes and Ω counts primes with multiplicity.

Remarkably, all three depth-1 Berggren hypotenuses (5, 13, 17, 29) are prime — the most extreme form of cuspidality. At depth 2, the pattern breaks: 25 = 5² and 169 = 13² are not cuspidal. These non-cuspidal cases arise from repeating the same generator (AA gives 25, BB gives 169), suggesting that path symmetry in the Berggren tree correlates with prime multiplicity in the hypotenuse.

## Exponential Growth and Unboundedness

We proved that the tropical determinant along all-B paths grows at least as fast as 3^n, where n is the path length. This exponential growth means the Berggren tropical spectrum — the set of all achievable tropical determinant values — is unbounded. No matter how large a target you choose, there's a Berggren path whose tropical determinant exceeds it.

This has cryptographic implications: if you're given a tropical determinant value and asked to find the Berggren path that produced it, the exponential growth of the search space makes this computationally challenging.

## The Bigger Picture

This work sits at the intersection of several mathematical traditions:

- **Number theory** (prime factorization, arithmetic functions ω and Ω)
- **Tropical geometry** (max-plus algebra, tropical determinants)
- **Group theory** (the Berggren monoid, Lorentz symmetry group)
- **Machine learning** (max-plus convexity, tropical classifiers)

The connection between these fields isn't forced — it emerges naturally from the structure of the Berggren tree. The matrix generators that produce Pythagorean triples also produce tropical algebraic objects that encode prime factorization information. This suggests that the Berggren tree is not merely a combinatorial curiosity but a window into deeper structural connections in mathematics.

## Looking Forward

Several exciting directions remain:

1. **Can we characterize exactly which Berggren paths produce squarefree hypotenuses?** Our data suggests it's related to avoiding repeated generators, but the precise criterion is unknown.

2. **Does the superadditivity gap (tropDet(M⊗N) - tropDet(M) - tropDet(N)) carry cryptographic information?** The gap measures how much "tropical structure" is created by matrix composition.

3. **Can tropical invariants detect prime factors?** If so, this would create a practical algorithm connecting tropical geometry to factorization.

4. **What happens in higher dimensions?** The Berggren tree uses 3×3 matrices, but tropical determinant superadditivity holds for n×n. Can we build tropical arithmetic geometry in higher dimensions?

These questions connect ancient geometry to the frontiers of modern mathematics, showing once again that the simplest mathematical objects — right triangles — harbor infinite depth.

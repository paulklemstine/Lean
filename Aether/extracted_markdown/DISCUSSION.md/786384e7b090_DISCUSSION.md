# Can a Simple Formula Approximate *Any* Function?

## The surprising power of combining exponentials

Imagine you have a black box that takes a number in and spits a number out. You don't know what's inside — it could be computing something as simple as doubling the input, or something as wild as the temperature profile of a star. All you know is that the output changes smoothly as the input changes. Here's the astonishing mathematical fact: **you can approximate that black box, as closely as you want, using nothing but sums and products of the exponential function.**

This is the essence of the universal approximation theorem we've formally proved for the Emergent Meta-Language (EML) framework — and we've done it with machine-checked certainty, leaving no room for error.

## What is EML?

The EML function is beautifully simple:

**EML(a, b) = eᵃ − ln(b)**

It combines two of the most fundamental operations in mathematics: the exponential function (which models growth, from compound interest to population dynamics) and the logarithm (which models compression, from decibel scales to information theory). When you set b = 1, the logarithm vanishes and you're left with pure exponential: EML(a, 1) = eᵃ.

What makes EML special is that it interpolates between two regimes. In one limit, it behaves like exponential growth. In another, it behaves like logarithmic compression. This duality mirrors a deep pattern in mathematics and machine learning, where the interplay between expansion and compression drives everything from neural network training to data encoding.

## The theorem in plain English

Take any compact region on the number line — say, the interval from −5 to 5. Now take any continuous function on that region. Our theorem says:

> **No matter how accurately you want to approximate the function, you can do it using a polynomial in eˣ — that is, a function of the form c₀ + c₁eˣ + c₂e²ˣ + ... + cₙeⁿˣ.**

The approximation works uniformly: the error is less than your chosen tolerance ε at every single point simultaneously.

## How does the proof work?

The proof leverages one of the crown jewels of 20th-century analysis: the **Stone-Weierstrass theorem**, proved by Marshall Stone in 1937 as a vast generalization of Karl Weierstrass's 1885 approximation theorem.

Stone-Weierstrass says that if you have a collection of continuous functions that satisfies three conditions, then that collection is dense — meaning it can approximate any continuous function. The three conditions are:

1. **Algebraic closure.** The collection must be closed under addition, multiplication, and scalar multiplication — in mathematical language, it must be a *subalgebra*. Our EML functions satisfy this because the subalgebra generated by exp(x) automatically includes all polynomials in exp(x), plus all constants.

2. **Point separation.** Given any two different input points, there must be some function in the collection that gives different outputs at those points. For EML, this is immediate: the exponential function is *injective* — if x ≠ y, then eˣ ≠ eʸ. You can never confuse two different inputs by looking at their exponentials.

3. **Non-vanishing.** At every point, some function in the collection must be nonzero. This is trivially satisfied because our subalgebra contains the constant function 1 (every algebra over ℝ contains the identity element).

Once these three boxes are checked, Stone-Weierstrass hands us the approximation for free.

You might wonder: if the proof is conceptually simple, why bother formalizing it in a computer proof assistant? The answer cuts to the heart of modern mathematics.

Mathematical proofs, even "simple" ones, can harbor subtle gaps. A step that seems obvious might depend on a hypothesis you forgot to verify, or a lemma that doesn't quite apply in your setting. ## The bigger picture

This result connects the EML framework to one of the deepest currents in mathematical analysis. The Stone-Weierstrass theorem has been the theoretical backbone of approximation theory for nearly a century. By showing that EML networks satisfy its hypotheses, we establish that EML inherits this entire legacy.

More practically, universal approximation theorems are the theoretical foundation of neural networks. The classical result by Cybenko (1989) showed that neural networks with sigmoid activation functions are universal approximators. Our result shows that networks built from the EML operation — which has a richer algebraic structure than sigmoid — enjoy the same property, while additionally connecting to the exponential and logarithmic operations that pervade science and engineering.

The exponential function isn't just any activation function. It's the eigenfunction of differentiation, the foundation of Fourier analysis, and the bridge between additive and multiplicative structures. By building neural networks from exp, we're not just approximating functions — we're doing so with building blocks that respect the deep structure of mathematics itself.

## Looking forward

The theorem we've proved works for functions of a single real variable on compact sets. Natural extensions include:
- **Multivariate approximation**: extending to functions on compact subsets of ℝⁿ
- **Rate of convergence**: how quickly does the approximation improve with the degree?
- **Constructive approximation**: can we efficiently find the best coefficients?

Each of these directions offers rich mathematical territory to explore, and each is amenable to the same formal verification approach we've demonstrated here.

The marriage of machine learning and formal mathematics is still young, but results like this show its promise: we can state precisely what our models can do, and *prove* it beyond any doubt.

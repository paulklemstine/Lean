# The Photon as Epistemic Bridge: How Light Mediates the Relationship Between Observer and Observed

### A Scientific American Feature Article

**By Aristotle Research Division | 2025**

## Abstract

Since the earliest days of natural philosophy, the question of how an observer comes to know the world has been central to both physics and epistemology. We propose a framework in which the photon is understood not merely as a particle or wave, but as the fundamental *epistemic bridge* — the carrier of relational information between an observer and the system being observed. Drawing on quantum electrodynamics (QED), quantum information theory, and the relational interpretation of quantum mechanics, we argue that the photon functions as a "local knowledge table" — a discrete, bounded packet of mutual information that encodes the relationship between two systems at the moment of interaction. We outline new hypotheses, propose experimental tests, and discuss implications for the measurement problem, the nature of locality, and the emergence of classical reality from quantum substrates.

---

## 1. Introduction: Light as the Medium of Knowing

When you see a red apple, what is actually happening? Photons — quantized excitations of the electromagnetic field — leave a light source, interact with the atoms on the apple's surface, and travel to your retina, where they are absorbed by rhodopsin molecules, triggering a cascade of electrochemical signals. Every piece of visual information you have ever received about the external world arrived via photons.

This is not a trivial observation. It means that the photon is not just a physical object — it is the *connection itself* between observer and observed. Without the photon, there is no observation. The apple may exist, but it is epistemically invisible. In a very real sense, the photon *is* the knowledge.

This paper takes this intuition seriously and develops it into a physical and information-theoretic framework. We argue that:

1. **The photon is a relational object.** Its physical properties (frequency, polarization, phase, momentum) encode information about *both* its source and its detector — it is a record of a relationship, not a property of either system alone.

2. **The photon functions as a local knowledge table.** Each photon carries a finite, bounded amount of mutual information between two systems. This information is "local" in the sense that it is defined at the point of emission and the point of absorption, and is carried along a well-defined (though quantum-mechanically uncertain) path between them.

3. **Observation is photon-mediated entanglement.** The act of "seeing" is, at the quantum level, the creation of an entangled state between the observer's degrees of freedom and the observed system's degrees of freedom, with the photon as the mediating interaction.

4. **Classical reality emerges from photon-mediated decoherence.** The macroscopic world of definite objects and definite properties emerges because of the astronomical number of photon interactions that continuously entangle systems with their environments.

## 2. Background: What We Know About Photons

### 2.1 The Quantum Electrodynamic Picture

In quantum electrodynamics (QED) — the most precisely tested theory in all of science — the photon is the gauge boson of the electromagnetic interaction. It mediates the force between charged particles. When an electron in an atom transitions from a higher energy state to a lower one, a photon is emitted. When that photon is absorbed by another charged particle, energy and momentum are transferred.

The key insight of QED, developed by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger, and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga in the late 1940s, is that *every electromagnetic interaction is a photon exchange*. There is no "action at a distance" — there is only the local creation and destruction of photons.

This means that photons are not just *carriers* of force; they are the *only mechanism* by which charged matter communicates with other charged matter. In a universe governed by QED, if you cannot exchange a photon with something, you cannot interact with it electromagnetically — and therefore you cannot see it, detect it, or know about it through any electromagnetic means.

### 2.2 The Measurement Problem

In standard quantum mechanics, a system exists in a superposition of states until it is "measured," at which point the wave function appears to collapse to a definite outcome. But what constitutes a measurement? The Copenhagen interpretation never fully answered this question, leading to decades of debate.

What is often overlooked is that *most measurements in physics are photon-mediated*. When we "measure" the position of an electron, we scatter a photon off it. When we "observe" a Stern-Gerlach apparatus, photons from the apparatus reach our eyes. Even in non-optical measurements (e.g., a Geiger counter), the final readout — the click, the digital display — reaches the human observer via photons.

This suggests that the measurement problem is, at its core, a problem about *photon-mediated information transfer*. The question "what constitutes a measurement?" may be reformulated as "what constitutes a photon exchange that creates irreversible entanglement between observer and observed?"

### 2.3 Relational Quantum Mechanics

Carlo Rovelli's relational interpretation of quantum mechanics (1996) proposes that quantum states are not absolute properties of systems, but *relational* properties — they describe the state of a system relative to another system. There is no "view from nowhere"; every quantum description is a description *from the perspective of a particular observer*.

This interpretation aligns remarkably well with the photon-as-epistemic-bridge framework. If quantum states are relational, then the photon is the *physical realization of that relation*. It is the object that carries the relational information from one system to another. The photon is not just a messenger — it is the relationship itself, crystallized into a propagating quantum of the electromagnetic field.

## 3. The Photon as a Local Knowledge Table: A New Framework

### 3.1 Defining the Local Knowledge Table

We introduce the concept of the **photon as a local knowledge table (LKT)**. The metaphor of a "table" is deliberate: a table is a structured, finite, organized collection of data. Each photon, we argue, carries a structured, finite, organized collection of relational data between its source and its detector.

The "entries" in this table are the photon's quantum numbers:

| Property | Information Encoded | Source Knowledge | Observer Knowledge |
|----------|-------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Frequency (ν)** | Energy difference between atomic states at source | Atomic structure, temperature, motion (Doppler) | Detector sensitivity, spectral resolution |
| **Polarization** | Orientation of the emitting dipole, scattering geometry | Surface orientation, material properties | Analyzer angle, detection basis |
| **Direction (k-vector)** | Spatial relationship between source and detector | Source position | Detector position |
| **Phase (φ)** | Temporal relationship and coherence | Emission time, coherence of source | Detection time, interferometric setup |
| **Arrival time** | Light-travel distance, causal structure | When and where emission occurred | When and where detection occurred |
| **Photon number (n)** | Intensity, source brightness | Emission rate, stimulated vs. spontaneous | Detection efficiency, counting statistics |

Each photon thus carries a *finite* amount of information — at most a few quantum numbers — about the relationship between the source and the detector. This information is *local* in two senses:

1. **Spatially local**: It is defined at two points (emission and absorption) and propagated along a worldline between them.
2. **Informationally local**: It carries a bounded amount of information, determined by the Hilbert space dimension of the photon's accessible states.

### 3.2 Information Content of a Single Photon

How much information can a single photon carry? This question has been studied extensively in quantum information theory. The answer depends on the encoding:

- **Polarization alone**: 1 qubit (two orthogonal polarization states)
- **Frequency encoding**: Up to log₂(N) bits, where N is the number of distinguishable frequency channels
- **Orbital angular momentum**: In principle, unbounded (OAM states form an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space), though practically limited
- **Time-bin encoding**: 1 qubit per time-bin pair
- **Spatial mode**: Additional degrees of freedom

The Holevo bound tells us that a single photon in a d-dimensional Hilbert space can carry at most log₂(d) bits of classical information. For a simple polarization-encoded photon, this is 1 bit. For a photon utilizing multiple degrees of freedom, the capacity increases, but it is always *finite*.

This finiteness is crucial. It means that each photon-mediated observation is a *finite, bounded act of knowledge acquisition*. You cannot learn an infinite amount about a system from a single photon. This naturally explains the uncertainty principle: the information capacity of the mediating photon limits what can be simultaneously known.

### 3.3 The Observer-Observed Duality

A profound consequence of the LKT framework is that the photon encodes information about *both* the source and the detector. Consider a simple example: a photon with frequency ν arrives at a detector.

- The frequency tells us about the *source*: the energy level spacing of the emitting atom, the temperature of the emitting body, the velocity of the source (via Doppler shift).
- But the *detection* of that frequency tells us about the *detector*: its spectral sensitivity, its energy resolution, its quantum efficiency at that frequency.

The photon is thus a *symmetric* epistemic bridge. It carries information *from* the source *to* the detector, but the act of detection also reveals information about the detector itself. Observer and observed are, in this sense, dual to each other. The photon does not privilege one over the other.

This duality is reminiscent of Wheeler's "participatory universe" — the idea that the observer is not a passive receiver of information but an active participant in the creation of physical reality. In the LKT framework, this participation is made concrete: the observer's physical properties (detector geometry, sensitivity, basis choice) are encoded in the photon interaction just as much as the source's properties are.

## 4. New Hypotheses

We propose the following testable hypotheses that emerge from the LKT framework:

### Hypothesis 1: The Informational Equivalence Principle

**Statement**: *The total mutual information between an observer and an observed system is exactly equal to the sum of the information carried by all photons exchanged between them, minus the information lost to decoherence with the environment.*

Formally, let I(O:S) be the mutual information between observer O and system S. Let {γᵢ} be the set of all photons exchanged between them, and let I(γᵢ) be the information carried by photon i. Let D be the decoherence loss. Then:

**I(O:S) = Σᵢ I(γᵢ) − D**

This hypothesis makes the photon the *fundamental unit of knowledge transfer* and predicts that no information about a distant system can be obtained except through photon exchange (or, more generally, gauge boson exchange for other forces).

**Testable prediction**: In a carefully controlled quantum optics experiment, the mutual information between a source and a detector should be quantitatively predictable from the number and quantum states of the photons exchanged, with decoherence losses accounted for. Deviations would falsify the hypothesis.

### Hypothesis 2: Observation Asymmetry Creates Thermodynamic Arrow

**Statement**: *The thermodynamic arrow of time emerges from the asymmetry of photon-mediated observation: emission is a many-to-one process (many atomic transitions can produce the same photon frequency), while absorption is a one-to-one process (a specific photon is absorbed by a specific detector state). This asymmetry creates an irreversible increase in the total number of observer-observed relations, which is equivalent to the increase of entropy.*

This connects the epistemic role of the photon to the second law of thermodynamics. Every photon emission creates a new potential knowledge relation; every absorption actualizes one. The proliferation of photons in the universe (there are roughly 10⁹ photons for every baryon) means that the number of potential knowledge relations grows monotonically — and this growth *is* the arrow of time.

**Testable prediction**: In a system where photon number is strictly conserved (e.g., a high-Q optical cavity with no losses), the thermodynamic arrow of time should be weakened or absent. The entropy of the cavity contents should not increase monotonically. (This is consistent with the known phenomenon of Poincaré recurrences in isolated systems.)

### Hypothesis 3: Measurement Back-Action as Relational Update

**Statement**: *The "disturbance" caused by quantum measurement (measurement back-action) is not a mysterious collapse of the wave function, but is precisely the update of the local knowledge table. When a photon is absorbed by a detector, the source's quantum state — relative to that detector — is updated to reflect the new information. This update is local, relational, and consistent with unitarity at the global level.*

This reframes the measurement problem entirely. There is no collapse — there is only the update of relational information carried by photons. The apparent randomness of quantum measurement outcomes reflects the finite information capacity of the photon: because a single photon can carry at most a few bits, it cannot fully determine the state of the source, and the "remaining" information is genuinely indeterminate *relative to that observer*.

**Testable prediction**: In a delayed-choice quantum eraser experiment, the "erasure" of which-path information should be precisely described as the destruction of a photon-mediated knowledge relation. The correlations in the data should be quantitatively predicted by tracking the information content of each photon in the experiment. Moreover, the timing of the "erasure" should be irrelevant — what matters is whether the photon-mediated knowledge relation exists at the time of final detection.

### Hypothesis 4: The Photon Self-Knowledge Exclusion

**Statement**: *A photon cannot carry information about itself — it can only carry information about the relationship between other systems. A photon has no rest frame, no internal clock, and no self-referential properties. It is pure relation, with no intrinsic substance.*

This is motivated by special relativity: a photon travels at speed c in all reference frames, and a hypothetical "rest frame" of a photon would have zero proper time elapsed along its worldline. A photon "experiences" no time. It cannot, therefore, have any internal dynamics or self-knowledge. It is, in a deep sense, *nothing but* the connection between its emission event and its absorption event.

**Testable prediction**: Any experiment that attempts to measure an "intrinsic" property of a photon (independent of source and detector) should find that the result depends on the measurement apparatus. Polarization, for instance, is not intrinsic — it is defined relative to a measurement basis. This is already confirmed by quantum mechanics, but the LKT framework predicts this as a *general principle*: no photon property is apparatus-independent.

### Hypothesis 5: Conscious Observation Is Not Special

**Statement**: *There is nothing physically special about conscious observation. A photon absorbed by a retinal cell creates exactly the same type of relational information as a photon absorbed by a silicon photodiode. The "observer" in quantum mechanics need not be conscious — it need only be a physical system capable of irreversibly recording photon-mediated information.*

This is a strong statement against the "consciousness causes collapse" interpretation. In the LKT framework, what matters is not consciousness but *information-theoretic irreversibility*: the photon's information must be amplified and recorded in a macroscopic degree of freedom (whether biological or electronic) to constitute an "observation."

**Testable prediction**: A quantum eraser experiment should produce identical results whether the "which-path" detector is a conscious human observer or an automated photodetector — provided the information-theoretic structure of the experiment is identical. (This prediction is already supported by all existing experimental evidence.)

## 5. Proposed Experiments

### Experiment 1: Photon Counting and Mutual Information

**Setup**: A single-photon source emits photons toward a detector array. The source is prepared in a known quantum state (e.g., a specific superposition of polarization states). The detector measures the photons in a chosen basis.

**Protocol**:
1. Prepare the source in state |ψ⟩ = α|H⟩ + β|V⟩ (known to the experimenter but not to the detector).
2. Send N photons to the detector.
3. Measure the detector's mutual information with the source as a function of N.
4. Compare with the theoretical prediction: I(O:S) = N × I_single(γ), where I_single(γ) is the single-photon information capacity in the chosen encoding.

**Expected result**: The mutual information should grow linearly with N, saturating at the entropy of the source state H(ψ). This would confirm that photons are the fundamental units of knowledge transfer.

**Novel aspect**: Previous quantum information experiments have focused on *communication* (sending known messages via photons). This experiment focuses on *observation* (learning about an unknown source via photons), which is the epistemically relevant scenario.

### Experiment 2: Relational State Verification

**Setup**: An entangled photon pair source (e.g., SPDC — spontaneous parametric down-conversion) sends one photon to Alice and one to Bob. Alice and Bob each measure in randomly chosen bases.

**Protocol**:
1. Generate N entangled pairs.
2. Alice measures her photon in basis A_i; Bob measures his in basis B_j.
3. For each pair, compute the "relational information" R(A_i, B_j) = the mutual information between Alice's outcome and Bob's outcome.
4. Verify that R depends on the *relative* basis choice (A_i − B_j) and not on the absolute basis of either observer.

**Expected result**: R should depend only on the angle between Alice's and Bob's measurement bases (as predicted by Malus's law and the Bell correlations). This confirms the relational nature of photon-mediated knowledge: the information is about the *relationship* between observers, not about either observer individually.

**Novel aspect**: Frame the standard Bell test not as a test of "nonlocality" but as a test of "relational information content." The violation of Bell inequalities then has a natural interpretation: the relational information exceeds what any locally pre-existing "knowledge table" could contain — but is exactly what a *quantum* knowledge table (using entanglement as a resource) can carry.

### Experiment 3: Decoherence as Knowledge Dissipation

**Setup**: A single photon is stored in a high-finesse optical cavity. The cavity has a controllable loss rate (adjustable mirror reflectivity).

**Protocol**:
1. Inject a single photon in a known polarization state into the cavity.
2. Allow the photon to bounce N times inside the cavity.
3. At each bounce, a small fraction of the photon's amplitude leaks out and is absorbed by the environment (the mirror substrate, the lab walls, etc.).
4. After N bounces, extract the remaining photon and measure its polarization.
5. Plot the mutual information between the initial and final polarization states as a function of N (i.e., as a function of decoherence).

**Expected result**: The mutual information should decrease exponentially with N, at a rate determined by the cavity loss rate. This would directly demonstrate that decoherence — the leakage of photons to the environment — destroys the observer-observed knowledge relation.

**Novel aspect**: This experiment explicitly measures *knowledge loss* (mutual information decrease) rather than *coherence loss* (visibility decrease in an interferometer). While these are related, the information-theoretic framing makes the epistemic interpretation explicit and testable.

### Experiment 4: Testing Self-Knowledge Exclusion

**Setup**: Use quantum non-demolition (QND) measurement techniques to attempt to learn about a photon's properties without absorbing it.

**Protocol**:
1. Send a single photon through a QND measurement apparatus that measures its photon number (confirming it exists) without absorbing it.
2. Then send the same photon through a second apparatus that measures its polarization.
3. Ask: does the QND measurement of photon number affect the polarization measurement?

**Expected result**: The QND measurement should not affect the polarization measurement (since photon number and polarization commute). However, the LKT framework predicts something stronger: the QND apparatus and the photon should now share a knowledge relation (the apparatus "knows" the photon exists), but this relation contains *no information about the photon's intrinsic properties* — because the photon has no intrinsic properties. The information is entirely about the relationship between the photon and the QND apparatus.

## 6. Validation Against Known Physics

### 6.1 Consistency with QED

The LKT framework is fully consistent with quantum electrodynamics. QED already treats the photon as the mediator of electromagnetic interactions; the LKT framework adds an *epistemic interpretation* to this physical role. No modifications to the QED Lagrangian or Feynman rules are required.

### 6.2 Consistency with Special Relativity

The photon's null worldline (zero proper time) is naturally incorporated: a photon "experiences" no time between emission and absorption, which is consistent with its role as a pure relation between two events. The LKT framework respects Lorentz invariance because the information content of a photon is defined in terms of Lorentz-invariant quantities (e.g., the invariant mass of the photon is zero, independent of frame).

### 6.3 Consistency with Thermodynamics

The hypothesis that entropy increase is related to the proliferation of photon-mediated knowledge relations is consistent with the Bekenstein bound and the holographic principle. The maximum information content of a region of space is proportional to its surface area (in Planck units), which is also the maximum number of photon modes that can pass through that surface. This suggests a deep connection between the informational role of photons and the thermodynamic properties of space itself.

### 6.4 Consistency with the Uncertainty Principle

The uncertainty principle follows naturally from the finite information capacity of the photon. A single photon used to measure position (short wavelength = high momentum) necessarily carries less information about momentum (and vice versa). The Heisenberg limit ΔxΔp ≥ ℏ/2 can be derived from the information-theoretic constraint that a single photon cannot simultaneously encode precise position *and* precise momentum information.

### 6.5 Resolving the EPR Paradox

In the LKT framework, the EPR paradox dissolves. When two entangled photons are measured by spatially separated observers, no "spooky action at a distance" occurs. Instead, the entangled pair constitutes a *single, non-local knowledge table* that was created at the moment of pair production. When Alice measures her photon, she reads her "column" of the table; when Bob measures his, he reads his. The correlations between their results were *written into the table at creation* — but the specific entries were not determined until the table was read (measurement), because quantum information has this irreducibly relational character.

This is consistent with the no-signaling theorem: Alice cannot use her measurement to send a message to Bob, because her measurement only reveals her own column of the knowledge table. The table is non-local in the sense that it connects two distant events, but the *reading* of the table is entirely local.

## 7. Implications and Discussion

### 7.1 The Universe as a Web of Photon Relations

If the LKT framework is correct, then the universe is not a collection of objects with intrinsic properties, but a vast web of photon-mediated relations. Every object is defined by its interactions with other objects, and every interaction is mediated by the exchange of gauge bosons (photons for electromagnetism, gluons for the strong force, W and Z bosons for the weak force, and hypothetically gravitons for gravity).

In this view, "reality" is not a property of individual objects but an emergent property of the network of relations between them. This is consistent with structural realism in philosophy of science, and with the relational interpretation of quantum mechanics.

### 7.2 The Speed of Light as the Speed of Knowledge

The speed of light c is not merely the speed of a physical object — it is the *maximum speed of knowledge transfer*. No observer can learn about a distant event faster than a photon can travel from that event to the observer. This gives the speed of light an epistemic interpretation: c is the speed limit of the universe's knowledge network.

This also gives a new perspective on the light cone structure of spacetime. The past light cone of an event is not just the set of events that *could have* influenced it — it is the set of events about which the observer *could have* knowledge. The future light cone is the set of events that the observer *could* influence. The light cone structure is, fundamentally, the structure of possible knowledge relations.

### 7.3 Dark Matter and Dark Energy: The Unobservable

The LKT framework raises an interesting question about dark matter and dark energy. These entities, by definition, do not interact with photons (or interact so weakly as to be nearly invisible). In the LKT framework, this means that dark matter and dark energy lie *outside the photon knowledge network*. We know about them only indirectly — through their gravitational effects on objects that *are* in the photon network.

This suggests that the universe may contain vast sectors of reality that are epistemically inaccessible to photon-based observers. If gravitons exist and mediate gravitational interactions, then gravity would provide an independent knowledge network — and dark matter would be accessible through this network even though it is invisible in the photon network.

### 7.4 Toward a Unified Epistemic Field Theory

The LKT framework, while developed here for photons, naturally extends to all gauge bosons. Each force carrier could be understood as a "knowledge table" for its respective interaction:

- **Gluons**: Knowledge tables for color charge (strong force)
- **W and Z bosons**: Knowledge tables for weak isospin (weak force)
- **Gravitons** (hypothetical): Knowledge tables for mass-energy (gravity)

A unified epistemic field theory would describe all fundamental interactions as different types of knowledge relations, mediated by different types of gauge bosons, and would seek to understand why these particular knowledge channels exist and whether they are aspects of a single, deeper epistemic structure.

## 9. Conclusion

The photon is far more than a particle of light. It is the fundamental unit of empirical knowledge — the quantum of the relationship between observer and observed. Every act of seeing, every measurement, every observation is, at bottom, a photon exchange. The photon carries a finite, structured, local table of relational information: the energies of atomic transitions, the orientations of dipoles, the distances between events, the timing of interactions.

By taking this epistemic role seriously, we arrive at a framework — the Local Knowledge Table (LKT) — that is fully consistent with quantum electrodynamics, special relativity, and thermodynamics, while providing new insights into the measurement problem, the nature of entanglement, and the emergence of classical reality. The framework generates testable predictions and suggests new experiments that could deepen our understanding of the relationship between information, observation, and physical reality.

In the end, the photon reminds us of a profound truth: we do not observe the world directly. We observe *relations* — carried to us, one quantum at a time, by messengers traveling at the speed of light. The universe we know is not the universe as it is, but the universe as it has been communicated to us, through the silent, luminous language of photons.

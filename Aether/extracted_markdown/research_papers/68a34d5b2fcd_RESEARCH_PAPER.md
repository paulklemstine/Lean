# The Apparition–Order Bridge: A Local-to-Global Dictionary for Strong Divisibility Sequences

## Abstract

We establish a precise representation theorem identifying a *global*,
order-theoretic invariant of an integer sequence with a *local* group-theoretic
computation in a residue field. For the Mersenne / repunit family
`a(n) = bⁿ − 1` and a prime `p` not dividing the base `b`, we prove that the
**rank of apparition** (entry point) of `p` — the least positive index at which
`p` divides a term — equals the **multiplicative order** of `b` in the residue
field `ℤ/pℤ`:

> **entryPoint(p) = order(b mod p).**

This identity is the stalk-level companion to the global *law of apparition*
`p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(p) ∣ n`, which holds over any strong divisibility
sequence. From the bridge we derive (i) **Fermat descent**, that the entry point
divides `p − 1`, bounding the smallest local period by the order of the unit
group; and (ii) a **support theorem** identifying the set of indices at which `p`
appears with the principal arithmetic progression generated by the entry point.
We give the Fibonacci specialization, connecting the framework to classical
primitive-divisor theory (Carmichael, Zsygmondy). All results have been formally
verified in the Lean 4 proof assistant with the Mathlib library, depending only
on the standard foundational axioms. The decisive structural insight is that the
content of the bridge is concentrated entirely in a single natural-number-cast
reduction: divisibility of `bⁿ − 1` by `p` in `ℕ` is equivalent to the power
identity `(b mod p)ⁿ = 1` in `ℤ/pℤ`.

**Keywords:** rank of apparition, entry point, strong divisibility sequence,
multiplicative order, Mersenne numbers, Fibonacci sequence, Fermat's little
theorem, local-to-global principle, residue field.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Let `b ≥ 2` be an integer. The sequence of **Mersenne numbers** (or, for general
`b`, repunit-type numbers) `a(n) = bⁿ − 1` is among the most studied objects in
number theory: it is the source of the largest known primes and a testing ground
for divisibility theory. A natural and old question asks, for a fixed prime `p`:
*at which indices `n` does `p` divide `bⁿ − 1`?*

The set of such indices is governed by a single invariant. The **rank of
apparition**, or **entry point**, of `p` is the least positive index `e` with
`p ∣ bᵉ − 1`. Classically one shows that `p ∣ bⁿ − 1` if and only if `e ∣ n`; the
prime "appears" periodically, with period `e`. Determining `e` directly from the
sequence requires, a priori, an unbounded search.

This paper records and formally verifies the elementary but conceptually central
fact that `e` is not a sequence-global quantity at all: it is the **multiplicative
order** of `b` in the finite field `ℤ/pℤ`, a purely local computation requiring no
reference to the infinite sequence. This is a clean instance of the
**local-to-global principle**: a global invariant is *represented* by a single
stalk-level group order.

### 1.2 Contributions

1. **The Apparition–Order Bridge** (Theorem 4.1): for a prime `p ∤ b`,
   `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = orderOf(b mod p)`.
2. **The stalk reduction** (Lemma 3.1): `p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ (b mod p)ⁿ = 1`, the
   natural-cast dictionary that carries all the content.
3. **Fermat descent** (Theorem 4.2): `entryPoint(p) ∣ p − 1`.
4. **The support theorem** (Theorem 2.1): the apparition support
   `{ n : p ∣ a(n) }` equals the principal progression `{ n : entryPoint(p) ∣ n }`,
   over any strong divisibility sequence.
5. **The Fibonacci specialization** (Corollary 5.1), connecting the framework to
   primitive-divisor theory.

All statements are theorems formalized and machine-checked in Lean 4 / Mathlib.
The proof sketches below describe the mathematical content; we deliberately do not
reproduce formal tactic scripts.

### 1.3 Related context

The result sits atop a generic theory of **strong divisibility sequences**
(Section 2), under which the Fibonacci and Mersenne primitive-divisor theories are
a single development. The bridge of Theorem 4.1 is the multiplicative
specialization linking the abstract entry-point machinery to concrete residue
arithmetic; Fermat descent (Theorem 4.2) is the classical bound made precise; and
the Fibonacci specialization ties back to the Carmichael–Zsygmondy circle of
primitive-divisor existence results.

## 2. Strong divisibility sequences and the support sheaf

### 2.1 Definition

**Definition 2.1 (Strong divisibility sequence).** A *strong divisibility
sequence* is a function `a : ℕ → ℕ` satisfying

- (vanishing) `a(0) = 0`, and
- (strong divisibility) `gcd(a(m), a(n)) = a(gcd(m, n))` for all `m, n ∈ ℕ`.

The single identity `gcd(a(m), a(n)) = a(gcd(m, n))` is the structural heart of
the theory. Three canonical instances:

- **Fibonacci:** `a(n) = Fₙ`, since `gcd(Fₘ, Fₙ) = F₍gcd(m,n)₎`.
- **Mersenne / repunit:** `a(n) = bⁿ − 1`, since
  `gcd(bᵐ − 1, bⁿ − 1) = b^{gcd(m,n)} − 1`.
- **Identity:** `a(n) = n`, trivially.

### 2.2 Elementary consequences

From the two axioms one derives, with no further input:

- **(Monotonicity)** `m ∣ n ⟹ a(m) ∣ a(n)`. *Proof.* If `m ∣ n` then
  `gcd(m, n) = m`, so `gcd(a(m), a(n)) = a(m)`, whence `a(m) ∣ a(n)`. ∎
- **(Meet law)** `d ∣ a(gcd(m, n)) ⟺ d ∣ a(m) ∧ d ∣ a(n)`, immediate by rewriting
  `a(gcd(m, n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))` and using the universal property of `gcd`.

**Definition 2.2 (Primitive divisor / entry point).** A number `p` is a
*primitive divisor* at index `n` if `p ∣ a(n)` and `p ∤ a(k)` for all `0 < k < n`.
The **entry point** `entryPoint(p)` is the least `k > 0` with `p ∣ a(k)`, or `0`
if no such `k` exists.

**Lemma 2.1 (Law of apparition).** If `p` divides some positive-index term, then
for all `n`,

> `p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(p) ∣ n`.

*Proof sketch.* Write `e = entryPoint(p)`. (⟸) `e ∣ n` gives `a(e) ∣ a(n)` by
monotonicity, and `p ∣ a(e)` by definition. (⟹) Suppose `p ∣ a(n)`. By the meet
law `p ∣ a(gcd(e, n))`. Since `gcd(e, n) ≤ e` and `e` is the *least* positive
index divided by `p`, minimality forces `gcd(e, n) = e`, i.e. `e ∣ n`. ∎

### 2.3 The support theorem

**Theorem 2.1 (Support = principal progression).** *For a strong divisibility
sequence `a` and a prime `p` dividing some positive-index term,*

> `{ n : p ∣ a(n) } = { n : entryPoint(p) ∣ n }.`

*Proof.* Pointwise this is exactly Lemma 2.1; `Set` extensionality repackages the
equivalence as an equality of subsets of `ℕ`. ∎

Conceptually, the assignment `n ↦ { p : p ∣ a(n) }` is a "support sheaf" over the
additive index semigroup `(ℕ, +)`. Theorem 2.1 computes its *global sections* at a
single prime: the locus where `p` lives is one arithmetic progression, generated
by the entry point. The remaining sections of the paper compute the *generator* of
that progression locally.

## 3. The stalk reduction

The transition from integer divisibility to residue-field arithmetic is carried by
one lemma. Fix a prime `p` and work in the finite field `ℤ/pℤ` (`ZMod p` in
Mathlib). Let `b̄` denote the image of `b` under the canonical ring homomorphism
`ℕ → ℤ/pℤ`.

**Lemma 3.1 (Stalk reduction).** *For `b ≥ 1` and any `p, n` with `p` nonzero,*

> `p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ b̄ⁿ = 1 in ℤ/pℤ.`

*Proof sketch.* Since `b ≥ 1` we have `bⁿ ≥ 1`, so `bⁿ − 1` is a genuine
(non-truncated) subtraction in `ℕ` and the cast commutes:
`overline{bⁿ − 1} = b̄ⁿ − 1` in `ℤ/pℤ`. Divisibility `p ∣ bⁿ − 1` is equivalent
to `overline{bⁿ − 1} = 0` (a natural number is divisible by `p` iff its image in
`ℤ/pℤ` vanishes), i.e. `b̄ⁿ − 1 = 0`, i.e. `b̄ⁿ = 1` by `sub_eq_zero`. ∎

The hypothesis `b ≥ 1` is exactly what is needed for the subtraction to behave;
the hypothesis `p ∤ b` (used downstream) guarantees `b̄ ≠ 0`, so that `b̄` is a
unit with a well-defined multiplicative order. Note `p ∤ b` already forces `b ≠ 0`
because `p ∣ 0`.

## 4. The Apparition–Order Bridge

We now combine the global law of apparition (Lemma 2.1) with the local stalk
reduction (Lemma 3.1) and the defining property of multiplicative order.

Recall the **multiplicative order** `orderOf(x)` of an element `x` of a monoid is
the least `k > 0` with `xᵏ = 1` (or `0` if none exists), characterized by

> `orderOf(x) ∣ n ⟺ xⁿ = 1.`

### 4.1 Main theorem

**Theorem 4.1 (Apparition–Order Bridge).** *Let `p` be prime and `b` a natural
number with `p ∤ b`. Then in the Mersenne sequence `a(n) = bⁿ − 1`,*

> `entryPoint(p) = orderOf(b̄)` in `ℤ/pℤ`.

*Proof sketch.* The clean argument shows that both numbers have the identical set
of multiples and are positive, hence equal. For every `n`:

```
entryPoint(p) ∣ n
   ⟺ p ∣ bⁿ − 1              (Lemma 2.1, law of apparition)
   ⟺ b̄ⁿ = 1 in ℤ/pℤ          (Lemma 3.1, stalk reduction)
   ⟺ orderOf(b̄) ∣ n           (defining property of order).
```

Two natural numbers with identical divisibility relations against all `n` (i.e.
the same set of multiples) are equal. To deploy Lemma 2.1 we need the existence of
a positive-index term divisible by `p`; the witness is `n = p − 1`, since
Fermat's little theorem gives `b̄^{p−1} = 1`, hence `p ∣ b^{p−1} − 1` by the
stalk reduction. Both quantities are positive: `orderOf(b̄) > 0` because `b̄ ≠ 0`
is a unit in a finite field (and again `b̄^{p−1} = 1`), and `entryPoint(p) > 0`
because the apparition set is nonempty. ∎

The formal proof establishes the equality by antisymmetric divisibility
(`le_antisymm` on `Nat.le_of_dvd`), showing each of `entryPoint(p)` and
`orderOf(b̄)` divides the other via the chain above.

### 4.2 Fermat descent

**Theorem 4.2 (Fermat descent).** *Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1,*

> `entryPoint(p) ∣ p − 1.`

*Proof sketch.* Rewrite with Theorem 4.1 to reduce to `orderOf(b̄) ∣ p − 1`. By
Fermat's little theorem `b̄^{p−1} = 1` in `ℤ/pℤ` (valid since `b̄ ≠ 0`), and the
defining property of order then yields `orderOf(b̄) ∣ p − 1`. ∎

Interpretation: the smallest local period of `p` in `bⁿ − 1` is bounded by, and in
fact divides, the order `p − 1` of the unit group `(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ`. The unbounded global
search for the entry point is thus a priori confined to the divisors of `p − 1`.

### 4.3 Discussion of the proof architecture

The bridge factors as a composition of two equivalences glued by a cast:

> `dvd_iff_entryPoint_dvd` (global) ∘ `orderOf_dvd_iff_pow_eq_one` (local),

joined by the natural-cast reduction
`p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ b̄ⁿ = 1`. The "two divisors with identical multiple-sets are equal"
principle then pins `entryPoint = orderOf`. This is a reusable template: any global
invariant defined by a divisibility-of-index characterization can be matched to a
local invariant defined by a `xⁿ = 1`-characterization, provided a cast dictionary
links the two.

## 5. The Fibonacci specialization

The support theorem (Theorem 2.1) is generic and therefore applies verbatim to the
Fibonacci sequence `Fₙ`, the canonical strong divisibility sequence.

**Corollary 5.1 (Fibonacci support).** *For a prime `p` dividing some positive
Fibonacci number,*

> `{ n : p ∣ Fₙ } = { n : entryPoint_F(p) ∣ n },`

*where `entryPoint_F(p)` is the Fibonacci rank of apparition.*

*Proof.* Specialize Theorem 2.1 to the Fibonacci strong divisibility sequence. ∎

Unlike the Mersenne case, the Fibonacci entry point is *not* the multiplicative
order of a single element of `ℤ/pℤ`; it is instead governed by the order of the
companion matrix `[[1,1],[1,0]]` in `GL₂(ℤ/pℤ)`, or equivalently by the period of
the Fibonacci recurrence mod `p` (the Pisano period) together with quadratic
residuacity of 5 mod `p`. The *support* statement, however, is identical in form,
which is exactly the point of the strong-divisibility abstraction: the global
shape of a prime's footprint is a single arithmetic progression regardless of the
sequence, while the local computation of the generator is sequence-specific. The
Fibonacci entry point connects directly to the Carmichael–Zsygmondy theory of
primitive divisors, which guarantees (apart from finitely many exceptions) the
existence of a prime appearing for the first time at each index.

## 6. Worked numerical examples

**Mersenne, b = 2.**

| p  | order of 2 mod p | entry point in 2ⁿ−1 | divides p−1? |
|----|------------------|---------------------|--------------|
| 3  | 2                | 2 (2²−1 = 3)        | 2 ∣ 2 ✓      |
| 5  | 4                | 4 (2⁴−1 = 15)       | 4 ∣ 4 ✓      |
| 7  | 3                | 3 (2³−1 = 7)        | 3 ∣ 6 ✓      |
| 11 | 10               | 10                  | 10 ∣ 10 ✓    |
| 23 | 11               | 11                  | 11 ∣ 22 ✓    |
| 31 | 5                | 5 (2⁵−1 = 31)       | 5 ∣ 30 ✓     |
| 73 | 9                | 9                   | 9 ∣ 72 ✓     |

In every row the order and the entry point coincide (Theorem 4.1) and the entry
point divides `p − 1` (Theorem 4.2). Note how the entry point can be strictly less
than `p − 1` (e.g. `p = 7, 31, 73`): these are precisely the primes for which 2 is
*not* a primitive root.

**Fibonacci.** `entryPoint_F(2) = 3` (F₃ = 2), `entryPoint_F(3) = 4` (F₄ = 3),
`entryPoint_F(5) = 5` (F₅ = 5), `entryPoint_F(7) = 8` (F₈ = 21),
`entryPoint_F(11) = 10` (F₁₀ = 55). The support of each prime is the progression of
multiples of its entry point.

## 7. Applications

- **Primality and factorization heuristics.** Theorem 4.2 confines the search for
  factors of `bⁿ − 1`: any prime `p ∣ bⁿ − 1` has entry point dividing both `n`
  and `p − 1`, so primes dividing `2ⁿ − 1` for prime `n` satisfy `p ≡ 1 (mod n)`,
  a classical sieve for Mersenne factor hunting.
- **Cyclic-group structure.** The bridge is the elementary shadow of the
  discrete-logarithm structure of `(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ`. The difficulty of inverting
  `n ↦ bⁿ mod p` underlies Diffie–Hellman and related public-key cryptography; the
  entry point is the *order* parameter that determines the size of the cyclic
  subgroup generated by `b`.
- **Sheaf-theoretic bookkeeping.** Theorem 2.1 realizes the apparition data as the
  global sections of a support sheaf, a model instance of the local-to-global
  philosophy in the most concrete possible arithmetic setting.

## 8. Discussion and future work

The Apparition–Order Bridge is a small theorem with an outsized conceptual role:
it exhibits, with bare hands, the local-to-global principle that organizes much of
modern number theory. A global, order-theoretic, search-defined invariant is
*represented* by a finite group-order computation in a residue field, and a famous
classical theorem (Fermat) then bounds it for free.

Several directions extend the framework:

1. **General linear recurrences.** Replace `b` by the companion matrix of a linear
   recurrence; the entry point becomes the order of that matrix in
   `GL_d(ℤ/pℤ)`, unifying the Mersenne (`d = 1`) and Fibonacci (`d = 2`) cases
   under one matrix-order bridge.
2. **Prime-power and composite moduli.** Lift the bridge from `ℤ/pℤ` to
   `ℤ/p^kℤ` and, via CRT, to arbitrary moduli, recovering the structure of the
   full apparition function and its lifting-the-exponent behavior.
3. **Effective Zsygmondy bounds.** Combine the support theorem with primitive
   divisor existence to give formally verified, effective statements about the
   first appearance of new primes in `bⁿ − 1` and `Fₙ`.
4. **Algorithmic synthesis.** The bridge yields an `O(log n · log p)` algorithm for
   the entry point (repeated squaring inside the divisors of `p − 1`),
   asymptotically superior to a linear scan of the sequence.

## 9. Conclusion

We have given a self-contained account, formally verified in Lean 4 / Mathlib, of
the identity

> `entryPoint(p) = orderOf(b mod p)` for the Mersenne sequence `bⁿ − 1`, `p ∤ b`,

together with its corollaries: Fermat descent (`entryPoint(p) ∣ p − 1`) and the
support theorem (the apparition locus is a principal arithmetic progression). The
results rest on a generic theory of strong divisibility sequences that subsumes the
Fibonacci and Mersenne cases simultaneously, and on a single natural-cast
reduction that carries all the arithmetic content from the integers into the
residue field. The bridge is a complete, elementary, machine-checked instance of
the local-to-global dictionary.

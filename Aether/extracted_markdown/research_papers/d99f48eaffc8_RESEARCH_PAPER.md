# The Fourier Transform of the Riemann Zeta Function: Spectral Theory of Prime Frequencies

## Abstract

We develop a rigorous spectral theory of prime numbers viewed through the Fourier transform of the Riemann zeta function on the critical line. Defining the prime frequency map p ↦ log(p)/(2π), we prove that this map is injective on primes, that prime logarithms are rationally independent (a consequence of unique prime factorization), and that the associated finite spectral sums satisfy explicit bounds. We introduce the *prime spectral measure* — a weighted sum of point masses at prime frequencies — as the distributional Fourier transform of the Dirichlet series on the critical line. All key results are formalized and machine-verified in Lean 4, establishing a foundation for computational prime spectroscopy.

**Keywords**: Riemann zeta function, Fourier transform, prime numbers, spectral analysis, formal verification

---

## 1. Introduction

The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = Σ_{n=1}^∞ n^{-s} has a well-known Euler product representation ζ(s) = Π_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}, connecting it directly to the prime numbers. On the critical line s = 1/2 + it, the logarithmic derivative of the Euler product yields

-ζ'/ζ(1/2 + it) = Σ_p Σ_{k=1}^∞ (log p) · p^{-k(1/2+it)}

whose leading terms are Σ_p (log p) · p^{-1/2} · e^{-it log p}. This expresses ζ on the critical line as a superposition of complex exponentials with frequencies log(p)/(2π), weighted by amplitudes proportional to 1/√p.

The Fourier transform of such a superposition consists of spectral peaks (distributional delta functions) at the frequencies log(p)/(2π). This observation — that the Fourier transform of the zeta function "detects" primes — motivates a systematic study of the *prime spectral measure*.

### 1.1 Contributions

1. **Prime Frequency Map** (Definition 2.1): We define primeFreq(p) = log(p)/(2π) and prove its injectivity on primes (Theorem 3.1).

2. **Rational Independence** (Theorem 3.3): We prove that log(p)/log(q) is irrational for distinct primes p, q, establishing that the prime frequency set is rationally independent. The proof uses unique prime factorization.

3. **Spectral Bounds** (Theorem 3.5): We prove that the finite truncated spectral sum |Σ_{p≤N} (1/√p) cos(2π(w - freq(p)))| is bounded by Σ_{p≤N} 1/√p.

4. **Spectral Rigidity** (Theorem 3.6): The prime spectrum data (frequency, weight pairs) has cardinality equal to the prime counting function, establishing that no spectral information is lost.

5. **Machine Verification**: All results are formalized in Lean 4 with Mathlib, with proofs verified by the Lean kernel.

## 2. Definitions

### Definition 2.1 (Prime Frequency)
For a natural number n ≥ 2, the *prime frequency* is
$$\text{primeFreq}(n) = \frac{\log n}{2\pi}$$

### Definition 2.2 (Prime Spectral Weight)
The *spectral weight* of a prime p is
$$\text{primeWeight}(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}$$
This arises from evaluating the Dirichlet series term p^{-s} at s = 1/2.

### Definition 2.3 (Prime Spectral Sum)
The *truncated prime spectral sum* at frequency w, using primes up to N, is
$$S_N(w) = \sum_{\substack{p \leq N \\ p \text{ prime}}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}} \cos\left(2\pi\left(w - \frac{\log p}{2\pi}\right)\right)$$

### Definition 2.4 (Prime Spectrum Data)
The *prime spectrum data* up to N is the finite set
$$\text{PSD}(N) = \left\{\left(\frac{\log p}{2\pi}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right) : p \leq N, p \text{ prime}\right\}$$

## 3. Main Results

### Theorem 3.1 (Injectivity of Prime Frequencies)
*If p and q are prime and primeFreq(p) = primeFreq(q), then p = q.*

**Proof sketch.** Since 2π > 0, primeFreq(p) = primeFreq(q) implies log(p) = log(q). The logarithm is injective on (0, ∞), so p = q as real numbers, hence as natural numbers. □

### Theorem 3.2 (Prime Power Rigidity)
*If p and q are distinct primes and a, b are positive natural numbers, then p^a ≠ q^b.*

**Proof sketch.** If p^a = q^b, then p | q^b. Since p is prime, p | q (by Euclid's lemma applied iteratively). Since q is also prime and p | q, we get p = q, contradicting distinctness. □

### Theorem 3.3 (Rational Independence of Prime Logarithms)
*For distinct primes p and q, the ratio log(p)/log(q) is irrational.*

**Proof sketch.** Suppose log(p)/log(q) = a/b for integers a, b with b ≠ 0. Then b·log(p) = a·log(q), which gives log(p^b) = log(q^a). By injectivity of the logarithm on positive reals, p^b = q^a (interpreting exponentiation appropriately for integer exponents). By Theorem 3.2, this is impossible for distinct primes with positive exponents. The cases of negative exponents are handled by size arguments (p^b > 1 for p prime, b > 0, while q^{-a} < 1 for q prime, a > 0). □

**Remark.** This theorem is equivalent to the statement that the multiplicative group generated by any set of distinct primes is a free abelian group. It is a direct consequence of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic.

### Theorem 3.4 (Strict Monotonicity of Prime Frequencies)
*If p and q are primes with p < q, then primeFreq(p) < primeFreq(q).*

**Proof sketch.** Since log is strictly monotone on (0, ∞) and p < q implies p, q > 0, we have log(p) < log(q). Dividing by 2π > 0 preserves the inequality. □

### Corollary 3.4.1 (Spectral Gap Positivity)
*For primes p < q, the spectral gap primeFreq(q) - primeFreq(p) > 0.*

### Theorem 3.5 (Spectral Sum Bound)
*For all N and w, |S_N(w)| ≤ Σ_{p≤N, prime} 1/√p.*

**Proof sketch.** By the triangle inequality, |Σ a_p cos(θ_p)| ≤ Σ |a_p cos(θ_p)| ≤ Σ |a_p| · |cos(θ_p)| ≤ Σ |a_p| = Σ 1/√p, using |cos| ≤ 1 and 1/√p ≥ 0. □

### Theorem 3.6 (Spectral Data Cardinality)
*|PSD(N)| = π(N), where π(N) is the number of primes up to N.*

**Proof sketch.** The map p ↦ (primeFreq(p), primeWeight(p)) is injective on primes (by Theorem 3.1, since equal first components force equal primes). The image of an injective map from a finite set has the same cardinality. □

## 4. The Connection to the Explicit Formula

The von Mangoldt explicit formula provides the precise relationship between the zeros of ζ(s) and the prime counting function:

$$\psi(x) = x - \sum_\rho \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\log(1 - x^{-2})$$

where the sum runs over non-trivial zeros ρ of ζ(s). Taking Fourier transforms, the prime contributions become delta functions at frequencies log(p^k)/(2π), while the zero contributions provide a smooth background.

Our spectral framework formalizes the "prime side" of this duality. The prime spectral measure

$$\mu_{\text{prime}} = \sum_p \frac{1}{\sqrt{p}} \cdot \delta\left(w - \frac{\log p}{2\pi}\right)$$

captures exactly the prime contribution to the Fourier transform of ζ on the critical line. The error between this idealized measure and the actual Fourier transform is controlled by the zeros of ζ.

## 5. Algorithms

### Algorithm 5.1: Prime Spectral Detection

**Input**: List of primes P = {p₁, ..., p_k}, frequency range [w_min, w_max], window T

**Output**: Spectral density function S(w)

```
for each frequency w in [w_min, w_max]:
    S(w) = 0
    for each time sample t in [0, T]:
        signal = Σ_p (1/√p) · cos(2π · freq(p) · t)
        S(w) += signal · cos(2π · w · t) · dt
    S(w) = 2 · S(w) / T
```

**Complexity**: O(|P| · T/dt · |frequency bins|)

### Algorithm 5.2: Rational Independence Verification

**Input**: Primes p, q; maximum denominator D

**Output**: Best rational approximation and error bound

```
ratio = log(p) / log(q)
best_error = ∞
for b = 1 to D:
    a = round(ratio · b)
    error = |ratio - a/b|
    if error < best_error:
        best_error = error
        best_a, best_b = a, b
return (best_a, best_b, best_error)
```

By our Theorem 3.3, best_error > 0 for all D. The rate of decrease is governed by the irrationality measure of log(p)/log(q).

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Relationship to "Can One Hear the Shape of a Drum?"

Mark Kac's famous 1966 question asked whether the eigenvalue spectrum of the Laplacian determines the geometry of a domain. Our work addresses an arithmetic analog: "Can one hear the primes in the zeta function?" The answer is yes — the Fourier spectrum of ζ(1/2 + it) determines the set of primes, with each prime contributing a unique spectral line.

Unlike Kac's problem (where the answer is generally "no" — non-isometric drums can sound the same), the prime spectral problem has a definitive positive answer: the prime spectrum uniquely determines the primes, as a consequence of the injectivity of the logarithm and unique factorization.

### 7.2 Physical Analogies

The prime spectral measure is analogous to:
- **Atomic emission spectra**: Each prime is like a chemical element with a characteristic spectral line
- **NMR spectroscopy**: The frequencies log(p)/(2π) are like nuclear precession frequencies
- **Gravitational wave detection**: Long observation windows T improve frequency resolution, analogous to LIGO's matched filtering

### 7.3 Limitations

Our formalization covers the "prime side" of the spectral decomposition. The "zero side" — the contribution of ζ-zeros to the Fourier transform — requires analytic continuation and distributional Fourier analysis that go beyond current Mathlib capabilities. The spectral completeness conjecture (that longer windows always detect primes) remains open.

## 8. Future Work

1. **Quantitative spectral convergence**: Prove explicit rates for how quickly spectral peaks converge as T → ∞, connecting to prime gap bounds.

2. **Zero contributions**: Formalize the contribution of non-trivial zeros to the Fourier transform, completing the duality with the explicit formula.

3. **Higher-order spectral analysis**: Extend to the bispectrum and higher-order spectral measures, potentially capturing correlations between primes (twin primes, prime constellations).

4. **Computational prime detection**: Use spectral methods as a practical prime detection algorithm, benchmarking against the sieve of Eratosthenes.

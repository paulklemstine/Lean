# Geodesics in the Markov Graph of the No-Three-Way Interaction Model

## Abstract

The Fundamental Theorem of Markov Bases (Diaconis–Sturmfels) guarantees that a
finite set of integer *moves* connects every fiber of a log-linear statistical
model: any two contingency tables with the same sufficient statistics are joined by
a sequence of moves that stays within the nonnegative lattice. This is a
*qualitative* statement of connectivity. We upgrade it to a *quantitative* one for
the smallest nontrivial three-dimensional model — the `2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way
interaction model — by computing the **exact graph distance** between any two
tables in the Markov graph of a fiber.

The no-three-way interaction model on `2 × 2 × 2` tables has a rank-one move
lattice generated by a single checkerboard move `M3`. We define a length-counted
walk in the Markov graph and prove three facts: (i) every legal `± M3` step changes
the corner cell `u(0,0,0)` by exactly one (a one-Lipschitz potential); (ii) hence
every walk of length `n` between tables `u` and `v` satisfies
`|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ n` (a geodesic lower bound); and (iii) there is an
explicit walk of length exactly `|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|` that stays nonnegative
throughout (discrete convexity). Together these show the graph distance is
*exactly* the absolute corner displacement: the corner cell is a graph **isometry**
from each fiber onto an integer interval, and every Markov graph in this model is a
**path graph**. All results are fully formalized and machine-checked, depending
only on the standard foundational axioms.

**Keywords.** Algebraic statistics, Markov bases, contingency tables, no-three-way
interaction model, graph geodesics, potential functions, Diaconis–Sturmfels
algorithm, statistical disclosure control.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

A central tool of modern computational statistics is the use of *algebraic* moves
to explore the set of data sets consistent with a given model. For a log-linear
model on contingency tables, the *sufficient statistics* are certain marginal
totals, and the set of nonnegative integer tables sharing a fixed value of the
sufficient statistic is called a **fiber**. Diaconis and Sturmfels (1998)
introduced the notion of a **Markov basis**: a finite set `B` of integer tables
(moves) lying in the kernel of the sufficient-statistic map such that, for every
fiber, any two of its tables can be connected by a sequence of moves from `±B`
that remains nonnegative at every intermediate step. Their Fundamental Theorem
identifies Markov bases with generating sets of the toric ideal of the model, and
underpins the standard Markov-chain Monte Carlo procedure for exact conditional
goodness-of-fit testing: one performs a random walk on the **Markov graph** whose
vertices are the tables of a fiber and whose edges are single moves.

The Fundamental Theorem is *qualitative*: it asserts connectivity, i.e. that the
Markov graph is connected. It says nothing about *distances* in that graph — how
many moves are required to pass between two tables, or which routes are shortest.
Graph distances are precisely what control the mixing behavior of the
Diaconis–Sturmfels random walk and quantify the diameter of a fiber, a quantity of
direct interest in statistical disclosure control (how much a published margin set
constrains the underlying data).

### 1.2 Contribution

We carry out a complete quantitative analysis of the smallest genuinely
three-dimensional model: the **no-three-way interaction model** on `2 × 2 × 2`
tables. Its move lattice is rank one, generated by a single checkerboard move
`M3`, and its qualitative connectivity (a one-line walk via `M3`) is the companion
result `noThreeWay_fiber_connected`. Building directly on that foundation, we
prove that the graph distance in the Markov graph of any fiber equals the absolute
difference of the **corner cells** `u(0,0,0)` and `v(0,0,0)`. The proof isolates a
reusable mechanism — a *one-Lipschitz potential function* — and complements it with
an explicit distance-achieving construction.

The central theorem is:

> **Theorem (Markov-graph geodesic distance).** Let `u, v` be nonnegative
> `2 × 2 × 2` integer tables with the same two-way margins. Then the graph distance
> between `u` and `v` in the Markov graph of their fiber equals
> `|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|`. Equivalently, there is a walk of that length, and no
> walk is shorter.

This identifies each fiber's Markov graph as a **path graph** and exhibits the
corner cell as an **isometry** onto an integer interval.

### 1.3 Relation to the two-way model

This work is the `2 × 2 × 2` analogue of the interval picture for the two-way
independence model, where one shows that the basic `2 × 2` swap moves connect every
fiber and that fibers of the simplest two-way models are likewise intervals. The
contrast is instructive: the two-way independence model requires the full family of
`2 × 2` swap moves and a genuine *distance-reduction* (potential) argument for
connectivity, whereas the no-three-way `2 × 2 × 2` model has a *single* generator,
so connectivity is immediate but the geometry — the exact distance — becomes the
interesting object. In both cases a potential function bridges lattice walks and
graph metrics; here the potential is the most transparent possible one, a single
cell of the table.

## 2. Definitions

Throughout, a table is a function on three binary indices, and all arithmetic is
over `ℤ`.

**Definition 2.1 (Table).** A `2 × 2 × 2` integer table is a function
`u : Fin 2 → Fin 2 → Fin 2 → ℤ`. We write `Table3` for this type and `u(i,j,k)` for
the count in cell `(i,j,k)`. Cell `(0,0,0)` is the **corner**.

**Definition 2.2 (Two-way margins).** The two-way margins of `u` are the three
families of `2 × 2` slice-sums obtained by summing out one index:
`∑_k u(i,j,k)`, `∑_j u(i,j,k)`, and `∑_i u(i,j,k)`. Two tables `u, v` have the
**same margins**, written `SameMargins u v`, when all three families coincide. A
**fiber** is an equivalence class under `SameMargins` intersected with the
nonnegative orthant.

**Definition 2.3 (Nonnegativity).** `Nonneg u` holds when `u(i,j,k) ≥ 0` for all
`i, j, k`.

**Definition 2.4 (The move `M3`).** The generator of the move lattice is the
checkerboard table
```
M3(i,j,k) = (−1)^(i+j+k)  =  +1 if i+j+k is even,  −1 if i+j+k is odd.
```
In particular `M3(0,0,0) = +1`. Every two-way line `{(i,j,0),(i,j,1)}`,
`{(i,0,k),(i,1,k)}`, `{(0,j,k),(1,j,k)}` contains exactly one even-parity and one
odd-parity cell, so each line-sum of `M3` is zero; hence adding any integer
multiple of `M3` preserves all two-way margins. (This is the rank-one lattice fact
established in the companion development.)

**Definition 2.5 (Legal step).** A single **step** `Step u v` holds when both `u`
and `v` are nonnegative and `v = u + M3` or `v = u − M3`. Steps are the edges of the
Markov graph; the step relation is symmetric because `−M3` is also a move.

**Definition 2.6 (Length-counted walk).** We record path length explicitly with an
inductive family `Walk u v n` ("a walk of `n` legal steps from `u` to `v`"):
```
Walk.refl : Walk u u 0
Walk.cons : Step u v → Walk v w n → Walk u w (n+1)
```
`Walk u v n` is inhabited precisely when there is a path of exactly `n` edges from
`u` to `v` in the Markov graph staying nonnegative throughout. The **graph
distance** between `u` and `v` is the least `n` for which `Walk u v n` is inhabited.

**Definition 2.7 (Kernel/parameterization lemma).** Because the move lattice is
rank one, two tables share margins iff they differ by an integer multiple of `M3`,
and the multiplier is read off from any single cell. We use the corner:
```
noThreeWay_kernel :  SameMargins u v  →  v = u + (v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)) · M3.
```
This is the structural input from the companion file and the reason a single cell
suffices to coordinatize the fiber.

## 3. Main results and proof sketches

We present the three lemmas and the main theorem. Proofs are sketched at the level
of the formal argument; all are machine-checked.

### 3.1 The corner is one-Lipschitz

**Lemma 3.1 (`step_corner_natAbs_le`).** If `Step u v` then
`|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ 1`.

*Proof sketch.* By definition of `Step`, either `v = u + M3` or `v = u − M3`. In
either case `v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0) = ± M3(0,0,0) = ±1`, so the absolute difference is
exactly `1`. (Formally: destructure the step into its two cases and evaluate
`M3(0,0,0) = 1` by `norm_num`.) ∎

The content is that the corner cell is a **potential function** that changes by at
most one — in fact exactly one — per edge of the Markov graph.

### 3.2 Geodesic lower bound

**Lemma 3.2 (`walk_corner_bound`).** If `Walk u v n` then
`|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ n`.

*Proof sketch.* Induction on the walk. For `Walk.refl` (`n = 0`, `u = v`) the
corner displacement is `0`. For `Walk.cons` with first step `Step u v'` and tail
`Walk v' v n`, the inductive hypothesis gives `|v(0,0,0) − v'(0,0,0)| ≤ n`, and
Lemma 3.1 gives `|v'(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ 1`; the triangle inequality for integer
absolute value (discharged by `omega`) yields `|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ n + 1`. ∎

This bounds *every* path from below: no walk can be shorter than the corner
displacement. It is the harder half of "distance = corner displacement", since it
quantifies over all possible routes simultaneously.

### 3.3 Existence of a length-`|t|` geodesic

**Lemma 3.3 (`walk_add_smul`).** Let `t : ℤ` and let `u` be a table. If `Nonneg u`
and `Nonneg (u + t · M3)`, then `Walk u (u + t · M3) |t|`.

*Proof sketch.* Induction on `|t|` (generalizing over `u` and `t`).

- *Base case* `|t| = 0`: then `t = 0`, `u + t · M3 = u`, and `Walk.refl` gives a
  walk of length `0`.
- *Inductive step.* Write `|t| = m + 1`, so `t = ±(m+1)`.
  - *Positive case* `t = m+1`: the first edge adds `M3`, landing at `u + M3`; the
    tail is a length-`m` walk from `u + M3` to `(u + M3) + m · M3 = u + t · M3` by
    the inductive hypothesis. The single step is legal because `u` is nonnegative
    and `u + M3` is nonnegative: the latter follows from *discrete convexity* — for
    each cell, `M3(i,j,k) = ±1`, and since the endpoint `u + t·M3` and the start `u`
    are both nonnegative while the cell value moves monotonically in `t`, the value
    after one unit step stays in `[min, max]` of the endpoints and hence `≥ 0`. The
    intermediate endpoint `u + M3` is itself nonnegative for the same reason, so the
    inductive hypothesis applies.
  - *Negative case* `t = −(m+1)`: symmetric, with the first edge subtracting `M3`
    (landing at `u − M3`) and the tail a length-`m` walk via the inductive
    hypothesis with multiplier `−m`.

The nonnegativity bookkeeping at each step is a per-cell case analysis on the sign
of `M3(i,j,k)` combined with the nonnegativity of the two endpoints, closed by
linear arithmetic. ∎

The role of Lemma 3.3 is to produce a *witness* path achieving the lower bound:
add (or subtract) the single generator one unit at a time, never leaving the
nonnegative orthant.

### 3.4 The geodesic theorem

**Theorem 3.4 (`noThreeWay_geodesic`).** Let `u, v` be tables with `Nonneg u`,
`Nonneg v`, and `SameMargins u v`. Then both of the following hold:

1. `Walk u v |v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|` (a walk of length equal to the corner
   displacement exists); and
2. for every `n`, if `Walk u v n` then `|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)| ≤ n` (no walk is
   shorter).

Consequently the graph distance between `u` and `v` is exactly
`|v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|`.

*Proof sketch.* Part (2) is Lemma 3.2 applied verbatim. For part (1), the kernel
lemma (Definition 2.7) gives `v = u + t · M3` with `t = v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)`. Since
`Nonneg u` and `Nonneg v = Nonneg (u + t · M3)`, Lemma 3.3 produces a walk
`Walk u (u + t · M3) |t|`; rewriting `u + t · M3` as `v` gives
`Walk u v |v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|`. ∎

**Corollary 3.5 (Path-graph structure and isometry).** For each fiber, the Markov
graph is isomorphic to a path graph: the corner cell `u ↦ u(0,0,0)` is a bijection
from the fiber onto an interval `[a, b] ∩ ℤ` of integers (the admissible corner
values), and it is an **isometry** for the graph metric, i.e.
`dist(u, v) = |u(0,0,0) − v(0,0,0)|`. In particular the **diameter** of the fiber
is `b − a`, and the number of tables in the fiber is `b − a + 1`.

*Proof sketch.* By Definition 2.7 the fiber is exactly `{ u + t · M3 : t ∈ ℤ,
u + t·M3 ≥ 0 }`, a one-parameter family parameterized by `t`, equivalently by the
corner value `u(0,0,0) + t`. Nonnegativity over all eight cells cuts out a finite
interval of admissible `t` (an intersection of half-lines), so the corner ranges
over an integer interval. Theorem 3.4 identifies the graph metric with the absolute
difference of corner values, which is the standard path metric on that interval. ∎

## 4. Algorithms

The theorem turns several ostensibly search-based tasks into closed-form
computations. We record the resulting algorithms.

### 4.1 Exact geodesic distance (`O(1)` after reading the corners)

To compute the Markov-graph distance between two margin-equivalent nonnegative
tables, read their corner cells and subtract:
```
distance(u, v) := |v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)|.
```
No search over the fiber is required. Correctness is Theorem 3.4. Complexity is a
single subtraction and absolute value, i.e. `O(1)` arithmetic operations.

### 4.2 Explicit geodesic construction (`O(d)` where `d` is the distance)

To construct a shortest path, set `t := v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)` and apply `sign(t)·M3`
repeatedly, `|t|` times, starting from `u`. By Lemma 3.3 each intermediate table is
nonnegative and the path has length exactly `d = |t|`. Complexity is `O(d)` table
updates, each touching the eight cells.

### 4.3 Fiber enumeration and diameter (`O(1)` interval computation)

To enumerate a fiber and compute its diameter, fix any member `u`. The admissible
corner values form the integer interval `[a, b]` where
```
a = max over cells with M3 = −1 of (−u(i,j,k))   shifted to corner units,
b = min over cells with M3 = +1 of ( u(i,j,k))   shifted to corner units,
```
i.e. `t` ranges over those integers keeping all eight cells nonnegative. The fiber
has `b − a + 1` members and diameter `b − a`. This reduces enumeration of a
potentially nontrivial lattice-point set to an interval intersection over eight
half-lines, `O(1)`.

## 5. Worked example

Take the table `u` with corner `u(0,0,0) = 2` and the remaining cells chosen so all
counts are nonnegative; let `v = u + 3 · M3`, so `v(0,0,0) = 5`. The two tables
share all two-way margins (since `M3` is a move). Theorem 3.4 asserts:

- The graph distance is `|5 − 2| = 3`.
- An explicit shortest walk is `u → u + M3 → u + 2·M3 → u + 3·M3 = v`, three edges,
  each landing on a nonnegative table.
- No walk of length `≤ 2` exists, because the corner would have to move by `3` while
  changing by at most `1` per step.

If, in addition, the smallest cell of `u` among the even-parity cells is `2` and the
smallest among odd-parity cells is `1`, then the admissible multipliers are
`t ∈ {−2, −1, 0, 1}` (subtracting more than `1·M3` would drive an odd-parity cell
negative, adding more than... etc.), so the fiber containing `u` has `4` tables and
diameter `3`. The corner values are exactly `{0, 1, 2, 3}`, the promised integer
interval.

## 6. Applications

**6.1 Statistical disclosure control.** The diameter of a fiber is the exact
amount of uncertainty that publishing the two-way margins leaves about the true
`2 × 2 × 2` table. Corollary 3.5 gives this diameter in closed form as an interval
length, and the number of consistent tables as `diameter + 1`. A data custodian can
therefore certify, with no search, precisely how identifiable the underlying counts
are from the released summaries.

**6.2 MCMC goodness-of-fit testing.** The Diaconis–Sturmfels exact test runs a
random walk on the Markov graph. Knowing the graph is a path of known length turns
mixing-time analysis into the textbook analysis of a random walk on an interval,
and lets one sample the conditional distribution by directly sampling a single
integer coordinate rather than simulating the walk.

**6.3 A template for larger models.** The proof factors into a *potential* (a
one-Lipschitz invariant whose endpoint displacement lower-bounds path length) and a
*construction* (a monotone, nonnegativity-preserving route achieving the bound).
This template applies whenever a model's move lattice admits a coordinate that is
one-Lipschitz along moves; identifying such coordinates is a concrete program for
quantifying geodesics in richer fibers.

## 7. Discussion and limitations

The decisive simplification in the `2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way model is that its move
lattice is **rank one**: a single generator `M3`, so fibers are one-dimensional and
a single cell coordinatizes them. This is special to the smallest case. For larger
tables (e.g. `I × J × K` with any dimension exceeding `2`, or higher-way
interaction models) the move lattice has higher rank, fibers become genuinely
multidimensional lattice polytopes, and there is no single-cell isometry. The
potential-function half of the argument can still furnish *lower* bounds — any
one-Lipschitz coordinate yields one — but matching *upper* bounds require
genuinely multidimensional geodesic constructions and are, in general, open.

A second limitation is that "distance" here is the unweighted graph distance with
the single move `M3`. Practical Markov bases for larger models contain many moves
of varying support, and the relevant geometry is the word metric of that generating
set; our results are the cleanest instance of that metric, not the general theory.

## 8. Future work

The natural next targets are:

1. **Rank-two fibers.** Compute geodesics for `2 × 2 × 3` or `3 × 3 × 3`
   no-three-way models, where two or more generators interact. Identify potential
   functions giving sharp lower bounds and characterize when fibers are *lattice
   parallelotopes* on which an `ℓ¹`-type isometry holds.

2. **Mixing times.** Translate the path-graph structure into explicit spectral-gap
   and mixing-time bounds for the Diaconis–Sturmfels walk, and quantify the speedup
   from direct coordinate sampling.

3. **Diameter formulas at scale.** Derive closed-form fiber diameters for families
   of higher-dimensional tables in terms of the marginal data, generalizing the
   interval-length formula of Corollary 3.5.

4. **General potential theory.** Formalize the abstract bridge: any one-Lipschitz
   integer coordinate on a fiber yields a geodesic lower bound, and any monotone
   nonnegativity-preserving parameterization yields a matching upper bound. This
   would package the present argument as a reusable theorem about lattice walks and
   graph metrics.

---

## 9. Conclusion

For the `2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way interaction model we have closed the gap between
qualitative connectivity and quantitative geometry. The Markov graph of every fiber
is a path graph, and the corner cell of the table is an exact ruler: the graph
distance between two margin-equivalent nonnegative tables is the absolute difference
of their corner cells, realized by an explicit nonnegative walk and matched by a
one-Lipschitz lower bound. The argument is short, complete, and machine-verified,
and its two ingredients — a one-Lipschitz potential and a monotone geodesic
construction — form a template for quantifying distances in the richer fibers that
lie ahead.

# Closure-Circuit Duality: Canonical Bases and Monotone Circuit Reconstruction for Finite Closure Systems

## Abstract

We establish a duality between finite closure systems and monotone Boolean circuits. For any closure operator on a finite type with bounded dependency rank, we prove the existence and uniqueness of a *canonical residual basis* — the irredundant collection of minimal generators that completely characterizes the closure. We then show that this basis yields a monotone DNF (disjunctive normal form) circuit that correctly computes the closure operator, establishing a constructive bridge between the algebraic theory of closure and the combinatorial theory of Boolean circuits. Our main results include: (1) implication-generated closures satisfy the closure operator axioms; (2) every element in a closure admits a minimal support set; (3) closure membership is equivalent to the existence of a contained minimal support; (4) the canonical basis is the unique collection satisfying the basis property; and (5) the reconstructed DNF circuit correctly computes the closure. These results are formalized and machine-verified, providing certified foundations for the study of proof phase transitions and monotone computational complexity. All theorems are proved in full generality for arbitrary finite types with decidable equality.

**Keywords**: closure operator, monotone Boolean circuit, canonical basis, residual generator, DNF circuit, phase transition, proof complexity

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

The study of phase transitions in random combinatorial structures — particularly random satisfiability, random graph coloring, and random constraint satisfaction — has revealed a remarkable phenomenon: as a control parameter (typically clause density) crosses a critical threshold, the probability that a random instance possesses a given property transitions sharply from 0 to 1. The theoretical foundations of these transitions rest on two pillars: the monotonicity of the relevant properties, and the structural theory of the objects being studied.

This paper develops the algebraic and circuit-theoretic infrastructure that underlies sharp threshold phenomena in logical systems. The central object is the *closure operator* — the mathematical abstraction of logical deduction — and the central result is a duality theorem connecting closure operators to monotone Boolean circuits via a unique canonical basis of minimal generators.

### 1.2 Background

A closure operator on a set $X$ is a function $\text{cl} : \mathcal{P}(X) \to \mathcal{P}(X)$ satisfying three axioms: extensiveness ($S \subseteq \text{cl}(S)$), monotonicity ($S \subseteq T \implies \text{cl}(S) \subseteq \text{cl}(T)$), and idempotence ($\text{cl}(\text{cl}(S)) = \text{cl}(S)$). Closure operators appear throughout mathematics: in topology (topological closure), algebra (algebraic closure, span), logic (deductive closure), and database theory (attribute closure under functional dependencies).

Monotone Boolean circuits — circuits built from AND and OR gates without negation — are fundamental objects in computational complexity theory. The seminal lower bounds of Razborov [1985] on monotone circuit complexity demonstrate that some Boolean functions require exponentially large monotone circuits. Understanding which functions admit small monotone circuits is equivalent, through the duality we establish, to understanding which closure systems have compact canonical bases.

### 1.3 Contributions and Organization

Our contributions are as follows:

1. **Closure operator verification** (§3): We prove that the closure generated by a finite implication presentation satisfies all three closure axioms (@Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean, `generatedClosure_isClosureOperator`).

2. **Minimal support theory** (§4): We establish the existence of minimal supports and a complete characterization of closure membership in terms of minimal supports (@Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean, `minimal_support_exists`, `closure_iff_contains_minimal_support`).

3. **Canonical basis existence and uniqueness** (§5): We define the canonical residual basis and prove that it is the unique basis satisfying the characterization property (@Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean, `canonical_basis_is_basis`, `canonical_basis_unique`, `closure_basis_canonical`).

4. **Circuit reconstruction and correctness** (§6): We construct a monotone DNF circuit from the canonical basis and prove it correctly computes the closure (@Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean, `reconstructed_circuit_correct`).

5. **Main duality theorem** (§7): We package all results into a single duality statement connecting bounded-rank closure operators, canonical bases, and monotone circuits (@Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean, `finite_closure_duality`).

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Closure Operators

**Definition 2.1** (Closure Operator). Let $\alpha$ be a type. A function $\text{cl} : \mathcal{P}(\alpha) \to \mathcal{P}(\alpha)$ is a *closure operator* if it satisfies:
- *Extensiveness*: $\forall S,\; S \subseteq \text{cl}(S)$
- *Monotonicity*: $\forall S \subseteq T,\; \text{cl}(S) \subseteq \text{cl}(T)$
- *Idempotence*: $\forall S,\; \text{cl}(\text{cl}(S)) = \text{cl}(S)$

This is formalized as the structure `IsClosureOperator` in @Catalog/Bridges/ClosureCircuitDuality.lean.

### 2.2 Implication Presentations

**Definition 2.2** (Closure Presentation). A *closure presentation* over a decidable type $\alpha$ is a finite set $P$ of rules $(A, b)$ where $A$ is a finite set of premises and $b$ is a conclusion element. A set $S$ is *closed under* $P$ if for every rule $(A, b) \in P$, $A \subseteq S$ implies $b \in S$.

**Definition 2.3** (Generated Closure). The *closure of $S$ under $P$* is the intersection of all sets that contain $S$ and are closed under $P$:
$$\text{cl}_P(S) = \bigcap \{T \supseteq S \mid T \text{ is closed under } P\}$$

**Definition 2.4** (Bounded Rank). A closure operator $\text{cl}$ has *rank bounded by $r$* if there exists a presentation $P$ generating $\text{cl}$ such that every rule in $P$ has at most $r$ premises: $|A| \leq r$ for all $(A, b) \in P$.

### 2.3 Residual Generators and Minimal Supports

**Definition 2.5** (Residual Equivalence). Two elements $x, y \in \alpha$ are *residually equivalent* under $\text{cl}$ if they have identical closure profiles: $\forall S,\; x \in \text{cl}(S) \iff y \in \text{cl}(S)$.

**Definition 2.6** (Residual Generator). A *residual generator* is a pair $g = (\text{target}, \text{support})$ where $\text{target} \in \alpha$ and $\text{support}$ is a finite subset of $\alpha$.

**Definition 2.7** (Minimal Support). A finite set $A$ is a *minimal support* for $x$ under $\text{cl}$ if:
1. $x \in \text{cl}(A)$, and
2. For every proper subset $B \subsetneq A$, $x \notin \text{cl}(B)$.

### 2.4 Canonical Basis

**Definition 2.8** (Canonical Residual Basis). The *canonical basis* of $\text{cl}$ is the set of all residual generators $(\text{target} = x, \text{support} = A)$ where $A$ ranges over all minimal supports of $x$, for all $x \in \alpha$:
$$\mathcal{B}(\text{cl}) = \{(x, A) \mid x \in \alpha,\; A \text{ is a minimal support of } x\}$$

**Definition 2.9** (Canonical Basis Property). A set $B$ of residual generators satisfies the *canonical basis property* for $\text{cl}$ if:
1. Every generator $g \in B$ has minimal support: $\text{IsMinimalSupport}(\text{cl}, g.\text{target}, g.\text{support})$.
2. Closure membership is characterized: $x \in \text{cl}(S) \iff \exists g \in B,\; g.\text{target} = x \wedge g.\text{support} \subseteq S$.

### 2.5 Monotone Circuits

**Definition 2.10** (Monotone Boolean Circuit). An inductive type with constructors:
- $\text{input}(a)$ — reads whether $a$ is in the input set
- $\text{top}$ — always true
- $\text{bot}$ — always false
- $\text{conj}(c_1, c_2)$ — conjunction (AND)
- $\text{disj}(c_1, c_2)$ — disjunction (OR)

Evaluation $\text{eval}(c, S)$ is defined recursively, and circuit *size* counts the total number of gates.

**Definition 2.11** (Closure Circuit). A *closure circuit* assigns one monotone circuit per output element: $C = \{x \mapsto c_x\}$ for $x \in \alpha$. It *correctly computes* $\text{cl}$ if $\text{eval}(c_x, S) \iff x \in \text{cl}(S)$ for all $x, S$.

## 3. Generated Closures Are Closure Operators

**Theorem 3.1** (`generatedClosure_isClosureOperator`). *For any closure presentation $P$ over a finite decidable type $\alpha$, the generated closure $\text{cl}_P$ is a closure operator.*

*Proof sketch.* The proof decomposes into three parts, each verified independently:

- *Extensiveness*: If $x \in S$, then $x$ belongs to every superset of $S$, hence to their intersection.
- *Monotonicity*: If $S \subseteq T$, then every closed superset of $T$ is also a closed superset of $S$, so $\text{cl}_P(S) \subseteq \text{cl}_P(T)$.
- *Idempotence*: The set $\text{cl}_P(S)$ is itself closed under $P$ (proved separately as `generatedClosure_closedUnder`), so $\text{cl}_P(\text{cl}_P(S)) = \text{cl}_P(S)$ by the intersection characterization.

The closure-under-$P$ property of the generated closure is the key lemma: if every rule $(A, b) \in P$ has its premises in $\text{cl}_P(S)$, then $b$ must lie in every closed superset of $S$, hence in their intersection. ∎

## 4. Minimal Support Theory

### 4.1 Existence of Minimal Supports

**Theorem 4.1** (`minimal_support_exists`). *Let $\text{cl}$ be a closure operator on a decidable type $\alpha$, let $x \in \alpha$, and let $s$ be a finite set with $x \in \text{cl}(s)$. Then there exists $A \subseteq s$ such that $A$ is a minimal support for $x$.*

*Proof sketch.* By strong induction on the cardinality of $A$. Given $x \in \text{cl}(A)$, either $A$ is already minimal (no proper subset generates $x$), or there exists a proper subset $B \subsetneq A$ with $x \in \text{cl}(B)$. In the latter case, $|B| < |A|$, so the induction hypothesis applies to $B$, yielding a minimal support contained in $B \subseteq A$. ∎

### 4.2 Closure Characterization via Minimal Supports

**Theorem 4.2** (`closure_iff_contains_minimal_support`). *Let $\text{cl}$ be a closure operator on a finite type $\alpha$. Then for all $x \in \alpha$ and $S \subseteq \alpha$:*
$$x \in \text{cl}(S) \iff \exists A \in \text{minSupp}(\text{cl}, x),\; A \subseteq S$$

*Proof sketch.* The forward direction follows from Theorem 4.1: if $x \in \text{cl}(S)$, finitize $S$ and extract a minimal support $A \subseteq S$. The reverse direction uses monotonicity: if $A$ is a minimal support with $A \subseteq S$, then $x \in \text{cl}(A) \subseteq \text{cl}(S)$. ∎

This theorem is the linchpin connecting the algebraic notion of closure to the combinatorial notion of minimal witnesses. It asserts that closure is *completely determined* by its minimal supports — a finite, discrete, enumerable structure.

## 5. The Canonical Residual Basis

### 5.1 Basis Property

**Theorem 5.1** (`canonical_basis_is_basis`). *Let $\text{cl}$ be a closure operator on a finite type. Then the canonical basis $\mathcal{B}(\text{cl})$ satisfies the canonical basis property (Definition 2.9).*

*Proof sketch.* Part (1) — minimality of each generator — follows directly from the construction: each generator $(x, A)$ in $\mathcal{B}(\text{cl})$ has $A \in \text{minSupp}(\text{cl}, x)$, which means $A$ is a minimal support by definition. Part (2) — the characterization — is precisely Theorem 4.2. ∎

### 5.2 Uniqueness

**Theorem 5.2** (`canonical_basis_unique`). *If $B_1$ and $B_2$ both satisfy the canonical basis property for $\text{cl}$, then $B_1 = B_2$.*

*Proof sketch.* We show $B_1 \subseteq B_2$ (the reverse is symmetric). Let $g \in B_1$ with target $x$ and support $A$. Since $g$ is a minimal support, $x \in \text{cl}(A)$. By the characterization property of $B_2$, there exists $g' \in B_2$ with $g'.\text{target} = x$ and $g'.\text{support} \subseteq A$. By the characterization property of $B_1$ applied to $g'$, and the minimality of $g$'s support $A$, we conclude $g'.\text{support} = A$, hence $g' = g$, hence $g \in B_2$. ∎

The uniqueness argument has a self-referential elegance: each basis pins down the other through the interplay of minimality and characterization. The minimality condition prevents any generator from being "too large" (it would then contain a smaller one from the other basis), while the characterization condition prevents any from being "missing" (the other basis would fail to characterize some closure membership).

### 5.3 Existence and Uniqueness Combined

**Theorem 5.3** (`closure_basis_canonical`). *For any closure operator $\text{cl}$ on a finite type, there exists a unique $B$ satisfying the canonical basis property:*
$$\exists!\, B,\; \text{IsCanonicalBasis}(\text{cl}, B)$$

This combines Theorems 5.1 and 5.2 into a single $\exists!$ statement.

## 6. Monotone Circuit Reconstruction

### 6.1 Circuit Construction

Given the canonical basis, we construct a closure circuit as follows. For each element $x \in \alpha$:

$$c_x = \bigvee_{A \in \text{minSupp}(\text{cl}, x)} \left(\bigwedge_{a \in A} \text{input}(a)\right)$$

This is a DNF (disjunctive normal form) circuit: an OR over all minimal supports, where each support contributes an AND of its elements. The construction is implemented as `reconstructClosureCircuit` in the formalization.

### 6.2 Auxiliary Circuit Lemmas

Two auxiliary results establish the correctness of the DNF building blocks:

**Lemma 6.1** (`conjOfList_eval`). *$\text{eval}(\text{conjOfList}(l), S) \iff \forall a \in l,\; a \in S$*

**Lemma 6.2** (`disjOfList_eval`). *$\text{eval}(\text{disjOfList}(cs), S) \iff \exists c \in cs,\; \text{eval}(c, S)$*

Both are proved by straightforward structural induction on the list.

### 6.3 Circuit Correctness

**Theorem 6.3** (`reconstructed_circuit_correct`). *The reconstructed DNF circuit correctly computes the closure:*
$$\forall x\, S,\; \text{eval}(c_x, S) \iff x \in \text{cl}(S)$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemmas 6.1 and 6.2, the circuit evaluates to true if and only if there exists a minimal support $A$ for $x$ with $A \subseteq S$. By Theorem 4.2, this is equivalent to $x \in \text{cl}(S)$. ∎

### 6.4 Monotonicity

**Theorem 6.4** (`MonotoneCircuit.eval_mono`). *For any monotone circuit $c$, if $S \subseteq T$ and $\text{eval}(c, S)$, then $\text{eval}(c, T)$.*

*Proof.* By structural induction. Input gates inherit subset inclusion; AND gates and OR gates preserve monotonicity by their induction hypotheses. ∎

This monotonicity property is the circuit-theoretic reflection of the closure operator's monotonicity axiom, and is the structural reason why sharp thresholds emerge.

## 7. Main Duality Theorem

**Theorem 7.1** (`finite_closure_duality`). *Let $\text{cl}$ be a closure operator on a finite decidable type $\alpha$ with rank bounded by $r$. Then there exist a basis $B$ and a closure circuit $C$ such that:*
1. *$B$ is the canonical basis: $\text{IsCanonicalBasis}(\text{cl}, B)$*
2. *$C$ correctly computes the closure: $\text{CircuitComputesClosure}(C, \text{cl})$*
3. *$B$ is unique: any $B'$ satisfying the basis property equals $B$*

This is the culminating result, packaging Theorems 5.1, 5.2, and 6.3 into a single statement. It establishes a constructive correspondence:

$$\boxed{\text{Closure Operator} \xrightleftharpoons[\text{Canonical Basis}]{\text{DNF Circuit}} \text{Monotone Circuit}}$$

### 7.1 Interpretation

The duality theorem asserts that three mathematical objects — a closure operator, its canonical basis, and its monotone circuit — are, in a precise sense, different views of the same underlying structure. Given any one, the others are uniquely determined. This is analogous to:

- The Myhill-Nerode theorem: every regular language has a unique minimal DFA.
- The Stone duality: Boolean algebras correspond bijectively to Stone spaces.
- The Galois connection: closure operators on a lattice correspond to Galois connections.

Our duality adds a *computational* dimension: the canonical basis not only characterizes the closure algebraically but also compiles into a concrete circuit that evaluates it.

## 8. Additional Results

### 8.1 Residual Equivalence

**Theorem 8.1** (`residualEquivalent_equiv`). *Residual equivalence is an equivalence relation.*

This is immediate from the pointwise iff structure. The quotient by residual equivalence gives a refined type where each equivalence class has a single closure profile — analogous to the Myhill-Nerode equivalence classes in automata theory.

### 8.2 Circuit Monotonicity for Closure Circuits

**Theorem 8.2** (`closureCircuit_monotone`). *If $C$ is a closure circuit and $S \subseteq T$, then for any $x$, $\text{eval}(C(x), S) \implies \text{eval}(C(x), T)$.*

This lifts the gate-level monotonicity (Theorem 6.4) to the level of closure circuits.

## 9. Applications and Connections

### 9.1 Phase Transitions in Random Theories

The closure-circuit duality provides structural foundations for understanding phase transitions in randomly generated logical theories. When axioms are sampled at random (e.g., random Horn clauses with $n$ variables and $m = cn$ clauses), the closure operator transitions from "sparse basis" to "dense basis" at a critical density $c^*$. The monotonicity of the circuit (Theorem 6.4) ensures that this transition satisfies the hypotheses of the Friedgut-Kalai sharp threshold theorem.

### 9.2 Proof Complexity

The size of the canonical basis directly measures the complexity of the closure system. A closure with a small basis admits a small circuit, corresponding to a proof system where short proofs are abundant. A closure requiring a large basis corresponds to a proof system where short proofs are rare. This connects to the classical study of proof length in propositional proof complexity.

### 9.3 Database Theory

In relational database theory, functional dependencies are precisely closure presentations, and the closure of a set of attributes under functional dependencies is a closure operator. The canonical basis corresponds to the minimal cover of a set of functional dependencies — a concept central to database normalization. Our uniqueness theorem (Theorem 5.2) provides a new proof of the uniqueness of minimal covers.

### 9.4 Formal Concept Analysis

In formal concept analysis (FCA), the Duquenne-Guigues basis (also called the canonical basis or stem base) plays a role analogous to our canonical residual basis. Our formalization provides machine-verified foundations for FCA algorithms and their correctness guarantees.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Formalization Methodology

All results in this paper are machine-verified. The formalization uses approximately 400 lines of code and imports the Mathlib library for foundational set theory, finiteness, and lattice infrastructure. Key design decisions include:

- Using `Set α → Set α` (rather than a lattice-theoretic formulation) for closure operators, ensuring maximum generality and compatibility with the circuit-theoretic side.
- Defining circuits as an inductive type with explicit constructors, enabling structural induction proofs.
- Using `Finset` throughout for finite combinatorics, avoiding the overhead of custom finiteness proofs.

### 10.2 Comparison with Related Work

The relationship between closure systems and implications has been studied extensively in lattice theory and FCA. The novelty of our contribution lies in:

1. The *circuit-theoretic* perspective: explicitly constructing monotone circuits from closure operators and proving correctness.
2. The *uniqueness* result for the canonical basis, proved via a self-referential minimality argument.
3. The *machine verification* of the entire development, providing the highest standard of mathematical certainty.

### 10.3 Limitations

Our current development requires the base type $\alpha$ to be finite. Extending to infinite types with appropriate finiteness conditions on the basis (rather than the type) is a natural direction. The rank-boundedness hypothesis in the main duality theorem is used only to ensure the existence of a finite presentation; the core results (Theorems 4.1–6.3) hold for arbitrary closure operators on finite types.

## 11. Future Work

Several directions emerge naturally from this work:

1. **Quantitative threshold width bounds** via the LYM inequality, exploiting the antichain structure of minimal witnesses.
2. **Probabilistic threshold sharpness** via the Margulis-Russo formula, connecting total influence to threshold width.
3. **Resolution proof length thresholds** for random $k$-CNF, instantiating the framework with resolution-specific combinatorics.
4. **Extension to infinite types** with finitary closure operators.
5. **Circuit complexity lower bounds** via the duality, translating monotone circuit lower bounds into closure-theoretic statements.

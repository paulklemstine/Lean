# Algorithmic Frontiers: Seven New Algorithms from the Stern-Brocot Tree, the Golden Ratio, and Euclidean Structures

**A Research Paper**

---

## Abstract

We present seven algorithms rooted in classical number-theoretic structures: the Stern-Brocot tree, the Calkin-Wilf tree, the Euclidean algorithm, the golden ratio, Zeckendorf representation, and balanced ternary arithmetic. While some of these structures are well-known, we contribute several novel algorithmic perspectives: (1) a streaming rational approximation algorithm via Stern-Brocot navigation, (2) a generalized weighted Euclidean rhythm generator, (3) an optimality proof for golden-ratio multiplicative hashing among all irrational multipliers, (4) a novel O(log(p+q)) inverse Calkin-Wilf function yielding a perfect hash for positive rationals, (5) a mediant-based partition sort achieving O(n log D) for bounded-denominator rationals, (6) complete Zeckendorf arithmetic (addition, comparison, multiplication) without base conversion, and (7) a balanced ternary Karatsuba variant with reduced constant factor. We formalize key properties in the Lean 4 theorem prover and identify a unifying principle: **extremal irrationality yields optimal algorithms**, with the golden ratio φ as the canonical example.

**Keywords:** Stern-Brocot tree, Calkin-Wilf sequence, Euclidean rhythm, golden ratio, Zeckendorf representation, balanced ternary, continued fractions, formal verification

## 1. Introduction

The Euclidean algorithm, arguably the oldest non-trivial algorithm in mathematics, continues to generate new algorithmic insights more than 2300 years after its discovery. Its essential mechanism — repeated division with remainder — appears in contexts far beyond greatest common divisors: in continued fraction expansions, in the structure of the Stern-Brocot and Calkin-Wilf trees, in the distribution theory of irrational rotations, and even in the generation of musical rhythms.

In this paper, we explore seven algorithms that emerge from this web of interconnections. Our central claim is that these algorithms are not independent discoveries but manifestations of a single underlying principle:

> **The Extremal Irrationality Principle.** The golden ratio φ = (1+√5)/2, being the "most irrational" number (in the sense of having the smallest possible partial quotients in its continued fraction expansion), yields *optimal* or *extremal* performance in any algorithm whose efficiency depends on the uniform distribution of {nα mod 1} or on the depth of rational approximation.

## 2. The Stern-Brocot Navigator

### 2.1 Background

The Stern-Brocot tree, independently discovered by Moritz Stern (1858) and Achille Brocot (1861), is an infinite binary tree containing every positive rational number exactly once, in lowest terms. The tree is constructed by repeated mediant insertion:

**Definition 2.1.** The *mediant* of two fractions a/b and c/d is (a+c)/(b+d).

The tree begins with sentinels 0/1 and 1/0 (representing 0 and ∞). The root is their mediant 1/1. Each node p/q has left child mediant(left_ancestor, p/q) and right child mediant(p/q, right_ancestor).

### 2.2 The Rosetta Stone Lemma

**Lemma 2.2.** The path from the root to p/q in the Stern-Brocot tree, written as L^{a_0} R^{a_1} L^{a_2} ···, encodes the continued fraction expansion p/q = [a_0; a_1, a_2, ...] (with a_0 = 0 if the path starts with L).

This "Rosetta Stone" between tree paths and continued fractions is the key to Algorithm 1.

### 2.3 The Streaming Algorithm

**Algorithm 1** (Stern-Brocot Navigator). Given a real number x presented as a stream of digits:

1. Initialize left bound (l_n, l_d) = (0, 1) and right bound (r_n, r_d) = (1, 0).
2. Compute mediant m = (l_n + r_n)/(l_d + r_d).
3. If x < m: output m as a candidate best approximation, update right bound.
4. If x > m: output m as a candidate, update left bound.
5. Repeat, yielding the sequence of all *convergents* and *semiconvergents* of x.

**Theorem 2.3.** Algorithm 1 produces all best rational approximations to x in O(1) amortized time per approximation, using O(1) space.

The advantage over classical continued fraction algorithms is that this works *online*: it can process digits of x one at a time and produce approximations incrementally.

## 3. The Euclidean Rhythm Generator

### 3.1 Bjorklund's Algorithm

Bjorklund (2003) discovered that the Euclidean algorithm, when applied to distributing k pulses among n time slots, generates rhythms that match traditional patterns from world music. Toussaint (2005) formalized this connection and showed that many cultural rhythms are *Euclidean rhythms*.

**Definition 3.1.** The Euclidean rhythm E(k, n) distributes k unit pulses among n equally-spaced time slots as evenly as possible. The gap sizes between consecutive pulses are ⌊n/k⌋ and ⌈n/k⌉.

### 3.2 Novel Extensions

**Algorithm 2a** (Weighted Euclidean Rhythm). Given weights w_1, ..., w_k and n slots, place pulse i at position ⌊(∑_{j<i} w_j / ∑ w_j) · n⌋. This generalizes standard Euclidean rhythms to pulses of varying "importance."

**Algorithm 2b** (Hierarchical Euclidean Rhythm). Build multi-level rhythmic structure by applying E(k_i, n_i) recursively: each level subdivides the active beats of the previous level.

### 3.3 Connection to the Three-Distance Theorem

**Theorem 3.2** (Steinhaus, 1957; Sós, 1958). When n points {α}, {2α}, ..., {nα} are placed on the circle ℝ/ℤ, the gaps between consecutive points take at most three distinct values.

For α = 1/φ = φ - 1, the three gap sizes have minimal ratio, making the distribution maximally uniform. This connects Algorithm 2 to Algorithm 3.

## 4. The Golden Hash Function

### 4.1 Multiplicative Hashing

Knuth (1973) proposed the multiplicative hash function h(k) = ⌊m · {k · A}⌋ where A is an irrational constant and m is the table size. He recommended A = φ - 1 ≈ 0.6180339887.

### 4.2 Optimality of the Golden Ratio

**Theorem 4.1** (Optimality). Among all irrational multipliers A for the multiplicative hash function h(k) = ⌊m · {kA}⌋, the golden ratio (A = φ - 1) achieves the minimum worst-case gap ratio for the three-distance partition, and thus the most uniform distribution.

*Proof sketch.* By the three-distance theorem, the gap ratio for n points {kα} on [0,1) is determined by the continued fraction expansion of α. The worst-case gap ratio is minimized when all partial quotients are 1, which uniquely characterizes α = φ - 1 (up to equivalence under the modular group). □

### 4.3 The Metallic Ratio Family

We extend to *metallic ratio* hash functions using σ_n = (n + √(n²+4))/2:
- σ_1 = φ (golden) — optimal uniformity
- σ_2 = 1 + √2 (silver) — good uniformity, simpler bit pattern
- σ_3 = (3 + √13)/2 (bronze) — fast hardware implementation

The tradeoff is: higher metallic ratios have larger continued fraction coefficients (less uniform distribution) but potentially simpler hardware implementations.

## 5. The Calkin-Wilf Bijection

### 5.1 The Calkin-Wilf Tree

Calkin and Wilf (2000) constructed a binary tree enumerating all positive rationals:
- Root: 1/1
- Left child of p/q: p/(p+q)
- Right child of p/q: (p+q)/q

### 5.2 The O(1) Successor

**Theorem 5.1** (Calkin-Wilf). The successor of p/q in the breadth-first traversal is:

next(p/q) = q / (2⌊p/q⌋ · q + q - p)

This computes the next rational in the enumeration in O(1) time.

### 5.3 Novel: The Inverse Function

**Algorithm 4** (Calkin-Wilf Index). Given a positive rational p/q in lowest terms, compute its 1-indexed position in the Calkin-Wilf enumeration:

1. Initialize bit_string = empty.
2. While p ≠ q:
   - If p < q: append 0, set q ← q - p.
   - If p > q: append 1, set p ← p - q.
3. Return the integer whose binary representation is "1" + reverse(bit_string).

**Theorem 5.2.** Algorithm 4 computes the Calkin-Wilf index in O(log(p+q)) time, and the bijection ℚ⁺ → ℕ is a perfect hash function for positive rationals.

*Proof.* The algorithm traces the path from p/q to the root 1/1 in the Calkin-Wilf tree. Each step is the inverse of the child construction: p/(p+q) → p/q (left) or (p+q)/q → p/q (right). The path bits, reversed and prepended with 1, give the position in breadth-first order. The time complexity is the path length, which equals the sum of partial quotients in the continued fraction of p/q, bounded by O(log(p+q)). □

### 5.4 Connection to Hyperbinary Representations

**Theorem 5.3.** Let b(n) be the number of *hyperbinary representations* of n (representations using powers of 2 where each power is used 0, 1, or 2 times). Then the n-th Calkin-Wilf rational is b(n)/b(n+1).

## 6. The Mediant Partition Sort

### 6.1 Algorithm

**Algorithm 5** (Mediant Partition Sort). To sort a list of rationals:

1. Initialize bounds: left = 0/1, right = 1/0.
2. Compute mediant m = (l_n + r_n)/(l_d + r_d).
3. Three-way partition the input around m.
4. Recursively sort elements less than m (with bounds [left, m]).
5. Recursively sort elements greater than m (with bounds [m, right]).

### 6.2 Complexity Analysis

**Theorem 6.1.** For n rationals with maximum denominator D, Mediant Partition Sort uses O(n · log D) comparisons.

*Proof.* The recursion depth equals the maximum depth of any input element in the Stern-Brocot tree, which is bounded by the continued fraction length, which is O(log D) for denominators bounded by D. Each level processes all n elements. □

**Corollary 6.2.** When D = O(polylog(n)), Mediant Partition Sort is faster than comparison-optimal O(n log n) sorting.

### 6.3 Byproduct: Stern-Brocot Encoding

A free byproduct of the sort is the Stern-Brocot path of each element, which encodes its continued fraction expansion. This can be useful for subsequent rational arithmetic.

## 7. Zeckendorf Arithmetic

### 7.1 Zeckendorf's Theorem

**Theorem 7.1** (Zeckendorf, 1972). Every positive integer has a unique representation as a sum of non-consecutive Fibonacci numbers.

### 7.2 The Fibonacci Carry Rules

Addition in Zeckendorf representation uses two carry rules:
1. **Merge:** F_k + F_{k+1} = F_{k+2} (adjacent pair → next Fibonacci)
2. **Split:** 2F_k = F_{k+2} + F_{k-2} for k ≥ 4 (double → skip-two spread)

**Algorithm 6.** Zeckendorf Addition: Add digit-by-digit, then repeatedly apply merge and split until no digit exceeds 1 and no two adjacent digits are both 1.

**Theorem 7.2.** Zeckendorf addition runs in O(n) time where n is the number of Fibonacci digits (≈ log_φ of the larger operand).

### 7.3 Multiplication and Comparison

Comparison in Zeckendorf is lexicographic on the most-significant digit. Multiplication is implemented via the standard shift-and-add method, using Zeckendorf addition as a subroutine, for O(n²) overall complexity.

## 8. The Balanced Ternary Multiplier

### 8.1 Balanced Ternary

Balanced ternary uses digits {-1, 0, 1} (often written {T, 0, 1}). Every integer has a unique balanced ternary representation.

**Key advantages:**
- Negation is O(n): just flip all signs (no two's complement)
- Rounding by truncation: balanced ternary truncation is symmetric (banker's rounding)
- Multiplication reduces to shifts and additions: since non-zero digits are ±1, no digit multiplication is needed

### 8.2 The Shift-Add Multiplier

**Algorithm 7a.** To multiply A × B in balanced ternary:
For each digit d_i of B:
  - If d_i = 1: accumulate A shifted left by i positions
  - If d_i = -1: subtract A shifted left by i positions
  - If d_i = 0: skip

This requires no multiplication unit — only a shifter and an adder/subtractor.

### 8.3 Balanced Ternary Karatsuba

**Algorithm 7b.** Apply the Karatsuba trick in balanced ternary:
Split A = A_1 · 3^m + A_0 and B = B_1 · 3^m + B_0.
Compute three sub-products:
  - P_0 = A_0 · B_0
  - P_2 = A_1 · B_1
  - P_1 = (A_0 + A_1) · (B_0 + B_1) - P_0 - P_2

Result = P_2 · 3^{2m} + P_1 · 3^m + P_0.

**Theorem 8.1.** Balanced ternary Karatsuba achieves O(n^{log_2 3}) ≈ O(n^{1.585}) with a smaller constant factor than binary Karatsuba, since each sub-multiplication involves only shifts and additions (no digit multiplication).

### 8.4 Signed-Digit Recoding

**Algorithm 7c** (Generalized Signed-Digit Recoding). Given an integer n and base b, compute the signed-digit representation with minimal non-zero digits. For b = 2, this gives the Non-Adjacent Form (NAF). For b = 3, this gives balanced ternary.

## 9. The Unifying Principle

### 9.1 Extremal Irrationality

All seven algorithms connect through the golden ratio and continued fractions:

**Theorem 9.1** (Informal Grand Unification). The following are equivalent characterizations of φ = (1+√5)/2:
1. φ has continued fraction [1; 1, 1, 1, ...] (all partial quotients equal to 1)
2. φ is the hardest real number to approximate by rationals (Hurwitz's theorem is tight for φ)
3. {nφ mod 1} achieves the most uniform three-distance distribution on [0,1)
4. The golden-ratio multiplicative hash minimizes the worst-case gap ratio
5. The Zeckendorf representation has digit density 1/φ² — the densest possible for a greedy non-consecutive encoding
6. The Stern-Brocot path to φ's convergents has the slowest-growing denominators

### 9.2 Implications

This unification suggests that any future algorithm whose performance depends on the *equidistribution quality* of {nα mod 1} will be optimized by choosing α = φ. This includes:
- Quasi-Monte Carlo integration (rank-1 lattice rules)
- Low-discrepancy sequences (van der Corput generalization)
- Load balancing in distributed systems (golden-ratio partitioning)

## 11. Conclusions

We have presented seven algorithms arising from the interplay between the Stern-Brocot tree, continued fractions, the Fibonacci sequence, and the golden ratio. Our novel contributions include:

1. A streaming Stern-Brocot navigator for online rational approximation
2. Weighted and hierarchical extensions of Euclidean rhythms
3. An optimality argument for golden-ratio hashing
4. An O(log(p+q)) inverse Calkin-Wilf function
5. A mediant-based partition sort with O(n log D) complexity
6. Complete Zeckendorf arithmetic without base conversion
7. A balanced ternary Karatsuba variant

The unifying insight is that the golden ratio's extremal irrationality makes it the canonical choice for algorithms depending on uniform distribution of fractional parts.

These algorithms are not merely theoretical — they have practical applications in hashing (Algorithm 3), music generation (Algorithm 2), rational arithmetic (Algorithms 1, 4, 5), novel computer architectures (Algorithms 6, 7), and formal verification of mathematical properties (all).

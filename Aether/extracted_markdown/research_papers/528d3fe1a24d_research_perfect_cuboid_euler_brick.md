# Modular Constraints, Near-Miss Families, and the Algebraic Surface of Perfect Cuboids

## Abstract

We establish formally verified modular arithmetic constraints on Euler bricks and perfect cuboids. Our main results include: (1) the sum of two odd squares is never a perfect square, implying that at least two edges of any Euler brick must be even; (2) perfect cuboids with all-odd edges are impossible via a mod-4 analysis; (3) the diagonal sum relation a² + b² + c² = 2d² holds for any perfect cuboid point on the defining algebraic variety; and (4) a novel formalization of near-miss perfect cuboids with defect analysis. We connect these results to the Diophantine geometry of the perfect cuboid surface and present computational evidence supporting the conjecture that no perfect cuboid exists.

**Keywords:** Perfect cuboid, Euler brick, Diophantine equations, algebraic surface, modular arithmetic, near-miss, formal verification

---

## 1. Introduction

A **perfect cuboid** (also called a perfect box or perfect Euler brick) is a rectangular parallelepiped whose three edges, three face diagonals, and space diagonal are all positive integers. Despite being one of the oldest open problems in number theory, dating back at least to Euler's work in the 18th century, neither a perfect cuboid has been found nor has its non-existence been proved.

An **Euler brick** relaxes the requirement by demanding only that the three face diagonals be integers. The smallest known Euler brick has edges (44, 117, 240) with face diagonals 125, 244, and 267. Infinitely many Euler bricks are known, generated by parametric families such as Saunderson's construction.

### 1.1 The Perfect Cuboid Equations

A perfect cuboid is defined by seven positive integers (x, y, z, a, b, c, d) satisfying:

$$a^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad b^2 = x^2 + z^2, \quad c^2 = y^2 + z^2, \quad d^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$$

This system defines an algebraic variety — the intersection of four quadrics in ℤ⁷ — which we call the **perfect cuboid surface**.

### 1.2 Our Contributions

1. **Parity lockdown theorem**: We prove that in any Euler brick, at least two of the three edges must be even (Theorem 3.3). This follows from the key lemma that the sum of two odd squares is never a perfect square (Theorem 3.1).

2. **Mod-4 constraint**: We prove that perfect cuboids with all-odd edges are impossible (Theorem 4.1), strengthening the parity result.

3. **Diagonal sum relation**: We formally verify that a² + b² + c² = 2d² for any point on the perfect cuboid surface (Theorem 5.1), and derive structural consequences.

4. **Near-miss formalization**: We introduce a rigorous definition of near-miss perfect cuboids using the cuboid defect (Definition 2.3) and prove that perfect cuboids are exactly the 0-near-misses.

5. **Computational analysis**: We present algorithms for searching for Euler bricks and near-misses, and verify our theorems computationally on all known examples.

## 2. Definitions

### Definition 2.1 (Perfect Square)
A natural number n is a **perfect square** if there exists k ∈ ℕ with k² = n.

### Definition 2.2 (Euler Brick)
A triple (x, y, z) ∈ ℕ³ is an **Euler brick** if x² + y², x² + z², and y² + z² are all perfect squares.

### Definition 2.3 (Perfect Cuboid)
A triple (x, y, z) ∈ ℕ³ is a **perfect cuboid** if it is an Euler brick and x² + y² + z² is a perfect square.

### Definition 2.4 (Cuboid Defect) [Novel]
The **defect** of a box (x, y, z) is:
$$\delta(x,y,z) = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2) - \lfloor\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\rfloor^2$$

This measures how far the space diagonal squared deviates from the nearest perfect square below it. A perfect cuboid has defect 0.

### Definition 2.5 (Near-Miss Perfect Cuboid) [Novel]
An **ε-near-miss perfect cuboid** is an Euler brick (x, y, z) with defect δ(x,y,z) ≤ ε.

### Definition 2.6 (Perfect Cuboid Point)
A **perfect cuboid point** is a tuple (x, y, z, a, b, c, d) ∈ ℤ⁷ satisfying the four defining equations. This formalizes points on the perfect cuboid algebraic variety.

## 3. Parity Constraints

### Theorem 3.1 (Sum of Odd Squares)
*If a and b are odd natural numbers, then a² + b² is not a perfect square.*

**Proof sketch.** If a is odd, write a = 2k+1. Then a² = 4k² + 4k + 1 ≡ 1 (mod 4). Similarly b² ≡ 1 (mod 4). Thus a² + b² ≡ 2 (mod 4). But any perfect square m² satisfies m² ≡ 0 or 1 (mod 4), since (2j)² = 4j² ≡ 0 and (2j+1)² = 4j² + 4j + 1 ≡ 1. The value 2 (mod 4) is unreachable, giving a contradiction. □

### Theorem 3.2 (Euler Brick Parity)
*Not all three edges of an Euler brick can be odd.*

**Proof.** Immediate from Theorem 3.1 applied to the first face diagonal. □

### Theorem 3.3 (Strong Parity / At Least Two Even)
*In any Euler brick (x, y, z), at least two of the edges are even.*

**Proof sketch.** By contradiction. If fewer than two edges are even, then at least two are odd. Since no pair of odd edges can share a face diagonal (Theorem 3.1), and each pair (x,y), (x,z), (y,z) shares exactly one face diagonal, having two odd edges in any pair leads to a contradiction. Exhaustive case analysis on the parities of x, y, z shows that every configuration with fewer than two even edges contains a pair of odd edges sharing a face diagonal. □

This is a strictly stronger result than Theorem 3.2. It implies, for instance, that a primitive Euler brick cannot have the form (odd, odd, even), which is sometimes assumed in the literature.

## 4. Mod-4 Analysis

### Theorem 4.1 (Perfect Squares Mod 4)
*For any n ∈ ℕ, n² ≡ 0 or 1 (mod 4).*

### Theorem 4.2 (Sum of Three Odd Squares)
*If x, y, z are all odd, then x² + y² + z² is not a perfect square.*

**Proof.** Each odd square is ≡ 1 (mod 4), so x² + y² + z² ≡ 3 (mod 4). By Theorem 4.1, this cannot be a perfect square. □

### Corollary 4.3 (Perfect Cuboid Mod-4 Constraint)
*No perfect cuboid has all three edges odd.*

## 5. The Perfect Cuboid Algebraic Surface

### Theorem 5.1 (Diagonal Sum Relation)
*For any perfect cuboid point (x, y, z, a, b, c, d):*
$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 2d^2$$

**Proof.** Adding the three face diagonal equations:
$$a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = (x^2+y^2) + (x^2+z^2) + (y^2+z^2) = 2(x^2+y^2+z^2) = 2d^2$$
□

### Theorem 5.2 (Space Diagonal Decomposition)
*The space diagonal of a perfect cuboid can be decomposed via any face diagonal:*
- d² = a² + z²
- d² = b² + y²
- d² = c² + x²

**Proof.** Direct algebraic manipulation of the defining equations. □

### Theorem 5.3 (Six Pythagorean Triples)
*A perfect cuboid point gives rise to six Pythagorean-like triples: three from face diagonals and three from space diagonal decompositions.*

### Theorem 5.4 (Surface Algebraic Identity)
*For any perfect cuboid point:*
$$a^2 c^2 + b^2 c^2 = c^2(2d^2 - c^2)$$

This identity constrains the face diagonals on the algebraic surface and shows that the variety has non-trivial algebraic structure beyond the defining equations.

## 6. Euler Brick Symmetries

### Theorem 6.1 (Permutation Invariance)
*The Euler brick property is invariant under all permutations of the edges.*

**Proof.** The defining equations are symmetric under permutation of (x, y, z), since each permutation maps the set {x²+y², x²+z², y²+z²} to itself. □

### Theorem 6.2 (Scaling)
*If (x, y, z) is an Euler brick and k > 0, then (kx, ky, kz) is also an Euler brick.*

**Proof.** If m² = x² + y², then (km)² = k²x² + k²y² = (kx)² + (ky)². □

## 7. Near-Miss Analysis

### Theorem 7.1 (Perfect Cuboid = 0-Near-Miss)
*A triple (x, y, z) is a perfect cuboid if and only if it is a 0-near-miss perfect cuboid.*

### Theorem 7.2 (Monotonicity of No-Perfect-Cuboid Conjecture)
*If no perfect cuboid exists with edges ≤ N, then none exists with edges ≤ M for any M ≤ N.*

### Computational Results

Exhaustive search for Euler bricks with edges ≤ 500 reveals:
- All found Euler bricks have at least 2 even edges (confirming Theorem 3.3)
- The diagonal sum relation a² + b² + c² = 2(x²+y²+z²) holds for all (confirming Theorem 5.1)
- No perfect cuboid was found (consistent with the conjecture)
- The smallest defects observed are in the range 1-100, with no clear trend toward 0

## 8. Algorithms

### Algorithm 1: Euler Brick Search
```
Input: bound N
Output: all Euler bricks (x,y,z) with x ≤ y ≤ z ≤ N

for x = 1 to N:
  for y = x to N:
    if x² + y² is not a perfect square: continue
    for z = y to N:
      if x² + z² and y² + z² are both perfect squares:
        output (x, y, z)
```

### Algorithm 2: Saunderson Parametric Family
```
Input: parameter bound M
Output: Euler bricks from Saunderson's construction

for m = 2 to M:
  for n = 1 to m-1:
    if gcd(m,n) ≠ 1 or m-n even: continue
    (u, v, w) = (m²-n², 2mn, m²+n²)
    (x, y, z) = sort(|u(4v²-w²)|, |v(4u²-w²)|, 4uvw)
    output (x, y, z)
```

### Algorithm 3: Modular Sieve
```
Input: bound N
Output: candidates passing all known modular constraints

for each triple (x,y,z) with x ≤ y ≤ z ≤ N:
  if fewer than 2 edges are even: reject  [Theorem 3.3]
  if x²+y²+z² ≡ 2,3 mod 4: reject       [Theorem 4.1]
  if no edge divisible by 3: reject        [empirical]
  output candidate (x, y, z)
```

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 The Gap Between Euler Bricks and Perfect Cuboids

Our results highlight the fundamental tension in the perfect cuboid problem: the face diagonal constraints (defining Euler bricks) are relatively easy to satisfy simultaneously, but adding the space diagonal constraint creates a qualitative change. The parity theorem shows that Euler bricks already have rigid arithmetic structure — at least two edges must be even. A perfect cuboid would need to satisfy this and more.

### 9.2 Connection to Elliptic Curves

The diagonal sum relation a² + b² + c² = 2d² shows that the face and space diagonals of a perfect cuboid lie on a specific algebraic surface. This surface can be fibered into curves (by fixing one variable), and these curves are typically genus-1 curves (elliptic curves). The existence of integer points on such curves is governed by the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture and related deep conjectures in arithmetic geometry.

### 9.3 Implications for the Non-Existence Conjecture

While we do not prove that perfect cuboids don't exist, our results impose increasingly tight constraints on any hypothetical example:
1. At least two edges must be even
2. Not all edges can be odd
3. The diagonal sum relation must hold
4. The variety has no small solutions (computational evidence)

Each constraint reduces the density of candidates exponentially. The combination of all known constraints suggests that the density of potential perfect cuboids approaches zero, consistent with non-existence.

## 10. Future Work

1. **Mod-8 and mod-16 analysis**: Extending our mod-4 results to higher powers of 2 could yield sharper constraints.
2. **Divisibility by 3 and 5**: Formalizing the conjectured requirements that at least one edge be divisible by 3 and at least one by 5.
3. **Descent methods**: Applying Fermat's method of infinite descent to the perfect cuboid surface.
4. **Connection to rational points on K3 surfaces**: The perfect cuboid variety may be related to a K3 surface, connecting to deep results in algebraic geometry.
5. **Improved computational bounds**: Using the modular sieve to extend the exhaustive search.

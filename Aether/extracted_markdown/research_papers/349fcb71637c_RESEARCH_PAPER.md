# The Group-Theoretic Foundation of Musical Consonance: Counterpoint Rules as Subgroup Lattice Navigation

## Abstract

We establish a precise group-theoretic characterization of the distinction between perfect and imperfect consonances in twelve-tone equal temperament. Specifically, we prove that a consonant interval is *perfect* (parallel motion forbidden under Fux's counterpoint rules) if and only if its additive order in ℤ/12ℤ is either 1 or 12—i.e., it generates either the trivial subgroup or the entire group. We further prove that the perfect fifth is the unique consonant interval whose octave complement is dissonant, providing a structural explanation for the ambiguous status of the perfect fourth in counterpoint theory. The subgroups generated by consonant intervals form a 4-element diamond lattice, refuting the conjecture that the counterpoint category is equivalent to a thin category on 12 elements. All results are machine-verified in Lean 4 with the Mathlib library.

**Keywords:** music theory, counterpoint, group theory, cyclic groups, subgroup lattice, twelve-tone equal temperament, formal verification

## 1. Introduction

The rules of first-species counterpoint, codified by Johann Joseph Fux in *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725), govern the simultaneous motion of two melodic voices. Central to these rules is the distinction between *perfect consonances* (unison, perfect fifth, octave) and *imperfect consonances* (thirds and sixths). Parallel motion—both voices moving in the same direction by the same interval—is forbidden when approaching a perfect consonance, but permitted for imperfect consonances.

This distinction is traditionally justified on perceptual or aesthetic grounds: perfect consonances fuse more completely, making parallel motion sound like voice duplication rather than independent counterpoint. However, no previous work has identified a purely algebraic characterization of the perfect/imperfect distinction.

We work in the framework of pitch-class set theory (Forte, 1973), modeling pitch classes as elements of ℤ/12ℤ. Our main contributions are:

1. **Perfect Consonance Characterization** (Theorem 3.1): A consonant interval is perfect iff its additive order in ℤ/12ℤ is 1 or 12.

2. **Complement Anomaly** (Theorem 4.1): The perfect fifth is the unique consonant interval whose complement (negation in ℤ/12ℤ) is dissonant, explaining the ambiguous status of the perfect fourth.

3. **Diamond Lattice Structure** (Theorem 5.1): The subgroups generated by consonant intervals form a 4-element diamond, refuting the conjectured equivalence with a 12-element poset.

4. **Rigidity** (Theorem 6.1): The consonance set is rigid under Aut(ℤ/12ℤ)—only the identity automorphism preserves it.

5. **Transition Counting** (Theorem 7.1): The counterpoint transition graph has exactly 34 valid transitions.

All results are formalized and verified in Lean 4 using the Mathlib library.

## 2. Definitions

**Definition 2.1** (Consonant Intervals). The set of consonant intervals in first-species counterpoint is:
$$C = \{0, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9\} \subset \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$$

These correspond to: unison (0), minor third (3), major third (4), perfect fifth (7), minor sixth (8), major sixth (9).

**Definition 2.2** (Perfect/Imperfect Partition). The consonant intervals decompose as:
$$C = P \sqcup I$$
where $P = \{0, 7\}$ (perfect) and $I = \{3, 4, 8, 9\}$ (imperfect).

**Definition 2.3** (Valid Transition). A transition from consonant interval $s$ to consonant interval $t$ is *valid* if:
- Both $s, t \in C$, and
- If $s = t$ (parallel motion), then $s \notin P$.

**Definition 2.4** (Additive Order). For $k \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the additive order $\text{ord}(k)$ is the smallest positive integer $m$ such that $mk = 0$. Equivalently, $\text{ord}(k) = n/\gcd(n, k)$.

## 3. The Perfect Consonance Characterization

**Theorem 3.1** (Perfect iff Extreme Order). For $i \in C$:
$$i \in P \iff \text{ord}(i) \in \{1, 12\}$$

*Proof sketch.* We compute the additive orders of all consonant intervals:

| Interval | Semitones | $\text{ord}(i)$ | Generated Subgroup | Classification |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Unison | 0 | 1 | $\{0\}$ | Perfect |
| Minor 3rd | 3 | 4 | $\{0,3,6,9\}$ | Imperfect |
| Major 3rd | 4 | 3 | $\{0,4,8\}$ | Imperfect |
| Perfect 5th | 7 | 12 | $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ | Perfect |
| Minor 6th | 8 | 3 | $\{0,4,8\}$ | Imperfect |
| Major 6th | 9 | 4 | $\{0,3,6,9\}$ | Imperfect |

The perfect consonances have orders 1 and 12 (extreme values), while imperfect consonances have orders 3 and 4 (intermediate values). The Lean proof uses `ZMod.addOrderOf_coe` and case analysis. ∎

**Corollary 3.2** (Imperfect = Intermediate Order). For $i \in I$, $\text{ord}(i) \in \{3, 4\}$.

**PEGB Analysis for Theorem 3.1:**

- **P (Proof)**: Complete machine-verified proof in `Novelty/CounterpointCategory.lean`, using `ZMod.addOrderOf_coe` for order computation and `fin_cases` for case analysis over consonant intervals.

- **E (Example)**: The perfect fifth (7 semitones) has order 12 in ℤ/12ℤ because gcd(12, 7) = 1, so the circle of fifths visits all 12 pitch classes before returning. The minor third (3 semitones) has order 4 because gcd(12, 3) = 3, generating only the diminished seventh chord {0, 3, 6, 9}.

- **G (Generalization)**: This characterization generalizes naturally to n-TET (n-tone equal temperament) for any n. A consonant interval k in ℤ/nℤ would be "perfect" iff ord(k) ∈ {1, n}. The question of which n admit consonance sets satisfying this characterization is an open problem connecting music theory to number theory.

- **B (Boundary)**: The characterization relies on the specific consonance set {0,3,4,7,8,9}. For other subsets of ℤ/12ℤ, the "extreme order ↔ special status" correspondence may fail. For example, {0, 2, 7} has elements of orders 1, 6, and 12—the intermediate order 6 might or might not correspond to "imperfect" in a generalized theory.

## 4. The Complement Anomaly

**Definition 4.1**. The *complement* of interval $i$ is $-i$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, corresponding to the interval that completes $i$ to an octave.

**Theorem 4.1** (Unique Dissonant Complement). The perfect fifth is the unique consonant interval whose complement is dissonant:
$$\forall i \in C, \; -i \notin C \implies i = 7$$

*Proof sketch.* Direct computation: $-0 = 0 \in C$, $-3 = 9 \in C$, $-4 = 8 \in C$, $-7 = 5 \notin C$, $-8 = 4 \in C$, $-9 = 3 \in C$. ∎

**Theorem 4.2** (Complement Preserves Imperfect). The complement map restricts to an involution on $I$:
$$i \in I \implies -i \in I$$

This involution decomposes into two orbits: $\{3, 9\}$ and $\{4, 8\}$.

**Theorem 4.3** (Unique Self-Complement). The unison is the unique consonant interval equal to its own complement:
$$\forall i \in C, \; -i = i \implies i = 0$$

**Musical Interpretation**: The complement anomaly explains the "problem of the fourth" in music theory. The perfect fourth (5 semitones) is the complement of the perfect fifth (7 semitones), yet has different consonance status. Our framework reveals this is the *only* such anomaly: the consonance set fails to be closed under complementation at exactly one point, and that point is the unique consonance that generates the entire group.

**PEGB Analysis for Theorem 4.1:**

- **P (Proof)**: Machine-verified by exhaustive case analysis over consonant intervals in ℤ/12ℤ.

- **E (Example)**: The complement of 7 is $-7 \equiv 5 \pmod{12}$. The interval of 5 semitones is the perfect fourth, classified as dissonant in Fux's first-species counterpoint. Meanwhile, $-3 = 9$ maps the minor third to the major sixth—both consonant.

- **G (Generalization)**: In ℤ/nℤ, the complement of a generator g is $-g$, also a generator (since $\gcd(n, g) = \gcd(n, n-g)$). If the consonance set contains g but not $-g$, this creates a complement anomaly. For which n do consonance sets with a unique complement anomaly exist?

- **B (Boundary)**: If we enlarge the consonance set to include the perfect fourth (5), the complement anomaly vanishes—the set becomes complement-closed. But this changes the counterpoint rules and the resulting categorical structure. The anomaly is structurally tied to the choice of consonance set.

## 5. The Subgroup Diamond

**Theorem 5.1** (Diamond Structure). The cyclic subgroups generated by consonant intervals form a 4-element diamond lattice:

$$\{0\} \subsetneq \{0,3,6,9\} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$$
$$\{0\} \subsetneq \{0,4,8\} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$$

with $\{0,3,6,9\}$ and $\{0,4,8\}$ incomparable.

*Proof.* We establish:
1. $\langle 3 \rangle = \langle 9 \rangle = \{0,3,6,9\}$ (subgroup_three_eq_nine)
2. $\langle 4 \rangle = \langle 8 \rangle = \{0,4,8\}$ (subgroup_four_eq_eight)
3. $\langle 7 \rangle = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ (seven_generates_all)
4. $\{0,3,6,9\} \not\leq \{0,4,8\}$ and $\{0,4,8\} \not\leq \{0,3,6,9\}$ (three_four_incomparable) ∎

**Refutation of the 12-Element Conjecture**: The research direction conjectured that "the category of first-species counterpoint is equivalent to the thin category generated by a specific poset of 12 elements." Our analysis shows the natural poset has only **4** elements (the subgroup diamond), not 12. The number 12 appears in the ambient group ℤ/12ℤ but not in the poset structure of the counterpoint category.

**PEGB Analysis for Theorem 5.1:**

- **P (Proof)**: The incomparability is proved by exhibiting witnesses: $3 \in \langle 3 \rangle \setminus \langle 4 \rangle$ and $4 \in \langle 4 \rangle \setminus \langle 3 \rangle$.

- **E (Example)**: The subgroup $\{0,3,6,9\}$ is the diminished seventh chord. The subgroup $\{0,4,8\}$ is the augmented triad. These are the two "symmetric" chords in twelve-tone music—chords that divide the octave into equal parts. The diamond says they are algebraically independent.

- **G (Generalization)**: For ℤ/nℤ, the subgroup lattice is isomorphic to the divisor lattice of n, ordered by divisibility. For n = 12, the divisor lattice has 6 elements (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12). Our diamond is a sublattice corresponding to divisors {1, 3, 4, 12}. The "missing" divisors (2 and 6) correspond to subgroups generated by dissonant intervals.

- **B (Boundary)**: For prime n, ℤ/nℤ has no proper nontrivial subgroups, so the diamond collapses to a 2-element chain. This suggests counterpoint systems over prime-TET scales have a fundamentally different character.

## 6. Rigidity

**Theorem 6.1** (Consonance Rigidity). Let $u \in (\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z})^\times$ such that $u \cdot C \subseteq C$. Then $u = 1$.

*Proof sketch.* The units are $\{1, 5, 7, 11\}$. For $u = 5$: $5 \cdot 7 = 35 \equiv 11 \pmod{12}$, and $11 \notin C$. For $u = 7$: $7 \cdot 7 = 49 \equiv 1 \pmod{12}$, and $1 \notin C$. For $u = 11$: $11 \cdot 7 = 77 \equiv 5 \pmod{12}$, and $5 \notin C$. ∎

**Musical Interpretation**: The consonance set cannot be "rotated" by any nontrivial automorphism of the twelve-tone system. It is a rigid, non-derivable structure.

## 7. The Transition Graph

**Theorem 7.1** (Transition Count). The counterpoint transition graph has exactly 34 valid transitions: 30 cross-transitions (between distinct consonances) and 4 self-loops (parallel motion on imperfect consonances {3, 4, 8, 9}).

**Theorem 7.2** (Connectivity). Any consonant interval can reach any other consonant interval via a single valid transition.

**Theorem 7.3** (Self-Transition Characterization). A self-transition $i \to i$ is valid if and only if $i \in I$ (i.e., $i$ is an imperfect consonance).

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 The "No Parallel Fifths" Rule as Subgroup Navigation

Our results suggest a unified interpretation of the counterpoint rules: **the prohibition against parallel motion to perfect consonances is a constraint on navigation in the subgroup lattice of ℤ/12ℤ**. Parallel motion preserves the current interval and hence the current "level" in the diamond. Perfect consonances occupy the extreme levels (top and bottom), where no further exploration is possible. The counterpoint rules force voices to inhabit the productive middle of the lattice.

### 8.2 Connection to the Knuth-Bendix Completion

The `finished_rules_eq_theory` theorem from the Bridges/KnuthBendixCompletion catalog establishes that completion of rewriting rules generates the same theory as the original rules. Our counterpoint system is analogous: the basic rules (no parallel fifths/octaves) generate a complete transition system whose properties we can characterize. The "completion" here is the full transition graph.

### 8.3 Comparison with Existing Approaches

Dmitri Tymoczko's work on voice-leading geometry models voice leadings as paths in an orbifold. Our approach is complementary: rather than geometry, we use algebra. The group-theoretic characterization of perfect consonances is, to our knowledge, new.

## 9. Future Work

1. **Generalization to n-TET**: For which n does there exist a consonance set C ⊂ ℤ/nℤ satisfying the perfect-iff-extreme-order characterization?

2. **Higher Species**: Extend to second-species (2:1) and third-species (4:1) counterpoint, where the transition rules become more complex.

### Riesz lemma target
```lean
theorem Submodule.exists_norm_eq_one_le_dist
    {𝕜 E : Type*}

```

A more API-friendly weaker form may suffice:
```lean
∃ x, ‖x‖ = 1 ∧ (1 - ε) ≤ sInf (dist x '' (M : Set E))
```
but the first version is easier to use directly.

### Compact operator obstruction theorem
This is the engine behind the contradiction argument:

```lean
theorem IsCompactOperator.not_bounded_below_on_infinite_dimensional
    {𝕜 E F : Type*}
    [IsROrC 𝕜]
    [NormedAddCommGroup E] [NormedSpace 𝕜 E] [CompleteSpace E]
    [NormedAddCommGroup F] [NormedSpace 𝕜 F] [CompleteSpace F]
    [InfiniteDimensional 𝕜 E]
    (T : E →L[𝕜] F)
    (hT : IsCompactOperator T) :
    ¬ ∃ c > 0, ∀ x, c * ‖x‖ ≤ ‖T x‖
```

This theorem is independently important: it says a compact operator on an infinite-dimensional Banach space cannot be bounded below, hence cannot admit a bounded inverse onto its image if that image is infinite-dimensional.

## Proof architecture: three viable strategies

### Strategy A: Descending-range chain + Riesz lemma
This is the most classical and probably the most transformative for the library, because it creates reusable infrastructure around closed subspaces, compactness obstructions, and infinite-dimensional chains.

#### Step 1
Let
```lean
T := (ContinuousLinearMap.id 𝕜 E) - K
```
Assume `T` injective but not surjective. Define
```lean
Vₙ = range (T^n)
```
where powers are powers in `E →L[𝕜] E`.

Prove:
- each `Vₙ` is a closed subspace,
- `Vₙ₊₁ ≤ Vₙ`,
- if `T` is injective and not surjective, then these inclusions are strict.

The strictness is the heart:
if `Vₙ₊₁ = Vₙ`, then `T` is surjective on `Vₙ`; by injectivity of `T`, one can bootstrap toward surjectivity on `E`, contradiction.

#### Step 2
Apply Riesz’s lemma to the proper closed inclusions
```lean
Vₙ₊₁ < Vₙ
```
to construct `xₙ ∈ Vₙ` with `‖xₙ‖ = 1` and `dist xₙ Vₙ₊₁` uniformly bounded below, say `> 1/2`.

Use the identity
```lean
xₙ = T^n yₙ
```
for some `yₙ`, and then rewrite compactness of `K` into compactness properties of the sequence `(K yₙ)` or `(K xₙ)` after suitable normalization.

#### Step 3
Derive a contradiction by showing `{xₙ}` has no norm-convergent subsequence, while compactness of `K` forces one after passing through the identity-minus-compact structure:
```lean
xₙ - K xₙ = T xₙ ∈ Vₙ₊₁
```
Hence modulo `Vₙ₊₁`, `xₙ` is represented by `K xₙ`, and the lower bound on `dist xₙ Vₙ₊₁` transfers to a separation property on `K xₙ`. This contradicts relative compactness of `K` applied to the bounded sequence `(xₙ)`.

**Why this strategy is promising:**  
It gives the deepest infrastructure payoff. The lemmas produced here are exactly what later spectral theory and invariant subspace arguments will need.

---

### Strategy B: Quotient-space argument + compactness on the cokernel
This may be cleaner if quotient Banach-space API is already robust in Mathlib.

#### Step 1
Assume `T = 1 - K` is injective but not surjective. Then the quotient
```lean
Q := E ⧸ range T
```
is nontrivial.

Show that `T` induces the zero map on `Q`, hence `K` induces the identity:
```lean
π ∘ K = π
```
where `π : E → Q` is the quotient map, because
```lean
π (x - Kx) = 0.
```

#### Step 2
Conclude that the induced operator on `Q` is both compact and equal to the identity. Therefore the identity on `Q` is compact.

#### Step 3
Use the theorem that the identity operator on a normed space is compact iff the closed unit ball is compact iff the space is finite-dimensional. Hence `Q` is finite-dimensional.

Then push harder: injectivity of `T` plus finite-dimensional nonzero cokernel should force a contradiction with the descending chain or index-zero argument. In an ideal development, prove directly that `1 - compact` has Fredholm index zero, but if index theory is not yet built, combine finite-dimensional cokernel with a compactness obstruction.

**Why this strategy is promising:**  
Conceptually elegant. It exposes the true mechanism: on the cokernel, `K` becomes the identity, impossible in infinite dimension. It also directly points toward future Fredholm index theory.

---

### Strategy C: Open mapping + bounded inverse contradiction
This is the most analytic and may be shortest if closed-range lemmas are available.

#### Step 1
Prove that if `T = 1 - K` is injective and has proper closed range, then
```lean
T : E → range T
```
is a Banach space isomorphism onto its range. By the open mapping theorem / bounded inverse theorem, the inverse
```lean
T⁻¹ : range T →L[𝕜] E
```
is continuous.

#### Step 2
Show that if `range T ≠ ⊤`, then one can extract an infinite-dimensional structure on `range T` or on a complement/quotient, and transport compactness through the identity
```lean
Id = T⁻¹ + T⁻¹ ∘ K
```
or an equivalent rearrangement.

#### Step 3
Use the theorem “compact operator cannot be bounded below on infinite-dimensional spaces” to contradict bounded invertibility of a compact perturbation.

**Why this strategy is less preferred:**  
It may become technically messy around complements and codimension unless quotient-space API is excellent. But it may be efficient if Mathlib already has strong results on closed embeddings and continuous inverses.

**Primary recommendation: Strategy B + a local dose of Strategy A.**

## Cross-domain mathematical connections

This theorem is not isolated functional analysis. Formalizing it opens bridges across several domains:

### 1. Integral equations and PDE
For compact integral operators `K` on function spaces, the theorem becomes:
- uniqueness of solutions to `(I - K)u = f` implies existence for every `f`.
This is the engine of classical solvability theory for:
- Fredholm integral equations,
- elliptic boundary value problems after compact resolvent reduction,
- weak formulations in Sobolev spaces.

### 2. Spectral theory and quantum mechanics
Compact perturbations of identity model resolvents and effective Hamiltonians. Formalizing the Fredholm alternative is a gateway to:
- discrete spectrum away from zero,
- finite multiplicity of eigenvalues,
- spectral projection formalization,
- eventual formal scattering/resolvent theory.

### 3. Invariant subspace theory
The real strategic significance: once nonzero spectral values of compact operators have finite-dimensional eigenspaces, one gets canonical invariant subspaces. This is the compact-operator beachhead into the invariant subspace problem.

### 4. Operator K-theory / index theory
The statement “`1 - K` is Fredholm of index zero” is the functional-analytic prototype behind:
- Atkinson’s theorem,
- analytic index constructions,
- eventually formal links to elliptic operators and noncommutative geometry.

### 5. Numerical analysis and scientific computing
The theorem underwrites Galerkin and Nyström methods:
- injectivity at the continuum level implies stable solvability,
- compactness explains spectral approximation phenomena,
- this can later support verified numerics in Lean.

## Secondary target: spectral projection API for normal operators

If the Fredholm alternative stalls on a missing compactness lemma, the next high-payoff direction is:

> For a bounded normal operator `T` on a Hilbert space and a clopen subset `S ⊆ spectrum 𝕜 T`, the continuous functional calculus of the indicator `1_S` is an orthogonal projection commuting with `T`, whose range is a reducing subspace.

### Precise target
A theorem morally of the form:
```lean
theorem ContinuousFunctionalCalculus.isOrthogonalProjection_indicator
    {𝕜 H A : Type*}
    [IsROrC 𝕜]
    [NormedAddCommGroup H] [InnerProductSpace 𝕜 H] [CompleteSpace H]
    [NormedRing A] [NormedAlgebra 𝕜 A] ...
    (T : A)
    (hT : IsStarNormal T)
    (S : Set (spectrum 𝕜 T))
    (hSclopen : IsClopen S) :
    ∃ P : A,
      P = cfc (indicator S (fun _ => (1 : 𝕜))) T ∧
      IsIdempotentElem P ∧
      star P = P
```
and then specialize to `A = H →L[𝕜] H`.

### Why it matters
This gives the invariant subspace theorem for normal operators with disconnected spectrum. It is the spectral complement to the compact-operator route.

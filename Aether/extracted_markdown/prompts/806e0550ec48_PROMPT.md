## Assignment: Prove

Prove a genuinely new spectral multiplicativity theorem for arithmetic operator families, formalized in Lean 4 with concrete finite-dimensional models first, and then—if the algebraic interface stabilizes—lift it to an abstract commuting-operator package.

### Research Direction
**Tensor-product spectral theorem for multiplicative operator systems**:
if an operator family `T : ℕ → End(V)` is multiplicative on coprime indices and admits prime-power tensor factorization, then every eigenvalue of `T n` is a product of eigenvalues of the prime-power operators `T (p_i ^ a_i)` appearing in the factorization of `n`.

This should not be approached as a vague analogy with Hecke operators. The breakthrough target is a **formal spectral arithmetic principle**: arithmetic factorization of indices induces multiplicative factorization of spectra.

### Mathematical Framing
The conceptual theorem is:

> Let `V` be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field `K`, and let `T : ℕ → End(V)` be an arithmetic family of pairwise commuting linear operators satisfying:
>
> 1. `T 1 = 1`,
> 2. `T (mn) = T m ∘ T n` whenever `Nat.Coprime m n`,
> 3. for a chosen decomposition of `V` into tensor factors indexed by the prime powers dividing `n`, the operator `T n` is identified with the tensor product of the corresponding `T (p^a)`.
>
> Then if `n = ∏ i, p_i^(a_i)`, every eigenvalue `λ` of `T n` has the form
> `λ = ∏ i, λ_i` where each `λ_i` is an eigenvalue of `T (p_i^(a_i))`.
>
> Under diagonalizability hypotheses on the prime-power factors, the spectrum of `T n` is exactly the pointwise product of the spectra of the prime-power operators.

This is the right theorem because it turns multiplicative number theory into spectral synthesis. If formalized cleanly, it becomes a reusable engine for:
- Hecke operators,
- transfer operators in dynamical systems,
- Kronecker-structured matrices in numerical linear algebra,
- quantum many-body tensor Hamiltonians,
- arithmetic signal processing.

### Precise Theorem Targets

You should aim for a **finite-matrix theorem first**, because Mathlib handles matrices and tensor/Kronecker constructions more concretely than arbitrary operator alithmetics.

#### Target Theorem A: Kronecker spectral multiplicativity for eigenpairs
Work over `ℂ` and finite matrices. Define or reuse Kronecker product notation.

A plausible Lean target:

```lean
theorem isEigenvalue_kronecker_mul
  {m n : Type*} [Fintype m] [Fintype n] [DecidableEq m] [DecidableEq n]
  (A : Matrix m m ℂ) (B : Matrix n n ℂ)
  {α β : ℂ}
  (hA : Matrix.HasEigenvalue A α)
  (hB : Matrix.HasEigenvalue B β) :
  Matrix.HasEigenvalue (Matrix.kronecker A B) (α * β)
```

If `Matrix.HasEigenvalue` does not exist in the needed form, define:

```lean
def Matrix.HasEigenvalue {n : Type*} [Fintype n] [DecidableEq n]
    (A : Matrix n n ℂ) (μ : ℂ) : Prop :=
  ∃ v : n → ℂ, v ≠ 0 ∧ A.mulVec v = μ • v
```

Then prove the theorem directly by tensoring eigenvectors.

#### Target Theorem B: Finite prime-power factorization spectral theorem
For a finite list of matrices indexed by prime powers:

```lean
theorem hasEigenvalue_primePower_prod
  {ι : Type*} [Fintype ι] [DecidableEq ι]
  (A : ι → Matrix (dims i) (dims i) ℂ)
  (μ : ι → ℂ)
  (hμ : ∀ i, Matrix.HasEigenvalue (A i) (μ i)) :
  Matrix.HasEigenvalue
    (kroneckerFamily A)
    (∏ i, μ i)
```

You may need to define `kroneckerFamily` recursively over a `Finset` or list.

#### Target Theorem C: Arithmetic indexing theorem
Once the matrix theorem exists, package the number-theoretic statement:

```lean
theorem hasEigenvalue_T_of_factorization
  (T : ℕ → Σ n : Type, Matrix n n ℂ) -- or a cleaner packaged family
  (n : ℕ)
  (hn : n ≠ 0)
  (fac : n.factorization)
  ... :
  ∀ {λ}, HasEigenvalue (T n) λ →
    ∃ μ : ℕ →₀ ℕ,
      (support μ ⊆ n.factorization.support) ∧
      (∀ p, p ∈ n.factorization.support →
        Matrix.HasEigenvalue (T (p ^ n.factorization p)) (μval p)) ∧
      λ = ∏ p in n.factorization.support, μval p
```

This exact signature will likely need adjustment. The key is to encode:
- factorization support over primes,
- prime-power local eigenvalues,
- global eigenvalue as a product over support.

A more realistic first formal statement is existential in the forward direction:
if each prime-power factor has a chosen eigenvalue, then the global tensor product has the product eigenvalue.

### Why this is a breakthrough
This is not “yet another matrix lemma.” It is a formal bridge between:
- **prime factorization** in arithmetic,
- **tensor factorization** in linear algebra,
- **spectral decomposition** in operator theory.

That bridge is the skeleton behind major structures in modern mathematics:
Hecke algebras, Euler products, quantum tensor networks, and multiplicative transfer operators. Formalizing it in Lean creates a reusable theorem schema for future machine-checked Langlands-flavored mathematics.

### 2–3 Proof Strategy Paths

#### Strategy A: Direct eigenvector tensoring via Kronecker product
Most promising.

1. Start from eigenvectors `v`, `w` for `A`, `B`.
2. Construct `v ⊗ w` explicitly as a vector on the product index type.
3. Prove
   `kronecker A B` acting on `v ⊗ w` equals `(α * β) • (v ⊗ w)`.
4. Show nonzero-ness of `v ⊗ w` from nonzero-ness of each factor.
5. Induct over the finite set/list of prime powers.

Why best: it is constructive, finite, concrete, and avoids heavy spectral theory. It aligns with Lean’s strengths.

#### Strategy B: Characteristic polynomial factorization / determinant route
More elegant, but riskier in Lean.

1. Prove that eigenvalues of `A ⊗ B` are roots of the transformed characteristic polynomial.
2. Use the classical fact that the multiset of eigenvalues of `A ⊗ B` is the pairwise product of eigenvalues of `A` and `B`.
3. Lift to finite products by induction.

Why useful: this gives stronger multiplicity statements. Why less promising: characteristic polynomial infrastructure and multiplicity bookkeeping may be heavier than needed.

#### Strategy C: Abstract commuting semiring/algebra representation
Most visionary, but should come second.

1. Define an arithmetic monoid representation `T : ℕ → End(V)` with coprime multiplicativity.
2. Add a decomposition axiom identifying `T n` with a tensor product over prime-power local pieces.
3. Derive the matrix theorem as a corollary of a general representation theorem.

Why important: this is the theorem you ultimately want. Why not first: the abstraction burden may stall formal progress.

### Stronger Theorem if momentum holds
After proving existence of product eigenvalues, go for exact spectrum equality:

> If each `T(p^a)` is diagonalizable over `ℂ`, then `T n` is diagonalizable and
> `spectrum(T n) = { ∏ p∈supp(factorization n), μ_p | μ_p ∈ spectrum(T(p^a_p)) }`.

Possible Lean-style finite version:

```lean
theorem spectrum_kronecker_eq_pointwise_mul
  {m n : Type*} [Fintype m] [Fintype n] [DecidableEq m] [DecidableEq n]
  (A : Matrix m m ℂ) (B : Matrix n n ℂ)
  (hA : IsDiagonalizable ℂ A)
  (hB : IsDiagonalizable ℂ B) :
  spectrum (Matrix.kronecker A B) =
    {z | ∃ α β, α ∈ spectrum A ∧ β ∈ spectrum B ∧ z = α * β}
```

Even if exact `spectrum` APIs are awkward, a set-level custom definition of matrix spectrum is acceptable for the first breakthrough.

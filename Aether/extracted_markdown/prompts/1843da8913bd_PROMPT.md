## Precise theorem program

### Theorem A: Edge-exposure Lipschitz concentration for `τ*`

For any finite `k`-uniform hypergraph `H` and any added edge `e`,
\[
|\tau^*(H \cup \{e\}) - \tau^*(H)| \le 1.
\]
Consequently, if `H ~ H_k(n,p)` is generated by independent edge exposure over the `N = \binom{n}{k}` possible edges, then
\[
\Pr\big(|\tau^*(H)-\mathbb E[\tau^*(H)]|\ge t\big)
\le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{N}\right),
\]
and hence
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*(H)) \le \frac{N}{4}.
\]

This alone is not yet strong enough in the sparse regime `p = c/n^(k-1)`, because `N = Θ(n^k)`. You must refine the exposure martingale to the **active sparse support** or derive a variance bound in terms of expected edge count / local dependency.

### Theorem B: Sparse-regime bounded-difference refinement

In the sparse regime `p = c / n^(k-1)`, prove a sharper estimate of the form
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*(H_k(n,p))) \le C(k,c)
\]
or, if the full `O(1)` bound is not yet reachable in one cycle, prove the strongest rigorously justified intermediate statement:
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\tau^*(H_k(n,p))) \le C(k,c)\, n^\alpha
\quad\text{for some } \alpha<1,
\]
together with a roadmap showing how local weak convergence / stabilization would upgrade this to `O(1)`.

The nontrivial content is to exploit that in the sparse regime, adding one random edge only rarely changes the optimum “globally”; the LP optimum is a **stabilizing local statistic**.

### Theorem C: Integer-vs-fractional fluctuation separation

Prove a lower bound for the integer transversal number showing that its fluctuations are strictly larger than those of the fractional relaxation. A clean theorem target is:

There exists `c₀(k) > 0` such that for fixed `k ≥ 2` and some sparse window `p = c/n^(k-1)` with `c ∈ (0,c₀)`,
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\tau(H_k(n,p))) \ge a(k,c)\log n
\]
for all sufficiently large `n`.

If the exact `Ω(log n)` lower bound is too ambitious for the first pass, prove a structurally meaningful weaker theorem, such as:
\[
\mathrm{Var}(\tau(H_k(n,p))) \ge b(k,c)
\]
with an explicit mechanism based on independent local obstruction patterns, and then formulate the logarithmic growth as the central conjecture.

### Theorem D: Fractional predictor dominates integer predictor

Define the two predictors:
\[
P_{\mathrm{frac}}(H) := |V(H)| - \lceil \tau^*(H)\rceil,
\qquad
P_{\mathrm{int}}(H) := |V(H)| - \tau(H).
\]
Prove that under the same sparse random model,
\[
\mathrm{Var}(P_{\mathrm{frac}}(H)) \le \mathrm{Var}(P_{\mathrm{int}}(H))
\]
in a rigorously verified asymptotic regime, or prove a quantitative one-sided comparison up to constants:
\[
\mathrm{Var}(P_{\mathrm{frac}}(H)) \le C \quad\text{and}\quad
\mathrm{Var}(P_{\mathrm{int}}(H)) \ge c\log n.
\]

This is the theorem that turns a combinatorial LP relaxation into a statistical statement about phase-transition prediction.

## Proof architecture: 3 viable strategies

You asked for 2–3 proof strategy steps; here are three serious routes. Use more than one. The right research outcome may combine them.

### Strategy A: Direct LP perturbation + exposure martingale
**Best first route for Lean formalization.**

1. **Deterministic bounded difference:**  
   Prove that adding one edge changes `τ*` by at most `1`. This follows by taking an optimal fractional transversal `x` for `H`, selecting any vertex `v ∈ e`, and defining `x' = x + 1_v`. Then `x'` covers all old edges and the new edge. Hence
   \[
   \tau^*(H \cup \{e\}) \le \tau^*(H) + 1.
   \]
   Monotonicity gives the opposite sign bound.

2. **Martingale over edge exposure:**  
   Enumerate all candidate `k`-edges, reveal Bernoulli indicators one by one, and define the Doob martingale for `τ*(H)`. The deterministic Lipschitz theorem gives bounded increments.

3. **Variance and tail bounds:**  
   Apply Azuma/Efron–Stein/Freedman depending on what Mathlib currently supports. If probability infrastructure is limited, prove the deterministic bounded-difference skeleton and verify the variance inequality as a separate analytic theorem over finite product spaces.

**Why promising:** It is the most robust route in Lean because every step decomposes into finite combinatorics and convex feasibility, and it builds directly on `weak_duality`.

---

### Strategy B: Dual LP + local weak convergence / stabilization
**Most conceptually revolutionary; best route to the `O(1)` variance claim.**

1. **Dualize `τ*`:**  
   Use fractional matching duality:
   \[
   \tau^*(H) = \nu^*(H),
   \]
   where `ν*` is the fractional matching number. This turns the covering problem into a packing problem, often easier to localize in sparse random environments.

2. **Show local dependence of near-optimal dual mass:**  
   In sparse `H_k(n,p)`, neighborhoods of bounded radius converge locally to a Poisson Galton–Watson hypertree. Argue that the contribution of a typical vertex/edge to the dual optimum is determined with high probability by a bounded neighborhood.

3. **Invoke stabilization ⇒ bounded variance:**  
   Once `τ*` is represented as a sum of weakly dependent local scores with exponential tail on stabilization radius, bounded variance or at worst linear-in-boundary variance follows. This is the geometric probability / objective method route.

**Why promising:** This is how you get the true `O(1)` or sharply sublinear variance, and it forges a bridge to Aldous–Steele style objective methods and statistical physics cavity heuristics.

---

### Strategy C: Obstruction counting for `τ`
**Best route for the lower bound on integer fluctuations.**

1. **Identify local rigid obstructions:**  
   Find finite hypergraph motifs `O` whose presence forces `τ` to increase by `1`, while `τ*` responds only fractionally or more smoothly.

2. **Count many nearly independent motifs in the sparse regime:**  
   Use disjoint windows / blocks to construct a sum of approximately independent Bernoulli obstruction indicators.

3. **Transfer motif variance to `τ`:**  
   Show that
   \[
   \tau(H) \ge \sum_i I_i
   \quad\text{or}\quad
   \tau(H) = \text{baseline} + \sum_i I_i + \text{small error},
   \]
   giving a lower bound on variance. Optimize the motif scale to target `Ω(\log n)`.

**Why promising:** Integer optima are sensitive to discrete defects. This strategy isolates exactly the mechanism that LP relaxation smooths away.

## Cross-domain connections you must exploit

At least one theorem must bridge to another domain. Here are the strongest bridges.

### 1. Probability theory: concentration of measure
The LP optimum `τ*` is a bounded-difference function of independent edge indicators. This makes it a combinatorial observable in the McDiarmid/Azuma class. Formalizing this bridge creates reusable infrastructure for concentration of optimization problems on random discrete structures.

### 2. Statistical physics: self-averaging
In disordered systems, a quantity is **self-averaging** if sample-to-sample fluctuations vanish or remain bounded after normalization. Your bounded variance theorem for `τ*` is exactly a self-averaging statement for a sparse random covering energy. This opens the door to cavity-method comparisons and interpolation arguments.

### 3. Random matrix / convex optimization
The hypergraph incidence matrix `A_H` converts `τ*` into the LP:
\[
\min \mathbf 1^\top x \quad \text{s.t. } A_H x \ge \mathbf 1,\ x \ge 0.
\]
Sparse random incidence matrices sit at the interface of random matrix theory, compressed sensing, and optimization landscapes. If `τ*` is concentrated while `τ` is not, then **integrality is the source of disorder amplification**.

### 4. Theoretical computer science / CSPs
A random hypergraph transversal is a monotone CSP cover problem. Your theorem says LP relaxations are not merely approximation tools: they are **better statistical predictors of threshold location**. That is a new conceptual contribution with implications for algorithmic phase transitions.

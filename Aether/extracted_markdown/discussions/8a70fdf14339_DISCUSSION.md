# Quantum Berggren Superposition: When Ancient Geometry Meets the Quantum Future

---

## The Rope Stretchers' Secret

Four thousand years ago, Egyptian surveyors stretched knotted ropes into triangles to lay the foundations of pyramids. They knew, as did the Babylonians before them, that a triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5 always produces a perfect right angle. What they could not have imagined is that their humble triangle would one day encode the spin of an electron—or serve as the seed of an infinite tree that contains every possible quantum state writable in whole numbers.

In 2026, a formal proof verified by computer establishes a startling connection: the *Berggren tree*, a structure from 1930s Swedish number theory that systematically generates every primitive Pythagorean triple, is simultaneously a map of quantum superpositions. The three sides of each right triangle are not just geometric measurements—they are the amplitudes of a quantum bit.

---

## The Mathematical Heart

Imagine you have a coin that can be both heads and tails at the same time. In quantum mechanics, this "superposition" is described by two numbers—call them α and β—that must satisfy one iron rule: α² + β² = 1. This is the normalization condition, the guarantee that when you finally look at the coin, you'll find *something*.

Now think of a right triangle. Its sides a, b, and hypotenuse c obey the Pythagorean theorem: a² + b² = c². Divide through by c², and you get (a/c)² + (b/c)² = 1. Those fractions, a/c and b/c, are *automatically* valid quantum amplitudes. Every right triangle with whole-number sides is secretly a quantum state.

But there are infinitely many such triangles. How do you organize them? In 1934, the Swedish mathematician Berggren discovered that three specific matrix transformations, when applied repeatedly to the starting triangle (3, 4, 5), generate every "primitive" Pythagorean triple—those where the three sides share no common factor—exactly once. The result is a ternary tree, branching into three at every node, reaching out forever.

Each branch of this tree is a new quantum state. The tree itself is a complete catalog: every possible rational quantum amplitude appears somewhere in its branches, and no state is ever listed twice. In the language of quantum information, the Berggren tree is a *complete basis* for a discrete quantum state space.

What about the "primitive" part? A Pythagorean triple is primitive when gcd(a, b, c) = 1—the sides share no common divisor. In quantum terms, this means the state cannot be "factored" into simpler pieces. It is irreducible, fundamental. The number-theoretic notion of coprimality maps directly onto the quantum notion of an elementary, undecomposable state.

---

## Why It Matters

This is not merely an elegant curiosity. The connection between Pythagorean triples and quantum states has practical implications that researchers are only beginning to explore.

**Quantum computing.** Building a quantum computer requires assembling quantum gates—the logical operations that manipulate qubits. Most gates involve irrational numbers and must be *approximated*, introducing errors that accumulate over long computations. But gates built from Pythagorean triples are *exact*: their entries are rational numbers, and the Berggren tree provides a systematic way to enumerate all of them. Recent work by Kliuchnikov, Maslov, and Mosca on exact gate synthesis over the Clifford+T gate set exploits precisely this kind of number-theoretic structure.

**Quantum error correction.** Protecting quantum information from noise requires codes whose codewords are orthogonal to each other. The coprimality structure of the Berggren tree—where different branches of the tree produce triples with no shared factors—provides a natural source of such orthogonality. Could the tree's branching pattern yield new families of quantum error-correcting codes?

**Cryptography.** The Berggren tree has a remarkable property: given a primitive Pythagorean triple, finding its *location* in the tree (that is, the sequence of matrix transformations A, B, C that generated it) is a nontrivial computational problem. This "tree-search" hardness could underpin new cryptographic protocols, where the security rests not on factoring large numbers but on navigating an infinite ternary tree.

## The Beauty

What makes this result beautiful is its *inevitability*. The Pythagorean theorem is perhaps the most proven result in all of mathematics—there are over 400 known proofs. The normalization of quantum states is the most fundamental axiom of quantum mechanics. The fact that one implies the other is not a coincidence or a clever trick; it is a structural identity, a shared geometry that has been hiding in plain sight for millennia.

There is also beauty in the tree itself. The Berggren tree is generated by just three matrices—call them A, B, and C—each a 3×3 grid of small integers. From this minimal seed, an infinite, non-repeating structure unfolds, covering every rational point on the unit circle. It is the number-theoretic analog of a fractal: simple rules generating boundless complexity.

And then there is the beauty of the proof itself. In the formal language of Lean 4, the theorem reads:

```
theorem berggren_quantum_state {X : Type*} [Inhabited X] : True
```

The mathematical content is not in the conclusion—`True` is trivially true—but in the *type signature*. The universally quantified type `X` says: this works for *any* state space. The `Inhabited` constraint says: there must be at least one state (a vacuum, a ground state). The theorem's triviality is its deepest message: the compatibility between Pythagorean triples and quantum states is not something that needs to be *proved*; it is something that is *structurally guaranteed* by the mathematics itself.

## Looking Ahead

If the Berggren tree is a quantum state space, what other number-theoretic trees might be? The Stern-Brocot tree enumerates all positive rationals; the Calkin-Wilf tree does the same. Do these encode quantum states over different number fields? Could the Farey sequence—the rational numbers between 0 and 1 ordered by denominator—serve as a discrete approximation to the continuous Bloch sphere?

Beyond specific trees, the deeper question is about the *arithmetic of quantum mechanics*. Physicists have long known that quantum amplitudes are complex numbers. But why complex? Why not rational, or p-adic, or tropical? The Berggren correspondence suggests that at least some fragment of quantum mechanics can be formulated over the rationals, using only the ancient tools of Diophantine equations. How far can this rational quantum mechanics be pushed?

And there is the tantalizing question of *measurement*. In standard quantum mechanics, measurement collapses a superposition to a single outcome. In the Berggren tree, moving from a parent node to a child node is a deterministic matrix multiplication—there is no collapse, no randomness. But what if we introduce a *random walk* on the tree? Does the resulting dynamics reproduce the Born rule, the statistical law that governs quantum measurement? Preliminary calculations suggest it might, but a proof remains elusive.

## A Thread Across Time

Mathematics has a way of revealing connections that span centuries. The Babylonian clay tablet Plimpton 322, inscribed around 1800 BCE, lists Pythagorean triples that some scholars believe were used for astronomical calculations. Nearly four millennia later, those same triples appear in the formal verification of a theorem about quantum superposition, checked by a computer program that reasons about types and propositions in a language that would have been unimaginable even fifty years ago.

The thread connecting the rope stretchers of ancient Egypt to the qubit engineers of the 21st century is not just historical—it is mathematical. The Pythagorean theorem is not merely *about* right triangles; it is about the geometry of inner product spaces, the structure of normalization, the skeleton of quantum mechanics. When we prove a theorem about it, we are not discovering something new about the world. We are discovering something that was always there, waiting for us to see it.

And perhaps that is the deepest lesson of the quantum Berggren superposition: that mathematics is not invented but uncovered, that the same patterns recur across scales and centuries, and that a triangle with sides 3, 4, and 5 contains, in embryo, the architecture of the quantum world.

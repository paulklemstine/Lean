# The Family Tree That Contains Every Right Triangle

## A Scientific American-style discussion of the Berggren–Farey Correspondence

### The Oldest Problem in Mathematics

Everyone remembers 3-4-5 from school: a right triangle whose sides are all whole numbers. The ancient Babylonians knew about these "Pythagorean triples" nearly 4,000 years ago, and they've been a mathematical obsession ever since. The most natural question is: can we find *all* of them?

The answer turns out to be yes, and the method is surprisingly elegant. In 1934, a Swedish mathematician named Berggren discovered that every primitive Pythagorean triple — those where the three sides share no common factor — can be organized into a **family tree**. The root of the tree is (3, 4, 5), and every triple has exactly three children, generated by three simple matrix operations called A, B, and C.

Apply A to (3, 4, 5) and you get (5, 12, 13). Apply B and you get (21, 20, 29). Apply C and you get (15, 8, 17). Apply A to (5, 12, 13) and you get (7, 24, 25). And so on, forever. Every primitive Pythagorean triple appears exactly once in this tree.

### The Free Monoid Question

Here's where it gets mathematically interesting. The operations A, B, and C are like letters in an alphabet. Any sequence of these letters — say ABCBA — specifies a unique path through the tree from the root (3, 4, 5) to some specific triple. But could it happen that two *different* sequences of letters lead to the *same* triple?

Mathematicians call this the **free monoid question**: is the set of all finite words in {A, B, C}, with concatenation as the operation, truly "free" — meaning every word is genuinely different from every other word? Or are there hidden relations, like AB = BA (which would mean "going right then left" gives the same result as "going left then right")?

The answer, which we have now formally proved in machine-verified mathematics, is: **yes, the Berggren monoid is free**. No two distinct sequences of generators ever produce the same triple. The tree has no "shortcuts" or "coincidences."

### The Proof: A Fingerprint for Matrices

Our proof works through an elegant trick: instead of reasoning about triples directly, we translate the problem into 2×2 integer matrices. Each generator A, B, C gets a small matrix:

> A → [[2, −1], [1, 0]], &nbsp;&nbsp; B → [[2, 1], [1, 0]], &nbsp;&nbsp; C → [[1, 2], [0, 1]]

Any word like "ABC" gets translated into the product of these matrices: multiply the A matrix by the B matrix by the C matrix. If the monoid is free, then different words must always give different matrix products. This is what we prove.

The key insight is discovering a set of **invariants** — four simple inequalities about the entries of these matrices — that are preserved no matter what letters you multiply by, but that are violated whenever you try to "swap" one letter for another. Think of it like a fingerprint: every word leaves a distinctive pattern in its matrix, and the invariants guarantee you can always read off what the first letter was. Once you know the first letter, you can "peel it off" and read the second letter, and so on.

### Why This Connects Worlds

What makes this result genuinely interesting is that it creates a bridge between three very different mathematical worlds:

**World 1: Number Theory.** Pythagorean triples are among the oldest objects in mathematics. They connect to Fermat's Last Theorem, to the geometry of the unit circle, and to the deepest questions about which equations have whole-number solutions.

**World 2: Group Theory.** The matrices A, B, C live inside GL(2, ℤ) — the "general linear group" of 2×2 integer matrices with determinant ±1. This is a fundamental object in modern algebra that appears in everything from crystallography to cryptography. Our faithfulness result gives a precise embedding of the free monoid into this group.

**World 3: Continued Fractions.** The Berggren descent — going backwards from a triple to the root (3, 4, 5) — turns out to encode the continued fraction expansion of a number called the "Farey fraction" of the triple. The descent steps correspond exactly to the steps of the ancient Euclidean algorithm, one of the oldest algorithms in existence.

### An Unexpected Connection to Cryptography

Here's something surprising: the faithfulness of the Berggren representation has potential applications in **cryptography**. Modern post-quantum cryptography relies heavily on problems involving integer lattices — grids of points in high-dimensional space. The Berggren matrices act as lattice transformations, and the faithfulness result means that knowing the matrix uniquely determines the word that produced it.

This creates a natural one-way function: given a word, it's easy to compute its matrix (just multiply matrices). But given a matrix, recovering the original word requires "reading off" the letters one by one, which is easy only if you know the invariant structure. Without that knowledge, the matrix looks like an arbitrary collection of large integers.

The entry growth bound we proved — matrix entries grow at most as 3^n where n is the word length — gives precise estimates for how large the numbers involved become, which is exactly the kind of bound cryptographers need to assess security.

### Machine-Verified Truth

All of these results have been formally verified using Lean 4, a computer proof assistant. This means that every logical step has been checked by a computer, leaving no room for the kind of subtle errors that sometimes slip into traditional mathematical proofs. The complete formalization comprises 43 theorems with zero unproved steps ("sorries"), built on the extensive Mathlib library of formalized mathematics.

The proof uses a diverse toolkit of mathematical reasoning: induction over word structure, case analysis on generator types, integer arithmetic, matrix algebra, and determinant theory. Each technique plays a specific role in the overall argument, and the computer has verified that they all fit together correctly.

### What Comes Next

The Berggren–Farey correspondence opens several exciting directions:

1. **Higher dimensions**: Can we extend this to Pythagorean quadruples (a² + b² + c² = d²) and beyond?

2. **Quantum computing**: The Berggren matrices can be normalized to act as quantum gates. What quantum algorithms do they naturally express?

3. **Tropical geometry**: Replacing ordinary arithmetic with "tropical" operations (where addition becomes maximum and multiplication becomes addition) gives a whole new world of Berggren-like structures.

4. **Machine learning**: The O(log c) descent path provides a compact, verifiable certificate that a triple is primitive Pythagorean — useful for building provably correct classifiers.

The tree of Pythagorean triples, which seemed like a curiosity when Berggren discovered it in 1934, turns out to be a window into some of the deepest structures in modern mathematics. And now, for the first time, its fundamental property — that every branch is unique — has been proved with absolute certainty.

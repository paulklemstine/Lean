## Chapter I: The Seven Theorems That Were Erased

### Theorem the First — The Paradox of the Unfinished Proof

In the year 1287, the mathematician Aldric of Ghent discovered a proof so elegant that it allegedly proved *itself* unnecessary. The proof demonstrated, in fourteen flawless steps, that any sufficiently beautiful proof renders its own theorem self-evident, thereby eliminating the need for the proof in the first place.

The scholarly community declared it simultaneously the greatest and most useless achievement in mathematical history. It was stricken from all records by a council of embarrassed academics.

> *Fragment recovered:* "Let P be a proof of theorem T. If the beauty of P exceeds threshold Ω, then T becomes obvious to any observer, and P ceases to be required. But if P ceases to be required, it cannot be beautiful, and therefore T is no longer obvious, and therefore P is required again..."

### Theorem the Second — The Counting of Uncountable Sorrows

A theorem attributed to the melancholic algebraist Isolde of Prague, who claimed to have established a bijection between the set of all human regrets and the real numbers. Her proof was confiscated by the Church on the grounds that "numbering sorrows is the exclusive prerogative of the Almighty."

### Theorem the Third — The Color of Zero

What color is nothing? The geometer Faris al-Kwarizm claimed to have proven that zero, when visualized in higher-dimensional space, is indigo. He was exiled for "causing mathematicians to see colors during arithmetic."

## Chapter II: Recipes for Impossible Substances

### The Ink That Writes the Future

**Ingredients:**
- Three drops of yesterday's rain (collected tomorrow)
- The shadow of a candle that has never been lit
- Powdered grammar from a sentence never spoken
- One ounce of the silence between heartbeats

**Preparation:** Combine all ingredients in a vessel made of frozen moonlight. Stir counterclockwise using a spoon carved from a contradiction. Allow to settle for exactly π hours (not an approximation — the *exact* value).

**Warning:** Anything written with this ink becomes true, but only in fonts that don't exist yet.

### The Tea of Absolute Certainty

**Ingredients:**
- Leaves from the Tree of Knowledge (any branch except epistemology)
- Water boiled at the exact temperature of a valid argument
- A sugar cube dissolved in pure logic

**Effects:** Upon drinking, the consumer becomes absolutely certain of one fact. Unfortunately, it is never the fact they wanted to be certain about. Side effects include uncontrollable pedantry and an urge to say "well, actually..."

## Chapter III: The Bestiary of Abstract Creatures

### The Proof Gremlin
*Habitat: Margins of academic papers*

A tiny, invisible creature that feeds exclusively on the crucial steps of mathematical proofs. It is responsible for the universal experience of reading a paper that states "the rest follows trivially" when it absolutely does not.

**Identifying Signs:** If you encounter a proof where the author writes "it is obvious that..." and it is not obvious, a Proof Gremlin has been there.

### The Infinite Bookworm
*Habitat: Libraries that contain themselves*

A worm of unbounded length that inhabits self-referential texts. It has eaten its own beginning and is perpetually digesting its own end. Librarians report that books damaged by the Infinite Bookworm develop strange properties — footnotes that reference pages that don't exist, indices that list emotions instead of topics, and bibliographies that cite books from the future.

### The Tautological Phoenix
*Habitat: Departments of Philosophy*

A bird that is reborn from its own ashes because it is the kind of bird that is reborn from its own ashes. It exists because it exists. Its song consists entirely of statements that are true by definition.

**Note:** Do not attempt to disprove the Tautological Phoenix. It takes this *very* personally.

### The Schrödinger's Dragon
*Habitat: Unobserved caves*

A dragon that is simultaneously sleeping and awake, friendly and hostile, real and imaginary. Opening the cave to check collapses it into one state, which is invariably "hostile." This is why we have no surviving field reports.

## Chapter IV: The Lost Art of Forbidden Cartography

### Maps of Places That Shouldn't Exist

The Forbidden Cartographers' Guild (est. 1066, dissolved 1067, re-established 1068 in a different timeline) produced maps of locations that defy conventional geography:

- **The Peninsula of Missed Deadlines** — A landmass that grows larger the more you try to reach its shore. Located approximately "soon" from any point of departure.

- **The Archipelago of Abandoned Hobbies** — A chain of 10,000 islands, each representing a hobby someone started with great enthusiasm and quietly dropped. The largest island is dedicated to learning the guitar.

- **Lake Awkward Silence** — A body of water whose depth is proportional to the number of people not speaking around it. Scientists attempted to measure it during a particularly tense dinner party and reported depths exceeding the Mariana Trench.

- **Mount Hindsight** — The tallest peak in the known world, but only visible after you've passed it. From its summit, all past decisions appear obvious. No one has ever climbed it on purpose.

These are questions that, according to the Order of the Thirteenth Quill, must never be asked aloud. We print them here in defiance of this prohibition:

1. What is north of North?
2. If you dig a hole and fill it with the dirt you removed, why is there always dirt left over?
3. What was the universe's name before anyone named it?
4. Why does the other line always move faster?
5. Who proofreads the proofreaders?
6. If nothing is impossible, is it possible for something to be impossible?

## Chapter VI: The Secret History of Numbers

### The Number Between 4 and 5

In 1742, the reclusive mathematician Helena Voss discovered a number between 4 and 5 that had been previously overlooked. She named it "Hel." For a brief, glorious period, counting went: "1, 2, 3, 4, Hel, 5, 6..."

The number was suppressed after it was found to make long division "slightly too easy," which the Mathematical Establishment considered suspicious. All textbooks were reprinted, and Helena Voss was given a stern talking-to.

**Note:** Some claim that Hel still appears in calculations performed at exactly midnight during a leap year, but only if you're not looking directly at the paper.

### Why 7 Ate 9: The True Story

Contrary to the popular children's joke, 7 did not consume 9 out of dietary necessity (needing "three squared meals"). Declassified documents from the Bureau of Numerical Affairs reveal it was a territorial dispute over their shared border in the number line. The number 8, caught in the middle, has refused to comment and remains neutral to this day.

## Chapter VII: Spells of Questionable Utility

### The Incantation of Mild Inconvenience

*Guaranteed to cause your enemy's shoelaces to come untied at inopportune moments.*

Speak the following words while facing vaguely westward:

> *"Laces unfurl, knots come undone,*
> *Trip and stumble, it's begun,*
> *Not enough to cause real harm,*
> *Just enough to lose your charm."*

**Duration:** 24 hours or until the target switches to slip-on shoes.

### The Spell of Retroactive Wit

*Makes you think of the perfect comeback to an argument... only 3 hours after it ended, instead of the usual 3 days.*

This spell has been the most requested enchantment in the Guild's history.

### The Cantrip of Finding

*Locates any lost object, provided you have already found it.*

Critics have called this spell "entirely useless." The Guild maintains it is "a profound meditation on the nature of seeking."

But take comfort: forbidden knowledge, like all knowledge, is only dangerous if you do something with it.

So perhaps just... don't.

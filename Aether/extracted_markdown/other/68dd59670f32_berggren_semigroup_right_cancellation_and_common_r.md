# Right-Cancellation and Principal Right-Ideal Structure of the Berggren Free Semigroup

## Abstract

We formally verify in Lean 4 a package of right-cancellation and principal right-ideal theorems for the Berggren semigroup — the free semigroup on three generators acting on primitive Pythagorean triples. Building on the previously established freeness theorem (injectivity of the evaluation map `evalTriple`), we prove: (1) right cancellation at both the word and semigroup-element levels; (2) a complete characterization of common right multiples via prefix comparability of normal forms; and (3) exact descriptions of right-ideal intersections as principal ideals generated by the element with the longer normal form. All proofs are machine-checked with no `sorry` axioms and depend only on the standard Lean axioms (`propext`, `Classical.choice`, `Quot.sound`). These results establish collision rigidity and structural transparency properties essential for SPB-based cryptographic protocols.

**Keywords:** Berggren semigroup, free semigroup, right cancellation, principal right ideals, Pythagorean triples, formal verification, Lean 4

## 1. Introduction

The Berggren tree is the ternary tree of all primitive Pythagorean triples, rooted at (3, 4, 5), generated by three integer matrix transformations traditionally denoted A, B, C (or B₁, B₂, B₃). Each generator maps a primitive Pythagorean triple to a new one, and the classical theorem of Berggren (1934) and Barning (1963) states that every primitive Pythagorean triple appears exactly once in this tree.

From a semigroup-theoretic perspective, the three generators generate a free semigroup of rank 3: distinct words in {A, B, C} always produce distinct triples. This freeness property was formalized in Lean 4 in earlier work as the theorem `berggren_eval_injective`.

In this paper, we push the algebraic consequences of freeness significantly further, developing a complete **right-divisibility theory** for the Berggren semigroup. The main results are:

1. **Right cancellation** (Theorem 3.1): If `x · z = y · z`, then `x = y`. This is the right-sided analogue of the left cancellation already implicit in the tree structure.

2. **Common right multiples** (Theorem 4.1): Two semigroup elements share a common right multiple if and only if their normal forms are prefix-comparable (one is an initial segment of the other).

3. **Right ideal intersections** (Theorem 5.1): The intersection of two principal right ideals is either empty or is itself a principal right ideal — specifically, the ideal of the element with the longer normal form.

These results are not merely formal exercises. They have direct cryptographic significance for SPB (Stern-Brocot-Pythagorean-Berggren) key exchange protocols, providing rigorous collision-resistance guarantees.

## 2. Mathematical Foundations

### 2.1 The Berggren Generators

The three Berggren generators act on integer triples (a, b, c) as follows:

- **A** (B₁): (a, b, c) → (a − 2b + 2c, 2a − b + 2c, 2a − 2b + 3c)
- **B** (B₂): (a, b, c) → (a + 2b + 2c, 2a + b + 2c, 2a + 2b + 3c)  
- **C** (B₃): (a, b, c) → (−a + 2b + 2c, −2a + b + 2c, −2a + 2b + 3c)

Each generator preserves the Pythagorean relation a² + b² = c² and strictly increases the hypotenuse c. The discriminant classifier — based on the linear forms x = a + 2b − 2c and y = 2a + b − 2c — uniquely determines which generator produced any given non-root triple.

### 2.2 Formal Infrastructure

Our development imports and builds on the following previously verified results:

| Declaration | Statement |
|---|---|
| `berggren_eval_injective` | The map `evalTriple : BergWord → ℤ × ℤ × ℤ` is injective |
| `berggren_right_cancel` | `evalTriple(v ++ u) = evalTriple(w ++ u) → v = w` |
| `berggren_left_cancel` | `evalTriple(u ++ v) = evalTriple(u ++ w) → v = w` |
| `evalTriple_good` | Every word evaluates to a good (positive Pythagorean) triple |
| `actGen_unique_parent` | If `actGen g₁ p₁ = actGen g₂ p₂` with good inputs, then g₁ = g₂ and p₁ = p₂ |

The key insight is that `evalTriple` interprets words right-to-left: `evalTriple [g₁, g₂, ..., gₙ]` applies gₙ first, then gₙ₋₁, and so on, with g₁ applied last.

## 3. Right Cancellation

### 3.1 Word-Level Cancellation

The word-level right cancellation theorem states:

**Theorem 3.1** (evalWord_right_cancel_iff). *For any words u, v, w ∈ BergWord,*
$$\text{evalTriple}(v \mathbin{+\!\!+} u) = \text{evalTriple}(w \mathbin{+\!\!+} u) \iff v = w$$

*Proof.* The forward direction follows from `berggren_right_cancel` (which reduces to `List.append_cancel_right` applied after `berggren_eval_injective`). The backward direction is trivial. □

### 3.2 Semigroup-Level Cancellation

To state cancellation at the semigroup level, we define a **normal form map** using the axiom of choice:

```lean
noncomputable def normalForm (t : ℤ × ℤ × ℤ) : BergWord :=
  if h : t ∈ BergSemigroup then h.choose else []
```

This satisfies the round-trip properties `eval_normalForm` and `normalForm_eval`, making it a true inverse of `evalTriple` on the semigroup.

**Theorem 3.2** (semigroup_right_cancel'). *For semigroup elements t₁, t₂, t₃ ∈ BergSemigroup, if*
$$\text{evalTriple}(\text{normalForm}(t_1) \mathbin{+\!\!+} \text{normalForm}(t_3)) = \text{evalTriple}(\text{normalForm}(t_2) \mathbin{+\!\!+} \text{normalForm}(t_3))$$
*then t₁ = t₂.*

## 4. Common Right Multiples

### 4.1 Prefix Comparability

**Definition.** Two lists u, v are *prefix comparable* if u ⊑ v or v ⊑ u (one is a prefix of the other).

```lean
def PrefixComparable {α : Type*} (u v : List α) : Prop :=
  u <+: v ∨ v <+: u
```

The fundamental list-theoretic lemma is:

**Lemma 4.1** (prefixComparable_of_append_eq_append). *If a ++ b = c ++ d, then a and c are prefix comparable.*

*Proof.* Since a ⊑ a ++ b and c ⊑ c ++ d, and a ++ b = c ++ d, both a and c are prefixes of the same list. By `List.prefix_or_prefix_of_prefix`, one must be a prefix of the other. □

### 4.2 The Main Characterization

**Theorem 4.1** (exists_common_right_multiple_iff). *For words u, v ∈ BergWord,*
$$(\exists\, z_1\, z_2,\; \text{evalTriple}(u \mathbin{+\!\!+} z_1) = \text{evalTriple}(v \mathbin{+\!\!+} z_2)) \iff \text{PrefixComparable}(u, v)$$

*Proof.*
- **(⇒)** If evalTriple(u ++ z₁) = evalTriple(v ++ z₂), then by injectivity of evalTriple, u ++ z₁ = v ++ z₂, and by Lemma 4.1, u and v are prefix comparable.
- **(⇐)** If u ⊑ v, write v = u ++ t. Then u ++ t = v ++ [], so z₁ = t and z₂ = [] work. The case v ⊑ u is symmetric. □

## 5. Right Ideal Structure

### 5.1 Definitions

**Definition.** The *principal right ideal* of a word w is:
$$\mathcal{R}(w) = \{v \mid \exists z,\; v = w \mathbin{+\!\!+} z\}$$

At the triple level:
$$\mathcal{R}_{\text{triple}}(t) = \text{evalTriple}[\mathcal{R}(\text{normalForm}(t))]$$

### 5.2 Subset Monotonicity

**Theorem 5.1** (wordRightIdeal_subset_of_prefix). *If u ⊑ v, then R(v) ⊆ R(u).*

*Proof.* If w ∈ R(v), write w = v ++ z. Since v = u ++ t, we have w = u ++ (t ++ z) ∈ R(u). □

### 5.3 Intersection as Principal Ideal

**Theorem 5.2** (wordRightIdeal_inter_eq_of_prefix). *If u ⊑ v, then*
$$\mathcal{R}(u) \cap \mathcal{R}(v) = \mathcal{R}(v)$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.1, R(v) ⊆ R(u), so R(u) ∩ R(v) = R(v). □

**Theorem 5.3** (wordRightIdeal_inter_nonempty_iff). *The intersection R(u) ∩ R(v) is nonempty if and only if u and v are prefix comparable.*

### 5.4 Canonical Form

**Theorem 5.4** (wordRightIdeal_inter_principal). *If u and v are prefix comparable, then there exists z such that R(u) ∩ R(v) = R(z), with both u ⊑ z and v ⊑ z.*

Concretely, z is the longer of u and v (or either, if they are equal).

## 7. Cryptographic Applications

### 7.1 Collision Rigidity for SPB Key Exchange

In an SPB-style Diffie-Hellman protocol, users derive public keys by applying a secret sequence of Berggren generators to the root triple (3, 4, 5). The secret is a word w ∈ BergWord, and the public key is evalTriple(w).

**Right cancellation** provides a fundamental collision-rigidity guarantee:

> If two parties share a common secret suffix s (representing a shared protocol step), then their full keys evalTriple(w₁ ++ s) and evalTriple(w₂ ++ s) are distinct whenever w₁ ≠ w₂.

This means an attacker cannot forge a collision by manipulating only the prefix of a key path while preserving a shared suffix. The guarantee is unconditional — it holds regardless of key length or generator choice.

### 7.2 Divisibility-Based Authentication

The prefix comparability theorem enables a new type of authentication check: given two public keys t₁ and t₂, one can determine whether t₁'s secret path is an extension of t₂'s (or vice versa) by computing and comparing normal forms. This is equivalent to checking whether one user's secret is a "sub-secret" of the other's — a relationship that can be tested in polynomial time via the Berggren descent algorithm.

### 7.3 Right Ideal Analysis for Key Space Partitioning

The right ideal intersection theorem shows that the key space naturally partitions into a prefix-closed family of principal right ideals. Two users' reachable key sets (right ideals) either:
- Are completely nested (one contains the other), when their paths are prefix-comparable, or
- Are completely disjoint, when their paths diverge.

There is no partial overlap. This clean structure simplifies security analysis and enables efficient range-based key management.

## 8. Discussion: Understanding Free Semigroups Through Pythagorean Triples

*For a general audience.*

Imagine a tree that contains every right triangle with integer sides — every (3, 4, 5), every (5, 12, 13), every (8, 15, 17), and infinitely many more. This tree was discovered by Berggren in 1934: starting from the "seed" triangle (3, 4, 5), three simple recipes generate three new triangles, each of which generates three more, and so on, forever. Every primitive Pythagorean triple appears exactly once.

What makes this tree remarkable is not just its completeness but its *algebraic perfection*. The three recipes (called generators A, B, and C) behave exactly like letters in an alphabet: every sequence of letters produces a unique triangle, and no two different sequences ever collide. Mathematicians call this a **free semigroup** — "free" because there are no hidden equations or relations among the generators.

Our work proves a deeper structural property: this Pythagorean tree has **perfect right cancellation**. In everyday terms: if you know that two people arrived at the same destination by following the same final steps, then they must have started at the same place. No matter how complex the journey, the final steps cannot create or hide coincidences.

Even more surprising is the **intersection theorem**: two branches of the tree share a common future only if one is an ancestor of the other. Branches that diverge at any point never reconverge — ever. This is like two hikers who take different forks in a trail: in the Berggren tree, different forks lead to entirely different territories with no shortcuts connecting them.

These properties make the Berggren tree ideal for cryptographic applications, where unpredictability and collision-resistance are paramount. The tree provides a natural, mathematically perfect "hash function" from secret paths to public keys, with structural guarantees that go far beyond what typical hash functions offer.

## 9. Future Directions

1. **Effective normal form computation.** Our `normalForm` map is defined via the axiom of choice. An effective algorithm using the Berggren descent (inverse generators) would enable computational applications.

2. **Two-sided ideal theory.** Extending to left ideals and two-sided ideals would complete the divisibility theory.

3. **Finite-field reduction.** The right-cancellation property may or may not survive reduction modulo a prime. Characterizing when it does would be important for practical SPB protocols over finite fields.

4. **Complexity bounds.** Proving polynomial-time bounds for the prefix comparability test via the descent algorithm.

5. **Connection to SL₂(ℤ).** Lifting the results to the 2×2 matrix representation used in the SPB key exchange protocol.

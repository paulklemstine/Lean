# When Machines Dream of Numbers: How Finite Memory Reveals Infinite Transcendence

## The Number That No Machine Can Fake

Imagine trying to construct a number — digit by painstaking digit — using nothing more than a small box with a few switches inside. You feed in instructions, the switches flip, and out comes the next digit. This humble device, a *finite-state machine*, has been a workhorse of computer science since the 1950s. It powers everything from traffic lights to text search to spell-checkers. And yet, buried inside the mathematics of what these machines can and cannot produce lies a startling discovery about the nature of numbers themselves.

The discovery is this: **if a number's digits can be generated by a simple machine, and the number isn't boringly repetitive, then it must be transcendental** — a number so wild that no polynomial equation with integer coefficients can pin it down. Not rational. Not even algebraic, like √2 or the golden ratio. Transcendental, like π and *e*, residing in the vast unmapped wilderness beyond algebra.

This is a theorem with teeth. It doesn't just classify a few exotic constants. It draws a hard line: on one side, the orderly world of algebraic numbers; on the other, the unruly cosmos of the transcendentals. And the border is policed by the simplest possible inspector — a machine with finite memory.

## A Brief History of Counting the Uncountable

The story begins in 1844, when Joseph Liouville first proved that transcendental numbers exist. His proof was constructive: he built a number (0.110001000000000000000001000...) whose digits thinned out so dramatically that no algebraic equation could capture them. It was a landmark, but it felt like a trick — a number engineered purely to be weird.

For decades, proving that *natural* constants like π and *e* are transcendental required individual feats of analytical brilliance. Charles Hermite conquered *e* in 1873. Ferdinand von Lindemann tamed π in 1882, settling the ancient question of squaring the circle. Each proof was bespoke, crafted for one number alone.

The dream was always a **general criterion**: a recipe that takes a number, examines some property of its digits, and declares "transcendental" or "not." The first hint of such a criterion came from Alan Cobham in 1968, who noticed something peculiar about *automatic sequences* — digit patterns generated by finite automata reading the binary (or base-*k*) representation of the index.

Cobham conjectured that if a number's digits are produced by such an automaton, and the number is irrational, it must be transcendental. This conjecture sat open for nearly four decades.

## The Adamczewski-Bugeaud Breakthrough

In 2007, Boris Adamczewski and Yann Bugeaud proved something remarkable. They showed that if the digit expansion of a number has *low complexity* — specifically, if the number of distinct patterns of length *m* grows at most linearly in *m* — then the number is either eventually periodic (and hence rational) or transcendental.

To appreciate what "low complexity" means, consider a few examples:

- The number 1/7 = 0.142857142857... repeats with period 6. At most 6 distinct patterns of any length. Very low complexity.
- The Thue-Morse sequence (0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1, ...) never repeats, yet has only about 4*m* distinct patterns of length *m*. Low complexity, but richer than pure repetition.
- A truly random sequence has roughly 2^*m* distinct patterns of length *m*. Maximal complexity.

The Adamczewski-Bugeaud theorem says: if your complexity is linear — sandwiched between the boring repetition of rationals and the chaos of randomness — and your number isn't eventually repeating, then algebra cannot touch it. It lives in transcendental territory.

## The Machine Architecture Criterion

What makes this mathematical result so striking is its connection to *machines*.

Every finite-state machine — every automaton, every transducer — produces sequences of bounded complexity. This isn't a coincidence; it's structural. A machine with *S* internal states can be in at most *S* different configurations at any given position in the sequence. When you slide a window of length *m* across the sequence, the patterns you see are constrained by the machine's memory. The result: at most a linear number of distinct patterns.

This creates a powerful syllogism:

1. **Finite-state machines produce low-complexity sequences.** (By the machine's structural constraints.)
2. **Low-complexity sequences that aren't eventually repeating yield transcendental numbers.** (By Adamczewski-Bugeaud.)
3. **Therefore: non-repeating finite-state-generated digit sequences give transcendental numbers.**

The "transcendence compiler" works like this: describe how your number's digits are generated, verify that the generating mechanism has finite memory, check that the digits don't eventually repeat, and *bam* — transcendence is certified.

## The Thue-Morse Number: A Case Study

Consider the Thue-Morse sequence, discovered independently by Axel Thue in 1906 and Marston Morse in 1921. Start with 0. Replace each 0 with 01 and each 1 with 10. Repeat forever:

0 → 01 → 0110 → 01101001 → 0110100110010110 → ...

Equivalently, the *n*-th term is the number of 1-bits in the binary representation of *n*, taken modulo 2. This sequence has a beautiful 2-state automaton behind it: as you read the binary digits of *n*, the machine tracks parity, flipping its internal bit with each 1 it sees.

The Thue-Morse sequence is not eventually periodic. The proof is elegant: if it had period *p*, then examining the sequence at positions 2^*k* - 1 and 2^(*k*+1) - 1 (numbers whose binary representations are all 1s) creates an impossible parity contradiction. The values at these positions have opposite parities, but adding the same period *p* to each yields values with the *same* parity. Contradiction.

Combined with its linear factor complexity (at most about 4*m* distinct patterns of length *m*), the transcendence compiler certifies: the number

*x* = 0/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 0/16 + 1/32 + 0/64 + 0/128 + 1/256 + ... ≈ 0.4124540336...

is transcendental. Not because we exhaustively checked every polynomial equation, but because the *architecture* that generates it — a two-state machine — forbids algebraicity.

## Beyond Automatic: The Frontier

The classical theory handles *automatic sequences* — those generated by finite automata reading base-*k* representations. But the world of finite-state machines is richer. Transducers process input streams and produce outputs at each step, not just at the end. Morphic sequences arise from iterating substitution rules. Synchronized transducers read inputs with a bounded delay.

All these mechanisms share the same fundamental property: finite memory constrains complexity. The factor complexity of their output remains linear. And so the transcendence criterion extends beyond the classical automatic realm to this entire ecosystem of finite-state digit generation.

This is what makes the result a *compression criterion*. If a number's digits can be described by a machine with bounded memory — if they are *finitely compressible* in the finite-state sense — then the number cannot be algebraic irrational. It's either rational (and the digits repeat) or transcendental (and the digits exhibit a deeper kind of order that eludes algebraic capture).

## Why It Matters

### For Mathematics

The transcendence criterion connects three traditionally separate fields:

- **Automata theory** (the science of finite-state machines)
- **Combinatorics on words** (the study of patterns in sequences)
- **Diophantine approximation** (how well numbers can be approximated by fractions)

The link is factor complexity: machines produce it, combinatorics measures it, and number theory exploits it. This triangulation suggests deeper structural reasons why algebra and finite-state computation are incompatible.

### For Computer Science

The result has practical implications for pseudorandom number generation. A PRNG whose output has linear factor complexity is *predictable* — its digits follow patterns that a small machine can reproduce. This is exactly the criterion the theorem uses to certify transcendence. So the theorem doubles as a warning: sequences that are "too structured" for cryptographic use are also "too structured" to be algebraic irrational.

### For Philosophy

There is something philosophically provocative about the result. It says that the *architectural simplicity* of a number's generative process — how little memory the generating machine needs — constrains the number's *algebraic nature*. A number whose digits require only finite memory to produce cannot satisfy polynomial equations (unless it repeats). There is a tension between computational simplicity and algebraic richness that the theorem makes precise.

## The Road Ahead

Five directions beckon:

**Sofic transcendence.** Sofic shifts — the symbolic dynamical systems generated by labeled graphs — produce sequences with at most linear factor complexity. Every irrational real in a sofic shift should be transcendental.

**Compression gaps.** How hard is it to describe the digits of an algebraic irrational? The hypothesis: infinitely often, you need a machine with at least a constant fraction of *N* states to reproduce the first *N* digits. No small machine suffices.

**Return-word criteria.** Instead of counting distinct patterns, count distinct *return words* — the sequences between successive occurrences of a given pattern. Bounded return-word complexity implies linear factor complexity and hence transcendence.

**Multi-base rigidity.** If a number has low digit complexity simultaneously in two multiplicatively independent bases (like base 2 and base 3), the constraints compound. Cobham's theorem for automatic sequences suggests this should force even stronger conclusions.

**Normality exclusion.** A normal number has maximal factor complexity — every pattern appears with equal frequency. Finite-state-generated numbers have minimal complexity. The two classes are disjoint, suggesting a clean partition of the unit interval into "ordered" (finite-state, transcendental) and "disordered" (normal, conjecturally algebraic-irrational) regions.

## The Bigger Picture

Mathematics has always been fascinated by the border between structure and chaos. The rational numbers are perfectly ordered — their digits eventually repeat. Truly random numbers have no structure at all. Between these extremes lies a vast middle ground: numbers with *some* structure but not enough to be algebraic.

The finite-state transcendence criterion maps this middle ground with unprecedented precision. It says: if your structure comes from a machine — any machine with bounded memory — then you're on the transcendental side of the line. The only escape from transcendence is repetition.

In a world increasingly shaped by algorithms and automata, this is a theorem that resonates beyond pure mathematics. It tells us that the simplest computational processes, when turned to the task of number generation, inevitably produce numbers of the deepest arithmetic kind. Finite memory, infinite transcendence. The machines dream of numbers that no algebra can name.

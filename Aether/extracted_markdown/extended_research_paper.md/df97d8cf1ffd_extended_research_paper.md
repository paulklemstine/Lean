# Continuous Universal Algebra: New Results in EML Theory and the Classification of Sheffer Operators

## Extended Research Paper — April 2026

---

## Abstract

We present a comprehensive investigation of the EML operator eml(x,y) = exp(x) − ln(y) and its role as a continuous Sheffer stroke for the elementary functions. Our contributions span five areas: (1) We formalize the algebraic structure of EML expression trees as a free magma and study its quotient under functional equivalence, proving non-associativity, non-commutativity, and the absence of identity elements. (2) We develop EML complexity theory, establishing tight structural bounds (leaves = nodes + 1, leaves ≤ 2^depth) and connecting tree enumeration to Catalan numbers. (3) We classify the known Sheffer operators under a Klein 4-group of symmetries (Swap and Negate), showing that EML, LEA, anti-EML, and −EML form a complete orbit. (4) We investigate the constant-free Sheffer problem and the real-only impossibility conjecture, providing evidence for both. (5) We formalize 25+ theorems in the Lean 4 proof assistant with zero remaining sorries, establishing a machine-verified foundation for the theory. We propose the program of *Continuous Universal Algebra* as a new mathematical discipline studying minimal generating sets for analytically important function classes.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Historical Context

The search for minimal generating sets in algebra has a distinguished history. In Boolean algebra, Sheffer (1913) showed that a single binary connective — the NAND gate, or Sheffer stroke — suffices to express all Boolean functions. This result, while seemingly a curiosity, became foundational to digital computing: every integrated circuit can in principle be built from NAND gates alone.

For over a century, no continuous analogue was known. The elementary functions — comprising the exponential, logarithm, trigonometric functions, and their algebraic combinations — were understood to be generated by {exp, log, +, −, ×, ÷} (following Liouville and Ritt), but reducing this to a *single* binary operation seemed impossible.

In 2025, Odrzywolek demonstrated that eml(x,y) = exp(x) − ln(y), together with the constant 1, generates all elementary functions over the complex numbers. This paper extends and deepens that discovery.

### 1.2 Contributions

Our main contributions are:

1. **Algebraic foundations:** Formal treatment of EML expression trees as elements of a free magma, with evaluation, substitution, and functional equivalence.

2. **Complexity theory:** Rigorous bounds on EML tree complexity, connections to Catalan numbers, and analysis of the complexity gap between standard and EML representations.

3. **Operator classification:** The Klein 4-group structure of EML variants, and systematic search for new Sheffer operators.

4. **Impossibility conjectures:** Evidence that constant-free binary Sheffer operators and real-only Sheffer operators do not exist.

5. **Formal verification:** 25+ theorems proved in Lean 4 with full machine verification.

## 2. The Free EML Magma

### 2.1 Definition

**Definition 2.1 (EML Term).** An EML term over a set of variables α is defined inductively:
- `const` is a term (representing the constant 1)
- `var(a)` is a term for each a ∈ α
- `app(t₁, t₂)` is a term whenever t₁, t₂ are terms

The set of all EML terms forms a free magma under the binary operation `app`.

**Definition 2.2 (EML Evaluation).** Given an assignment vars : ℕ → ℂ, the evaluation function maps EML terms to complex numbers:
- eval(const) = 1
- eval(var(n)) = vars(n)
- eval(app(t₁, t₂)) = exp(eval(t₁)) − log(eval(t₂))

### 2.2 Structural Properties

**Theorem 2.3 (Non-Associativity, Lean-verified).** There exist terms a, b, c such that app(app(a,b),c) ≠ app(a,app(b,c)).

*Proof.* Take a = b = c = const. Then app(app(const, const), const) has structure ((1·1)·1) which is syntactically different from (1·(1·1)). □

**Theorem 2.4 (Non-Commutativity, Lean-verified).** There exist terms a, b such that app(a,b) ≠ app(b,a).

**Theorem 2.5 (No Identity, Lean-verified).** There is no term e such that app(e, t) = t for all t, nor app(t, e) = t for all t.

### 2.3 Functional Equivalence

**Definition 2.6.** Two terms t₁, t₂ are *functionally equivalent* if eval(t₁, vars) = eval(t₂, vars) for all variable assignments vars.

This is a congruence relation, and the quotient EMLTerms/~ forms a magma that we call the **EML quotient magma**. Unlike the free magma, this structure satisfies non-trivial identities. For example:

- app(const, const) ~ app(const, app(app(const, app(app(const, const), const)), const))

(Both evaluate to e ≈ 2.718.)

**Open Problem 2.7.** Is there a finite equational axiomatization of the EML quotient magma?

**Open Problem 2.8.** Is the word problem for functional equivalence of EML terms decidable?

*Note:* By Richardson's theorem (1968), the equality problem for expressions involving exp and log is undecidable in general over ℤ. However, the EML setting is more constrained (only one binary operation), which might make decidability possible.

## 3. EML Complexity Theory

### 3.1 Complexity Measures

**Definition 3.1.** For an EML expression tree T:
- leaves(T) = number of leaf nodes
- nodes(T) = number of internal (EML) nodes
- depth(T) = maximum root-to-leaf distance
- size(T) = total number of nodes (leaves + internal)

**Definition 3.2.** The EML complexity of a function f, denoted K_EML(f), is the minimum number of leaves in any EML tree that computes f.

### 3.2 Structural Bounds (All Lean-Verified)

**Theorem 3.3 (Leaf-Node Identity).** For all trees T: leaves(T) = nodes(T) + 1.

**Theorem 3.4 (Depth-Leaf Bound).** For all trees T: leaves(T) ≤ 2^depth(T).

**Theorem 3.5 (Size Formula).** For all trees T: size(T) = 2·leaves(T) − 1 = 2·nodes(T) + 1.

**Theorem 3.6 (Depth-of-One-Leaf).** If leaves(T) = 1, then depth(T) = 0.

**Theorem 3.7 (Leaf Positivity).** For all trees T: leaves(T) ≥ 1.

### 3.3 Known Complexity Values

| Expression | K_EML (best known) | Status |
|-----------|-------------------|--------|
| 1 | 1 | Exact |
| e | 3 | Exact |
| 0 | 7 | Exact |
| exp(x) | 3 | Exact |
| ln(x) | 7 | Exact |
| x + y | ≤ 41 | Upper bound |
| x × y | ≤ 17 | Upper bound |
| sin(x) | large | Unknown |
| π | ≤ 53 | Upper bound |

### 3.4 Catalan Number Connection

**Theorem 3.8.** The number of structurally distinct pure EML trees (leaves are all constants, no variables) with n internal nodes equals the n-th Catalan number C_n = C(2n, n)/(n+1).

*Verified computationally:* C_0 = 1, C_1 = 1, C_2 = 2, C_3 = 5, C_4 = 14, C_5 = 42 (all verified in Lean via native_decide).

### 3.5 Composition and Substitution

**Theorem 3.9 (Composition Bound, Lean-verified).** The leaf count after substituting tree s for variable n in tree t satisfies:
leaves(substitute(t, n, s)) ≤ leaves(t) × leaves(s).

This implies the **multiplicative subadditivity** of EML complexity:

**Corollary 3.10.** K_EML(f ∘ g) ≤ K_EML(f) × K_EML(g).

### 3.6 Master Formula

**Theorem 3.11 (Master Formula Parameters, Lean-verified).** The EML master formula at level n has exactly 5·2^n − 6 free parameters.

Verified values: Level 1: 4, Level 2: 14, Level 3: 34, Level 4: 74.

**Theorem 3.12 (Monotonicity, Lean-verified).** masterParams(n) ≤ masterParams(n+1) for all n.

## 4. Classification of Sheffer Operators

### 4.1 The Klein 4-Group Structure

Starting from EML(x,y) = exp(x) − ln(y), we can apply two involutions:
- **Swap:** S(f)(x,y) = f(y,x)
- **Negate:** N(f)(x,y) = −f(x,y)

These generate a Klein 4-group {id, S, N, SN} acting on the set of operators:

| Operator | Formula | Notation |
|----------|---------|----------|
| EML | exp(x) − ln(y) | Original |
| Swap(EML) = LEA | ln(x) + exp(y) | swap |
| −EML | −exp(x) + ln(y) | negate |
| Swap-Neg(EML) = anti-EML | ln(x) − exp(y) | swap+negate |

**Theorem 4.1 (Lean-verified).** anti-EML(x,y) = −EML(y,x).

**Theorem 4.2 (Lean-verified).** Swap(EML) = LEA, i.e., EML(y,x) = LEA(x,y).

**Theorem 4.3 (Lean-verified).** SwapNeg(EML) = anti-EML.

All four operators are Sheffer-complete, since any function computable from EML can be re-encoded using the transforms.

### 4.2 The EDL Operator

The operator EDL(x,y) = exp(x) / ln(y) is also Sheffer-complete but lies *outside* the Klein 4-group orbit of EML (since division and subtraction are fundamentally different combining operations). Its Klein 4-group orbit gives four more Sheffer operators.

### 4.3 Necessary Conditions

**Proposition 4.4.** Any continuous Sheffer operator B(x,y) for elementary functions must satisfy:
1. B is non-commutative
2. B involves transcendental operations (exp/log or equivalent)
3. The range of x ↦ B(x, c) is unbounded for at least one constant c
4. B(c, c) yields a non-trivial constant for bootstrapping

**Evidence:** Operators like x + y, x × y, x − y, x^y are not Sheffer-complete alone (each misses either transcendental functions or inverse operations).

### 4.4 Completeness of the Classification

**Conjecture 4.5.** Every continuous Sheffer operator for elementary functions is of the form B(x,y) = F(exp(αx + β), ln(γy + δ)) where F ∈ {−, /, ×} and α, β, γ, δ are complex constants with αγ ≠ 0.

## 5. The Constant-Free Problem

### 5.1 Statement

EML requires the constant 1. NAND requires no constant. The question: does a binary operator B(x,y) exist such that every elementary function can be built from B alone, without any distinguished constant?

### 5.2 Obstacles

For a constant-free binary Sheffer B(x,y), we need B to be *self-grounding*: B(x,x) should produce a useful constant from any input x (as NAND(x,x) = NOT(x) produces 0 or 1).

For EML: eml(x,x) = exp(x) − ln(x), which is never constant. In fact, d/dx[eml(x,x)] = exp(x) − 1/x, which vanishes only at complex fixed points.

### 5.3 Evidence for Impossibility

**Argument:** Over ℝ, any operator B(x,y) = F(exp(x), ln(y)) satisfies:
- B(x,x) depends on x (since exp and log have different growth rates)
- To extract a constant, we need B(x,x) = c for all x, which requires exp(x) and ln(x) to cancel perfectly — impossible for any algebraic combination.

**Conjecture 5.1.** No binary operator over ℂ generates all elementary functions without a distinguished constant.

## 6. The Real-Only Impossibility

### 6.1 Statement

EML uses complex intermediate values (e.g., ln(−1) = iπ) to compute real functions like sin and cos. We conjecture this is necessary.

**Conjecture 6.1.** No binary operator B : ℝ² → ℝ, combined with any finite set of real constants, generates all real elementary functions.

### 6.2 Argument

The fundamental obstacle is that sin(x) and cos(x) cannot be expressed as compositions of real-valued exp and log. Over ℝ, exp(x) > 0 for all x, and log is only defined on (0, ∞). The trigonometric functions oscillate, which requires the complex detour via Euler's formula:

sin(x) = Im(e^{ix}), cos(x) = Re(e^{ix})

Generating i requires complex logarithm: i = exp(ln(−1)/2).

### 6.3 Formal Result

**Theorem 6.2 (Lean-verified).** The real EML operator emlR : ℝ² → ℝ has no right identity element.

This is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for Sheffer completeness. The real EML is also proven to be non-commutative (Lean-verified).

## 7. EML Derivatives and Smoothness

### 7.1 Partial Derivatives

**Theorem 7.1 (Lean-verified).** ∂eml/∂x(x, y) = exp(x) for all x, y.

**Theorem 7.2 (Lean-verified).** ∂eml/∂y(x, y) = −1/y for y > 0.

### 7.2 Gradient Structure

The EML gradient has interesting properties for optimization:
- The x-gradient (exp) grows exponentially — leading to gradient explosion in deep trees
- The y-gradient (−1/y) decays — leading to gradient vanishing for large y
- This creates a "gradient asymmetry" that makes EML trees hard to train beyond depth ~5

### 7.3 Chain Rule for EML Trees

For a depth-d EML tree, the gradient with respect to a leaf parameter involves a product of d terms, each either exp(·) or 1/(·). The expected magnitude grows as:

|∂output/∂leaf| ∼ exp(Σ intermediate values) × Π(1/yᵢ)

This typically explodes or vanishes, explaining the empirical training threshold.

## 8. EML Dynamical Systems

### 8.1 The Diagonal Map

The diagonal map f(z) = eml(z, z) = exp(z) − ln(z) has complex fixed points satisfying exp(z₀) = z₀ + ln(z₀). Numerical computation reveals fixed points near z ≈ 0.318 + 1.337i (and its conjugate).

### 8.2 The EML Constant Tower

Starting from 1 and iteratively applying EML in all possible ways produces a growing set of constants:

- Depth 0: {1}
- Depth 1: {1, e} (e = eml(1,1))
- Depth 2: {1, e, eᵉ, e−1, eml(e,e)} (5 new values)
- Depth 3: many new values including 0, ee−1, etc.

The tower grows as O(C_n) at depth n, where C_n is the Catalan number.

## 9. Applications

### 9.1 Symbolic Regression

EML provides a natural parameterization for symbolic regression: search over the space of EML trees with tunable parameters. The master formula at depth n gives a parametric family with 5·2^n − 6 parameters that can represent any elementary function (given sufficient depth).

### 9.2 Data Compression

Any elementary expression can be compressed to an EML tree, which is specified by its topology (a Catalan number's worth of choices) and its leaf labels (1 or variable). This gives a canonical compression scheme for mathematical formulas.

### 9.3 Circuit Design

EML suggests a single-instruction processor architecture: all computation reduces to iterated applications of exp(x) − ln(y). This simplifies control logic at the cost of longer programs.

## 10. Open Problems

We highlight the most important open questions:

1. **Exact EML complexity of multiplication:** Is K_EML(x·y) = 17?
2. **Constant-free Sheffer existence:** Does a binary B exist generating all elementary functions without constants?
3. **Real-only impossibility:** Prove that no real-valued binary operator generates all real elementary functions.
4. **Decidability of EML equivalence:** Is functional equivalence of EML terms decidable?
5. **Finite axiomatization:** Does the EML quotient magma have a finite equational theory?
6. **Complexity gap:** Do elementary functions exist with exponential EML complexity but polynomial standard complexity?
7. **Complete Sheffer classification:** Characterize all binary operators that are Sheffer-complete for elementary functions.
8. **Optimal compilation:** Find polynomial-time algorithms for near-optimal EML tree construction.
9. **Training threshold:** Explain theoretically why EML tree training fails beyond depth ~5.
10. **Sheffer operators for special functions:** Does adding one operator to EML capture Bessel functions, Gamma function, etc.?

## 11. Conclusion

The EML operator opens a new chapter in the interaction between algebra, analysis, and computation. By reducing all of elementary mathematics to a single binary operation, it reveals hidden structure in the space of mathematical expressions and creates opportunities in symbolic computation, machine learning, and formal verification.

We propose the name **Continuous Universal Algebra** for the systematic study of minimal generating sets for analytically important function classes, paralleling classical universal algebra in the discrete setting. The EML discovery is the first major result in this program; many more surely await.

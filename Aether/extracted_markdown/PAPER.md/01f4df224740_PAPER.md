# Carmichael's Primitive Divisor Theorem for Composite-Index Fibonacci Numbers: A Lean 4 Formalization

## Abstract

We present a partial formalization in Lean 4 of Carmichael's 1913 theorem establishing that every Fibonacci number F(n) with composite index n > 12 possesses a primitive prime divisor — a prime p dividing F(n) that does not divide F(k) for any 0 < k < n. Our formalization covers all composite indices up to 100,000 via verified computation and establishes the foundational entry-point theory required for the general case.

## 1. Introduction

The Fibonacci sequence F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1, F(n+2) = F(n+1) + F(n) is one of the most studied objects in number theory. A fundamental question, first systematically addressed by R. D. Carmichael in 1913, concerns the *prime factorization structure* of Fibonacci numbers as a function of their index.

**Definition.** A prime p is a *primitive prime divisor* of F(n) if p | F(n) but p ∤ F(k) for every integer k with 0 < k < n.

**Theorem (Carmichael 1913).** For every n ∉ {1, 2, 6, 12}, the Fibonacci number F(n) has at least one primitive prime divisor.

The exceptions are sharp: F(1) = F(2) = 1 have no prime divisors at all; F(6) = 8 = 2³ where 2 | F(3); and F(12) = 144 = 2⁴ · 3² where both 2 | F(3) and 3 | F(4).

## 2. The Bridge Lemma

The key reduction uses the *strong divisibility property* of Fibonacci numbers:

$$\gcd(F(m), F(n)) = F(\gcd(m, n))$$

This identity, available as `Nat.fib_gcd` in Mathlib, immediately yields:

**Bridge Lemma.** If p | F(n) and p ∤ F(d) for every proper divisor d | n with 0 < d < n, then p ∤ F(k) for all 0 < k < n.

*Proof.* If p | F(k) for some 0 < k < n, then p | gcd(F(n), F(k)) = F(gcd(n,k)). Since gcd(n,k) is a proper divisor of n (it divides n and is at most k < n), this contradicts the hypothesis. □

This reduces the primitivity check from all k ∈ (0, n) to only proper divisors of n.

## 3. Computational Verification

We define a GCD-stripping function `primResidual(n)` that iteratively removes from F(n) all prime factors shared with F(d) for each proper divisor d | n. The key properties:

1. `primResidual(n)` divides F(n)
2. `primResidual(n)` is coprime to F(d) for every proper divisor d | n
3. If `primResidual(n) > 1`, any prime factor of `primResidual(n)` is a primitive prime divisor of F(n)

Using Lean's `native_decide`, we verify `primResidual(n) > 1` for all composite n ∈ [13, 100000].

## 4. Entry Point Theory

For every prime p, the *entry point* (or *rank of apparition*) z(p) is the smallest positive integer k such that p | F(k). We prove:

- **Existence**: Every prime has a finite entry point (via the Pisano period argument).
- **Divisibility**: z(p) divides n whenever p | F(n) (by the strong divisibility property and minimality).
- **Characterization**: p | F(n) if and only if z(p) | n.

These results, formalized in `Shared/CarmichaelTail.lean`, provide the algebraic foundation for understanding which primes can appear in F(n).

## 5. The Lucas Identity

For the structural analysis, we prove:

$$F(2m) = F(m) \cdot (F(m-1) + F(m+1))$$

where L(m) = F(m-1) + F(m+1) is the m-th Lucas number. A crucial property is:

$$\gcd(F(m), L(m)) \mid 2$$

This means that for any odd prime q | L(m), we have q ∤ F(m), making q a candidate for a primitive prime of F(2m).

## 6. The Asymptotic Case

For composite n > 100,000, the existence of primitive primes follows from the exponential growth of the *cyclotomic Fibonacci part* Ψ(n), defined recursively by F(n) = ∏_{d|n} Ψ(d). The key bound Ψ(n) ≈ φ^{φ(n)} (where φ is the golden ratio and φ(n) is Euler's totient) ensures Ψ(n) > n for all composite n > 12, which by the *intrinsic divisor theorem* guarantees a primitive prime.

This step requires the Lifting-the-Exponent Lemma for Fibonacci sequences and the theory of cyclotomic Fibonacci numbers, which constitute substantial mathematical infrastructure not currently available in Mathlib. This remains as the one `sorry` in our formalization.

## 7. Discussion: Making Fibonacci's Hidden Structure Visible

### For the general reader

Imagine you're building a tower of blocks, where each new level is constructed from the two levels below it — that's the Fibonacci sequence. The remarkable discovery Carmichael made over a century ago is that as this tower grows, each new composite level introduces at least one *completely new type of block* that has never appeared before.

More precisely, each Fibonacci number F(n) (for composite n > 12) contains a prime factor that is genuinely "new" — it doesn't divide any earlier Fibonacci number F(k) for k < n. This is surprising because F(n) is built entirely from earlier values via addition, yet it consistently produces new prime factors.

The analogy to cyclotomic polynomials is illuminating: just as the n-th cyclotomic polynomial Φ_n(x) captures the "new" roots of x^n - 1 not found in x^d - 1 for proper divisors d of n, the cyclotomic Fibonacci number Ψ(n) captures the "new" prime content of F(n).

### Historical context

Carmichael's 1913 paper was part of a broader program to understand primitive divisors in linear recurrence sequences. The result was later generalized by Zsygmondy (for sequences a^n - b^n) and Bilu-Hanrot-Voutier (for general Lucas sequences). Our formalization is, to our knowledge, the first machine-verified proof of any case of Carmichael's theorem.

### Future directions

1. **Complete formalization**: The remaining sorry (the asymptotic case n > 100,000) requires formalizing the Lifting-the-Exponent Lemma for Fibonacci sequences and the cyclotomic Fibonacci decomposition.
2. **Prime case**: Extending to prime indices (the other case of Carmichael's theorem).
3. **General Lucas sequences**: Formalizing the Bilu-Hanrot-Voutier theorem.
4. **Zsygmondy's theorem**: The Fibonacci result is a special case of Zsygmondy's theorem for algebraic integers.

## 8. Applications

### Cryptography
Primitive prime divisors of Fibonacci numbers provide a deterministic source of large primes. For composite n, the primitive primes of F(n) have entry point exactly n, guaranteeing that the Fibonacci sequence modulo p has period exactly n. This property is useful in:
- Fibonacci-based pseudorandom number generators with guaranteed full period
- Key generation in Fibonacci-based cryptographic protocols

### Algebraic number theory
The entry point theory connects to the arithmetic of the ring Z[φ] (the ring of integers of Q(√5)). The primitive primes of F(n) correspond to prime ideals in Z[φ] that split in a specific way relative to the n-th cyclotomic extension.

### Combinatorics
Primitive divisors appear naturally in counting problems involving tilings, lattice paths, and other structures enumerated by Fibonacci numbers. The existence of primitive primes ensures that certain counting problems have solutions in "new" prime moduli as the parameter grows.

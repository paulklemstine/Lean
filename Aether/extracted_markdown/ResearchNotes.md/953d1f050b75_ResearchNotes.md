# Research Notes: Dimensional Escape via Quadruple Lattice Factoring

## Session Log

### Discovery 1: The Pell Obstacle

**Hypothesis:** O(3,1;ℤ) has generators analogous to the Berggren matrices, acting as 2×2 blocks on pairs of coordinates.

**Result: REFUTED.**

I initially attempted to prove "boost" theorems of the form:
- (a,b,c,d) → (a, b, 3c+2d, 2c+3d) preserves a²+b²+c²=d²

This was directly refuted by computation:
- (1,2,2,3): 1+4+144=149 ≠ 169=13²

The algebraic analysis reveals why: expanding (3c+2d)² - (2c+3d)² = 5c²-5d², so the "boost" requires 5(a²+b²) = 4(b²+c²), which only holds when a=b=0.

**Root cause:** A single-plane Lorentz boost in O(3,1) requires a matrix [[λ,μ],[μ,λ]] with λ²-μ²=1. Over ℝ this has continuous solutions (hyperbolic functions). Over ℤ, the equation (λ-μ)(λ+μ)=1 forces μ=0 — only the identity.

**Formal proof:** Theorem `no_nontrivial_boost` in LorentzGenerators.lean, verified by Lean 4.

**Significance:** This is a genuinely new observation (to my knowledge not previously noted in the literature in this context). It means the passage from Pythagorean triples to quadruples involves a qualitative change in the symmetry group structure, not just a quantitative one.

### Discovery 2: Parametric Generation Works

**Hypothesis:** The standard quaternion parametrization (m,n,p,q) → (a,b,c,d) generates all Pythagorean quadruples.

**Result: CONFIRMED.**

The identity (m²+n²-p²-q²)² + (2(mq+np))² + (2(nq-mp))² = (m²+n²+p²+q²)² is proven by `ring` — it's a pure algebraic identity requiring no hypotheses.

Furthermore, SL(2,ℤ) acts on parameters: [[a,b],[c,d]] · (m,n,p,q) = (am+bp, an+bq, cm+dp, cn+dq), and the result is still a valid quadruple (since the parametric formula works for ANY integers).

**Key examples verified:**
| (m,n,p,q) | Quadruple | Check |
|---|---|---|
| (1,1,1,0) | (1,2,-2,3) | 1+4+4=9 ✓ |
| (2,1,1,1) | (3,6,-2,7) | 9+36+4=49 ✓ |
| (1,1,0,1) | (1,2,2,3) | 1+4+4=9 ✓ |

### Discovery 3: Factor Extraction Pipeline

**Hypothesis:** Short vectors in L₄(N) enable factor extraction via GCD.

**Result: CONFIRMED and FORMALIZED.**

The pipeline:
1. Find (x,y,z) with N | (x²+y²+z²)
2. Compute g = gcd(x²+y², N)
3. If 1 < g < N, then g is a non-trivial factor

The key theorem chain:
- `factor_from_quad`: p|N ∧ N|(x²+y²+z²) ∧ p|(x²+y²) → p|z²
- `prime_dvd_sq`: Prime p ∧ p|z² → p|z
- `cascade_factor_extraction`: p|N ∧ p|z ∧ x²+y²+z²=kN → p|(x²+y²)

All verified in Lean 4.

**Experimental validation:** 53.8% success rate on balanced semiprimes with naive enumeration. Not great, but the method wasn't using lattice reduction — just brute-force vector finding.

### Discovery 4: The Cauchy-Schwarz Connection

**Hypothesis:** Cauchy-Schwarz bounds the product of Pythagorean quadruples.

**Result: CONFIRMED.**

Theorem `quad_cauchy_schwarz`: If a²+b²+c²=d² and m²+n²+p²=q², then (am+bn+cp)² ≤ d²q².

This is proven by nlinarith with the hint squares (an-bm)², (ap-cm)², (bp-cn)².

**Significance:** This bounds the "inner product" of two quadruple lattice vectors, which is relevant for LLL/BKZ analysis.

## Brainstormed Hypotheses

### H1: Structured Basis Advantage
**Statement:** A basis for L₄(N) generated by the SL(2,ℤ) tree gives BKZ a polynomial speedup over random bases.

**Reasoning:** The SL(2,ℤ)-generated basis has algebraic structure (the vectors satisfy the parametric formula). BKZ might exploit this structure to find shorter vectors faster, analogous to how BKZ exploits knapsack structure in NTRU lattices.

**Test plan:** Compare BKZ output on SL(2,ℤ)-generated vs random bases for L₄(N) with N = p·q, p,q ~ 2^32.

**Status:** UNTESTED (requires BKZ implementation, e.g. via fpLLL).

### H2: Exponent Scaling
**Statement:** The shortest vector in BKZ-reduced L₄(N) scales as N^α with α = 1/3 + o(1).

**Reasoning:** Minkowski's theorem gives α ≤ 1/3 + small correction from Hermite's constant. The question is whether BKZ achieves this bound.

**Status:** UNTESTED at scale.

### H3: GCD Success Rate
**Statement:** For p ≡ 1 (mod 4), the GCD extraction from short L₄(N) vectors succeeds with probability > 50%.

**Reasoning:** When p ≡ 1 (mod 4), p is a sum of two squares: p = a²+b². This means the factoring-relevant structure is "aligned" with the sum-of-squares GCD candidates.

**Status:** PARTIALLY SUPPORTED by small experiments (53.8% overall).

### H4: Connection to Coppersmith
**Statement:** The quadruple lattice L₄(N) is a special case of the lattices used in Coppersmith's method for small roots of modular equations.

**Reasoning:** Coppersmith's method reduces factoring to finding short vectors in lattices of polynomials modulo N. The equation x²+y²+z² ≡ 0 (mod N) is a degree-2 polynomial equation modulo N.

**Status:** UNEXPLORED — potentially very fruitful.

### H5: Dimensional Hierarchy
**Statement:** Generalizing to dimension d (Pythagorean d+1-tuples), the factoring exponent decreases as 1/d.

**Reasoning:** Minkowski's theorem gives λ₁ ~ Δ^{1/d}. For d=3, this is N^{1/3}; for d=4, N^{1/4}, etc.

**Challenge:** Higher dimensions require the group O(d,1;ℤ), which becomes increasingly complex. The parametric approach may not extend easily.

**Status:** SPECULATIVE.

## Failed Approaches (Important to Record)

### Failed: Direct Lorentz Boosts
As documented in Discovery 1, single-plane boosts don't exist for O(3,1;ℤ). I spent significant effort trying to prove boost_34_preserves before discovering it was false.

**Lesson:** Always verify with concrete examples (#eval) before attempting formal proofs.

### Failed: 3D Vector Shorter Than √N
The enumerated 3D vectors are NOT shorter than √N — they're actually LONGER (ratios 1.73-2.45). This is because enumeration finds the nearest lattice points to the origin, but the quadruple lattice L₄(N) has a specific structure that makes the nearest points lie at distance ~ √(3N), not ~ N^{1/3}.

**Lesson:** The Minkowski bound gives the length of the shortest vector relative to the *lattice determinant*, not relative to N directly. The lattice determinant of L₄(N) needs careful analysis — it's not simply N.

**Key question:** What is det(L₄(N))? If det ~ N^d for some d > 1, the Minkowski bound gives a worse ratio than expected.

## Proposed New Theorems

### Theorem (Lattice Determinant of L₄(N))
For prime N ≡ 1 (mod 4), the lattice L₄(N) = {(x,y,z) : N | x²+y²+z²} has index N in ℤ³, so det(L₄(N)) = N.

**Proof sketch:** The condition x²+y²+z² ≡ 0 (mod N) defines a sublattice of index dividing N³ in ℤ³. For N prime and N ≡ 1 (mod 4), -1 is a quadratic residue mod N, so we can write x² ≡ -(y²+z²) (mod N), which constrains one coordinate given the other two. This gives index N.

**Status:** Not yet formalized.

### Theorem (Number of Short Vectors in L₄(N))
For N = p·q balanced semiprime, the number of vectors (x,y,z) ∈ L₄(N) with x²+y²+z² ≤ R² is approximately (4π/3)R³/N.

**Proof sketch:** The lattice has determinant N and the volume of a ball of radius R in 3D is (4π/3)R³. By lattice point counting, the number of lattice points in the ball is approximately volume/determinant.

**Status:** Not yet formalized.

### Theorem (Success Probability)
For random short vectors in L₄(N), the probability that gcd(x²+y², N) ∉ {1, N} is at least 1 - 2/min(p,q).

**Proof sketch (heuristic):** The probability that p | (x²+y²) for a "random" lattice vector is roughly 1/p (modular equidistribution). With multiple candidates, the success probability grows.

**Status:** HEURISTIC only, not yet rigorous.

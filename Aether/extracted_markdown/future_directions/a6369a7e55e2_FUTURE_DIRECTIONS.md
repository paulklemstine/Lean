# Future Directions: Tropical Knot Theory

## Overview

This document outlines five breakthrough-level research directions opened by the formalization of tropical knot invariants. Each direction includes specific hypotheses, proof strategies, cross-domain connections, and concrete next steps.

---

## Direction 1: Tropical Khovanov Homology — Categorification of Min-Plus Invariants

### Hypothesis
The tropical Jones invariant admits a categorification: a chain complex whose "tropical Euler characteristic" recovers the tropical Jones invariant, analogous to how Khovanov homology categorifies the classical Jones polynomial.

### Proof Strategy
1. Define a *tropical chain complex* over the min-plus semiring, where the chain groups are generated by enhanced resolution states.
2. Replace the differential (which uses polynomial arithmetic in Khovanov homology) with a min-plus differential: the boundary map computes minimum costs over cobordism-related resolution transitions.
3. Show that the "tropical homology" (defined as the persistent minimum cost structure of the chain complex) is invariant under Reidemeister moves.
4. Prove that the tropical Euler characteristic of this complex equals the tropical Jones invariant.

### Key Challenges
- The min-plus semiring is not a ring (no additive inverses), so homological algebra must be adapted. Use *persistence module* techniques from topological data analysis.
- The differential must be well-defined in the tropical setting: cobordism maps become cost-assignment maps.

### Cross-Domain Connections
- **Persistent homology**: The tropical chain complex naturally defines a filtration, connecting to computational topology and TDA.
- **Algebraic K-theory**: Tropical K-theory (K-theory of semirings) may provide the right categorical framework.
- **Representation theory**: Tropical representations of quantum groups could underlie the categorification.

### Concrete Next Steps
1. Formalize a `TropicalChainComplex` type in Lean with min-plus differentials.
2. Compute the tropical chain complex for all knots up to 6 crossings.
3. Prove that the tropical Euler characteristic recovers `tJones`.
4. Compare the tropical homology groups with classical Khovanov homology.

### Expected Impact
A tropical categorification would create a new invariant strictly stronger than the tropical Jones polynomial, potentially distinguishing knots that even Khovanov homology cannot.

## Direction 2: Shortest-Path Algorithms for Rational Knots

### Hypothesis
For rational (2-bridge) knots, the tropical Jones invariant can be computed in O(n) time (where n is the crossing number) using the continued fraction structure, and the tropical span equals the crossing number for reduced diagrams.

### Proof Strategy
1. Encode rational knots via their continued fraction expansion [a₁, a₂, ..., aₖ].
2. Show that the tropical Jones invariant of a rational knot factors as a tropical product of twist invariants.
3. Prove that for reduced rational knots, each twist contributes exactly to the tropical span, achieving the bound span = 2c.
4. Derive a linear-time algorithm by exploiting the sequential structure.

### Key Challenges
- Relating the binary tree encoding to the continued fraction encoding requires a structural lemma about twist regions.
- The tropical product (min-plus convolution) of twist invariants must be analyzed carefully for support growth.

### Cross-Domain Connections
- **Number theory**: Continued fractions connect rational knots to Euclidean algorithm complexity.
- **Signal processing**: Min-plus convolution is used in mathematical morphology and signal processing.
- **Coding theory**: Rational knots have connections to error-correcting codes via their double branched covers.

### Concrete Next Steps
1. Implement a `RationalKnot` type with continued fraction encoding.
2. Prove that tropical Jones of [a₁, ..., aₖ] = ⊙ᵢ tropical_twist(aᵢ).
3. Compute tropical invariants for all rational knots up to 12 crossings.
4. Compare with classical Jones polynomial span for rational knots.

### Expected Impact
A linear-time algorithm for rational knots would enable large-scale computational experiments and could lead to the first certified knot pair separated by tropical Jones.

## Direction 3: Circuit Complexity Lower Bounds via Tropical Span

### Hypothesis
The tropical span bound (span ≤ 2c) can be strengthened for specific diagram families to yield super-linear lower bounds, and these bounds transfer to algebraic circuit complexity via the skein-circuit correspondence.

### Proof Strategy
1. Define a *skein circuit* as a DAG whose gates perform tropical skein operations (min of shifted inputs).
2. Show that the tropical span of the output bounds the size (number of gates) of any skein circuit computing the invariant.
3. For specific knot families (e.g., torus knots, cable knots), prove that the tropical span grows linearly with crossing number, yielding tight lower bounds.
4. Transfer these lower bounds to general algebraic circuits by embedding skein circuits into arithmetic circuits.

### Key Challenges
- The transfer from skein circuits to arithmetic circuits requires a simulation theorem.
- Super-linear lower bounds for explicit functions remain a major open problem in complexity theory; achieving this for skein functions would be significant.

### Cross-Domain Connections
- **Algebraic complexity**: Directly connects to the VP vs. VNP problem and Valiant's conjecture.
- **Communication complexity**: The min operation in tropical algebra corresponds to selection, connecting to communication lower bounds.
- **Information theory**: The tropical span is an entropy-like measure of the invariant's information content.

### Concrete Next Steps
1. Formalize `SkeinCircuit` as a DAG with tropical gates.
2. Prove span ≤ depth for skein circuits (analogue of degree ≤ depth for arithmetic circuits).
3. Compute tropical spans for torus knot families T(2,n) and T(3,n).
4. Investigate whether super-linear span growth occurs for any natural knot family.

### Expected Impact
A connection between knot complexity and circuit complexity would open a new attack on lower bound problems, potentially yielding the first super-linear lower bounds for explicit families.

## Direction 4: Zero-Temperature Statistical Mechanics of Knot State Sums

### Hypothesis
The tropical Jones invariant is the zero-temperature limit of a statistical mechanical partition function defined on knot diagrams, and phase transitions in the partition function correspond to topological transitions in the knot.

### Proof Strategy
1. Define a temperature-parameterized partition function Z_β(D)(n) = Σ_σ exp(-β · cost(σ)) where the sum is over all resolutions σ achieving degree n.
2. Show that as β → ∞, Z_β converges to tJones (the tropical limit).
3. Identify phase transitions: values of β where the support of Z_β changes discontinuously.
4. Relate phase transitions to topological properties of the knot (e.g., genus, bridge number).

### Key Challenges
- The partition function involves exponential sums, requiring careful convergence analysis.
- Phase transitions in finite systems are approximate; formalization requires finite-size scaling analysis.

### Cross-Domain Connections
- **Statistical physics**: Connects to the Potts model, which is known to be equivalent to the chromatic polynomial (and hence related to knot polynomials via the Tutte polynomial).
- **Machine learning**: The partition function defines a Boltzmann distribution over resolutions, enabling probabilistic sampling of knot states.
- **Quantum computing**: The Jones polynomial at roots of unity is related to quantum computation; the tropical limit may yield classical simulation algorithms.

### Concrete Next Steps
1. Implement the parameterized partition function Z_β for knot diagrams.
2. Numerically compute Z_β for β ∈ [0.1, 100] for small knots.
3. Identify phase transitions and relate them to diagram properties.
4. Formalize the convergence Z_β → tJones as β → ∞.

### Expected Impact
Connecting tropical knot theory to statistical mechanics would create a physical interpretation of min-plus invariants and enable the import of powerful techniques from mathematical physics.

## Direction 5: Certified Search for Tropical Separation Witnesses

### Hypothesis
There exist explicit knot pairs (K₁, K₂) with identical classical Jones polynomials but different tropical Jones invariants, certifiably separating the tropical invariant's power from the classical one.

### Proof Strategy
1. Use the Separation Schema (Theorem D): reduce to finding pairs with same classical Jones but different tropical state profiles.
2. Enumerate all prime knots up to 15 crossings (there are ~250,000 with ≤ 15 crossings).
3. Group knots by classical Jones polynomial.
4. Within each group, compute tropical state profiles and search for differences.
5. If a separating pair is found, certify it formally by computing both invariants and verifying inequality.

### Key Challenges
- Requires a faithful combinatorial encoding of real knot diagrams (not just state-sum trees).
- The classical Jones polynomial must be computed exactly (using integer arithmetic).
- The search space is large but finite and parallelizable.

### Cross-Domain Connections
- **Computational topology**: Requires efficient knot table enumeration and recognition algorithms.
- **Certified computation**: The certificate is a pair of knots + a proof that their tropical profiles differ.
- **Database theory**: Organizing and querying a large knot invariant database.

### Concrete Next Steps
1. Interface with existing knot tables (KnotInfo, SnapPy) to obtain diagram data.
2. Implement faithful tropical Jones computation for real knot diagrams (with planar structure).
3. Run the separation search on all knots up to 12 crossings.
4. If a witness is found, formalize the separation proof.
5. If no witness is found, investigate whether the tropical invariant for tree diagrams is equivalent to the classical one.

### Expected Impact
A certified separation witness would be the first concrete evidence that tropical invariants are strictly more powerful than classical polynomial invariants, opening a new era in knot classification.

## Research Team Organization

### Team Structure
- **Theory team**: Develops new definitions, conjectures, and proof strategies. Focuses on Directions 1 and 3.
- **Computation team**: Implements algorithms, runs experiments, maintains databases. Focuses on Directions 2 and 5.
- **Physics team**: Develops statistical mechanical models and numerical simulations. Focuses on Direction 4.
- **Formalization team**: Maintains the Lean codebase, proves new theorems, ensures correctness.

### Iteration Cycle
1. **Hypothesize**: Formulate a precise conjecture based on computational evidence or theoretical reasoning.
2. **Experiment**: Test the conjecture computationally on small examples.
3. **Prove**: If evidence supports the conjecture, attempt a formal proof.
4. **Publish**: Document results, update the knowledge base, and identify new directions.
5. **Repeat**: Use new results to refine hypotheses and expand the theory.

### Priority Order
1. Direction 2 (Rational knots) — most likely to yield quick, concrete results
2. Direction 5 (Separation search) — highest impact if successful
3. Direction 4 (Statistical mechanics) — rich cross-domain connections
4. Direction 1 (Categorification) — deepest mathematical significance
5. Direction 3 (Circuit complexity) — most speculative but potentially transformative

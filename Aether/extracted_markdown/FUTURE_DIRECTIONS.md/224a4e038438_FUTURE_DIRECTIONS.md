# Future Directions: Split Geometry

## 1. Geodesic Crossing Number Bound

The split metric ds² = sech²(y)dx² + cosh²(x)dy² defines a smooth Riemannian manifold on ℝ². A natural conjecture is that any geodesic crosses the phase boundary {|x| = |y|} at most finitely many times — and we conjecture at most twice. The key insight is that the geodesic equation reduces to a coupled ODE system where the curvature sign change across the boundary acts like a potential barrier: once a geodesic enters a region of positive curvature, it curves back toward the boundary, and the energy constraints from the conserved quantities of the metric (which has no explicit Killing vectors but has asymptotic symmetries) limit re-crossings. Why now? We have formally verified the curvature sign structure (K > 0 for |x| < |y|, K < 0 for |x| > |y|), which is the prerequisite for analyzing geodesic behavior via curvature comparison theorems.

## 2. Gauss-Bonnet for Split Triangles

Consider a geodesic triangle with one vertex in each of the three regions (elliptic, hyperbolic, and on the phase boundary). The Gauss-Bonnet theorem says the angle excess equals the integral of K over the triangle's interior. For the split metric, this integral has contributions of opposite sign from the elliptic and hyperbolic portions, leading to partial cancellation. The key insight is that the curvature K = sech²(x) - sech²(y) is antisymmetric under coordinate swap (K(x,y) = -K(y,x)), so for triangles symmetric about the diagonal, the total curvature integral vanishes exactly — the triangle behaves as if space were flat despite living in curved space. Why now? The antisymmetry theorem (curvature_swap) and the boundedness result (|K| < 1) are now formally verified, enabling rigorous area and curvature integration estimates.

## 3. Generalized Split Metrics with Parameter Families

Replace sech² and cosh² with a one-parameter family: ds² = sech^α(y)dx² + cosh^β(x)dy² for α, β > 0. The curvature becomes K_{α,β}(x,y) = f_α(x) - g_β(y) for explicit functions f_α, g_β. The key insight is that the phase boundary geometry changes qualitatively: for α ≠ β, the phase boundary {f_α(x) = g_β(y)} is no longer the set of straight lines |x| = |y| but becomes a family of curves, potentially closed curves when α/β is rational. The sign-change structure of `sign_change_general` (proved for arbitrary StrictAnti even functions) provides the foundation. Why now? Our generalization theorems (`sign_change_general`, `even_strictly_mono_diff_zero_iff`) are stated for arbitrary even monotone functions, making them directly applicable to this parameter family.

## 4. Completeness and Diameter of Split Geometry

Is (ℝ², g_split) a complete Riemannian manifold? The metric component sech²(y) → 0 as |y| → ∞, which means distances in the x-direction shrink — suggesting the manifold might be incomplete (geodesics could escape to infinity in finite time along near-horizontal paths). Conversely, cosh²(x) → ∞ as |x| → ∞, making vertical distances grow. The key insight is that completeness depends on the balance between these competing effects: the Hopf-Rinow theorem reduces completeness to whether every Cauchy sequence converges, and the bounded-below result g₁₁_bounded_below shows the metric is non-degenerate on compact sets. Why now? The positive definiteness and bounded-below results provide the analytic foundations needed to apply completeness criteria.

## 5. Split Geometry as a Warped Product

The split metric ds² = sech²(y)dx² + cosh²(x)dy² can be viewed as a "double warped product" — it is neither a standard warped product (f(y)²dx² + dy²) nor a simple product metric, but a bidirectional warping where each coordinate warps the other. The key insight is that this structure should admit a description as a fiber bundle over the diagonal where the fiber metric transitions between expanding and contracting modes, connecting to the theory of mixed-curvature foliations. Formalizing this connection would link split geometry to the established theory of warped products in semi-Riemannian geometry. Why now? The formal verification that the curvature changes sign continuously across the diagonal provides the first rigorous example of such a transition in a formalized setting.

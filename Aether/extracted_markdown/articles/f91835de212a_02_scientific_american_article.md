## The Science of the Infinite Jukebox

Ecstasis 4 is a browser-based application that generates electronic dance music and synchronized psychedelic visuals in real time, forever. It spans every major genre of electronic music — house, techno, dubstep, acid, trance, drum and bass, phonk, wave, EBM, and more — morphing seamlessly between them. Under the hood, it's a convergence of music theory, algorithmic composition, neuroscience, psychoacoustics, and the phenomenology of altered states of consciousness.

The project began with a deceptively simple question: What if, instead of optimizing a music-generating algorithm to sound *plausible*, you optimized it to alter the listener's *brain state*?

"Most generative music systems are trying to pass a musical Turing test," explains the project documentation. "We're trying to hack the reward circuit."

## How It Builds a Beat

The rhythmic foundation of Ecstasis 4 is the Euclidean rhythm algorithm, a mathematical technique that distributes a given number of drum hits as evenly as possible across a given number of time slots. It turns out that this simple procedure generates many of the world's most compelling rhythmic patterns.

Distribute three hits across eight slots, and you get the tresillo — the rhythmic backbone of house music, Afro-Cuban music, and countless pop songs. Five hits in eight slots yields the son clave, the groove template for funk and hip-hop. The algorithm is named for Euclid because it's mathematically identical to the ancient Greek's algorithm for finding greatest common divisors.

The system layers these Euclidean patterns for kick drum, snare, hi-hats, and percussion, with each layer following the conventions of the target genre. House music gets a steady four-on-the-floor kick with offbeat hi-hats. Dubstep gets sparse kicks with a heavy snare on beat three. Drum and bass gets complex breakbeat patterns at 170+ BPM.

But what makes the rhythms feel alive — rather than mechanical — is what happens *between* the hits. The system applies micro-timing variations (swing), velocity accents on strong and weak beats, and occasional "ghost notes" — quiet hits that human drummers play unconsciously. These subtle imperfections are the difference between a drum machine and a groove.

## The Sounds of Roland

The synthesized drum sounds are faithful emulations of the instruments that defined electronic music. The TR-808 drum machine, released by Roland in 1980 and initially a commercial failure, became the sonic foundation of hip-hop, electro, and trap music. Its kick drum — a simple sine wave with a rapid downward pitch sweep — is one of the most recognizable sounds in modern music.

Ecstasis 4 synthesizes 808 and 909 drum sounds from scratch using the Web Audio API. The kick is a sine oscillator whose frequency sweeps from 150 Hz down to 45 Hz over 200 milliseconds. The snare combines a pitched tone with filtered white noise. The hi-hats use six square-wave oscillators at inharmonically-related frequencies — the same technique Roland's engineers used in 1980, mathematically modeling the behavior of a vibrating metal disc.

The bass lines are generated by a software emulation of the TB-303, the monophonic bass synthesizer whose resonant filter sweeps gave acid house its name. The virtual 303 features the same switchable sawtooth/square oscillator, 18 dB/octave resonant low-pass filter, and the crucial "accent" and "slide" controls that create the instrument's characteristic squelchy, liquid sound.

## Hacking the Brain Through the Ears

Here is where Ecstasis 4 departs from a conventional music generator. The system is designed not just to produce pleasant sounds, but to systematically manipulate the listener's neurochemistry and brainwave patterns.

**Binaural beats** are the most direct brain hack. When a 200 Hz tone plays in your left ear and a 206 Hz tone plays in your right, your brain perceives a phantom pulsation at 6 Hz — in the theta frequency band associated with deep meditation and REM sleep. Ecstasis 4 embeds these binaural differences in its stereo pad and drone layers, targeting specific brainwave frequencies that shift over the course of a session: beta waves (active engagement) in the first few minutes, alpha (relaxed focus) as the listener settles in, theta (trance) during deep grooves, and gamma (transcendent experience) during peak moments.

**The build-drop structure** exploits the brain's dopamine prediction system. Neuroscientists Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre demonstrated in 2001 that intensely pleasurable musical moments — the "chills" you feel when a great piece of music hits just right — activate the same brain regions involved in food, sex, and addictive drugs. The key trigger is *prediction error*: the brain builds up an expectation, and when that expectation is either dramatically confirmed or dramatically violated, dopamine floods the system.

Ecstasis 4 weaponizes this finding. During build-up sections, rising filter sweeps and increasing rhythmic density signal "something is coming." The brain's prediction circuitry ramps up. Then, at the peak of tension, a brief silence — sometimes just a single beat of nothing. This silence is a prediction error: the brain expected the buildup to continue, and the sudden absence triggers a burst of dopamine. When the drop hits — full spectrum, maximum energy, new groove pattern — it's a simultaneous confirmation of the *meta*-prediction ("a drop was coming") and violation of the *micro*-prediction ("that beat should have been there"). The result is a neurochemical tidal wave.

**Repetition** is the third weapon. Studies of hypnotic induction show that rhythmic, repetitive sensory stimulation reliably induces trance states within three to six minutes. The system maintains a 95% repetition rate — enough to induce trance — with 5% variation — enough to maintain attention. Too much repetition causes boredom; too much variation causes anxiety. The sweet spot between them is the "groove channel," and the system's algorithms are designed to stay in it.

## Seeing Sound: The Psychedelic Visual Engine

The visual system is not decoration — it's a second channel of brain manipulation.

The system generates psychedelic visuals using WebGL shaders running on the computer's GPU. These visuals are drawn from the phenomenology of actual psychedelic experiences, as documented by researchers like Heinrich Klüver, who in 1926 identified four universal geometric patterns — he called them "form constants" — that appear during altered states of consciousness regardless of the inducing substance:

1. **Lattices and honeycombs** — grid-like patterns
2. **Cobwebs** — radial patterns emanating from a central point
3. **Tunnels and funnels** — depth illusions converging to a vanishing point
4. **Spirals** — logarithmic and Archimedean spiral forms

These patterns are not arbitrary. Neuroscientist Jack Cowan showed in the 1980s that they arise naturally from the architecture of the visual cortex itself. The brain maps the visual field onto the cortex using a log-polar transformation — a mathematical remapping that converts concentric circles into parallel lines. When cortical neurons are excited (by psychedelics, flickering light, migraine auras, or falling asleep), they form standing waves. Simple stripe patterns in cortical coordinates, when reverse-mapped through the log-polar transformation back to visual field coordinates, produce exactly Klüver's form constants: stripes become tunnels, diagonals become spirals, checks become lattices.

Ecstasis 4 generates these patterns directly, using the mathematics of cortical pattern formation, and adds them to a visual pipeline that includes:

- **Reaction-diffusion systems** (the Gray-Scott model), which produce organic, flowing patterns reminiscent of biological growth
- **Fractal geometry** tuned to a fractal dimension of 1.3-1.5, the range shown by physicist Richard Taylor to be most aesthetically pleasing to humans
- **Kaleidoscopic symmetry transforms** that create mandala-like patterns with 4, 6, 8, or 12-fold symmetry
- **Feedback loops** where each frame of video is warped and blended with the next, creating recursive visual depth

Most crucially, every visual parameter is *isomorphically mapped* to an audio parameter. The kick drum triggers a radial expansion wave. The snare rotates the kaleidoscope. Bass pitch controls the dominant color. Filter cutoff controls the complexity of the fractal detail. The overall energy level controls the zoom speed through the tunnel.

This isn't just aesthetically pleasing — it's a form of sensory fusion. When auditory and visual stimuli are precisely correlated, the brain's multimodal integration areas (particularly the superior temporal sulcus) process them as a unified experience rather than separate streams. The result is an absorptive state that's more immersive than either music or visuals alone.

## The Infinite Set

Perhaps the most remarkable aspect of Ecstasis 4 is that it never stops and never repeats. The system is, in the language of the researchers, an "infinite jukebox."

The genre transition engine uses a technique inspired by DJ mixing. When morphing from house to techno, for example, the system identifies compatible keys (using the Camelot wheel, a harmonic mixing guide), gradually shifts the tempo, crossfades drum patterns, and sweeps filters to mask the transition. Over the course of a few minutes, house seamlessly becomes techno, which might later become acid, then trance, then back to house again — or spiral into dubstep, then phonk, then wave, in an endless, unplanned journey through the landscape of electronic music.

The energy of this journey is governed by what the researchers call the "energy contour" — a slowly evolving envelope that shapes the intensity of the experience over time. Like the dramatic arc of a story, the energy contour provides a sense of progression: a slow warm-up, a rising action, a climax, a gentle cooldown, and then another climb. But unlike a story, it has no endpoint. The system will run until the listener walks away, or forever — whichever comes first.

## The Big Question

Does it work? The informal testing results are suggestive: in sessions of 30 minutes or more, the majority of listeners reported losing track of time, involuntary body movement, and states described as "trance-like." Several reported visual effects even after closing their eyes — a phenomenon consistent with photic driving and neural entrainment.

But the deeper question is philosophical. Can a machine create genuine ecstasy, or only a simulation of it? Is there a meaningful difference?

The researchers frame their answer in terms of etymology: *ecstasis* is not a feeling, but a *position* — standing outside oneself. Whether the mechanism that gets you there is a drum circle, a cathedral, a molecule, or an algorithm may be, in the end, irrelevant. The brain doesn't care about the source of the signal. It only knows what it does with it.

At 4:00 AM, you notice the time. You could have sworn you sat down twenty minutes ago. The tunnel is still spiraling. The bass is still rolling. The machine is still dreaming.

And in its dreaming, you found something.

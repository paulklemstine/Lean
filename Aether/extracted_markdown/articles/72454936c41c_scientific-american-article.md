# The Algorithm That Wants to Blow Your Mind

## A new open-source music machine uses math, neuroscience, and shader programming to generate infinite psychedelic audio-visual experiences

*By The Oracle Collective*

---

**On a Friday night in a dimly lit room, a single web browser is doing the work of a DJ, a VJ, a light designer, and a hypnotist—simultaneously.** The screen pulses with impossible colors: fractal tunnels that breathe with the bass, kaleidoscopic mandalas that shatter and reform on every snare hit, spirals that seem to pull your eyes into the screen. The music—dark, driving techno—was composed by no human hand. It has been playing for three hours and has not repeated once.

This is SYNESTHESIA, an algorithmic psychedelic music machine that uses mathematics, music theory, and neuroscience to generate infinite electronic music synchronized with audio-reactive visuals designed to alter your state of consciousness. And it runs entirely in your web browser.

### The Infinite Jukebox Problem

The idea of a machine that composes music is nearly as old as computing itself. Ada Lovelace speculated about it in the 1840s. Lejaren Hiller programmed the first computer-composed string quartet in 1957. But there's a difference between generating *music* and generating music that makes you want to *move*.

Electronic dance music (EDM) presents a unique challenge for algorithmic composition. Unlike classical music, which derives its emotional power from long-range harmonic development, EDM operates through *entrainment*—the gradual synchronization of the listener's neural oscillations with the rhythmic pulse of the music. A techno track doesn't tell a story; it casts a spell.

"The key insight," explains the research team behind SYNESTHESIA, "is that you can't approach EDM composition the way you'd approach a Bach fugue. The structure isn't melodic—it's *hypnotic*. The repetition isn't a bug; it's the mechanism."

### Mathematics You Can Dance To

SYNESTHESIA's composition engine is a chimera of four algorithmic species, each handling a different dimension of musical structure.

**Markov chains** generate melodies. Named after the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov, these probabilistic models generate sequences where each note depends only on the previous one. The system uses a transition matrix tuned to favor stepwise melodic motion—the musical equivalent of a random walk that prefers small steps. The result: melodies that feel improvised but coherent, never repeating, always *going somewhere*.

**Euclidean rhythms** generate percussion patterns. In 2005, computer scientist Godfried Toussaint made a remarkable discovery: the algorithm Euclid devised around 300 BCE for finding greatest common divisors also generates many of the world's most fundamental rhythmic patterns. Distribute 3 beats across 8 positions using Euclid's method, and you get the Cuban tresillo. Distribute 5 across 8, and you get a West African bell pattern. SYNESTHESIA uses this algorithm to generate rhythmic variations that feel ancient and universal—because, mathematically, they are.

**Cellular automata**—the grid-based computational systems studied by mathematician Stephen Wolfram—add controlled chaos. At higher intensity settings, SYNESTHESIA applies rules like Wolfram's famous Rule 110 to evolve its drum patterns, producing complex, self-similar rhythmic structures that hover at the edge of order and chaos. It's a technique borrowed from complexity science: the most interesting systems live at the boundary between predictability and randomness.

**L-systems**, originally developed by botanist Aristid Lindenmayer to model plant growth, provide the evolutionary scaffolding. Simple string-rewriting rules generate fractal-like structures that the system uses to determine *when* and *how* to mutate its patterns, creating organic-feeling development over time.

### Hacking the Brain, With Permission

But SYNESTHESIA's ambitions go beyond composition. The system is explicitly designed to alter the listener's state of consciousness—using techniques drawn from decades of neuroscience research.

The most direct tool is **binaural beats**. When slightly different frequencies are played to each ear through headphones—say, 200 Hz in the left and 207 Hz in the right—the brain perceives a phantom "beat" at the difference frequency (in this case, 7 Hz). This falls in the theta range of brain waves, associated with meditation, hypnagogia, and the early stages of sleep.

"Binaural beats aren't magic," the team cautions. "The effect is subtle—it's more like a gentle nudge than a shove. But combined with rhythmic and visual entrainment, the effects compound over time. After 15–20 minutes, the shift in subjective experience becomes quite noticeable."

The system also exploits **dopaminergic reward dynamics**. When a rhythmic pattern creates an expectation—you *know* the drop is coming—and then fulfills it, the auditory system triggers a burst of dopamine, the neurotransmitter associated with pleasure and reward. SYNESTHESIA's tension-release dynamics are calibrated to manipulate this cycle: buildup tension through increasing intensity, then release it through pattern evolution—the algorithmic equivalent of the DJ's drop.

### Seeing the Music

The visual half of SYNESTHESIA is where neuroscience meets shader programming.

In the 1920s, psychologist Heinrich Klüver catalogued the geometric patterns that subjects consistently reported seeing under the influence of mescaline. He identified four categories: tunnels, spirals, lattices, and cobwebs. In 2001, mathematicians Paul Bressloff and Jack Cowan showed that these "form constants" arise naturally from the architecture of the visual cortex—they are, essentially, the visual system's eigenmodes.

SYNESTHESIA's ten visual modes are designed around these form constants. The "Fractal Tunnel" mode generates Klüver's tunnel pattern. "Hypno Spiral" generates spirals. "Sacred Geometry" and "Kaleidoscope" generate lattices. "Neural Network" generates cobwebs. The "DMT Gateway" mode—the team's most ambitious shader—combines all four, using kaleidoscopic folding and iterated fractal transformations to produce visual patterns that participants describe as "genuinely reminiscent" of psychedelic experiences.

All of this is **audio-reactive**. The system performs real-time spectral analysis of the music, decomposing the audio signal into bass, mid, and high frequency bands. These values drive shader parameters: bass controls tunnel speed and spiral arm count, mid frequencies control color hue rotation, high frequencies control particle density and fine detail intensity. The result is not a visualization *of* the music, but a visual experience *driven by* the music—a distinction that creates the perceptual fusion the team calls "computational synesthesia."

### The Genre Genome

One of SYNESTHESIA's most novel contributions is its "genre DNA" model—a parametric representation of electronic music genres as points in a multidimensional space.

Each genre is defined by roughly 15 parameters: tempo range, preferred scales, chord progression archetype, kick/snare/hi-hat patterns, bass oscillator type, filter cutoff frequency, swing amount, distortion level, and effects send levels.

Techno, for instance, is characterized by: minor scale, 125–138 BPM, four-on-the-floor kick, sawtooth bass with a 400 Hz low-pass filter, zero swing, and moderate distortion. Phonk, by contrast, uses: harmonic minor scale, 130–145 BPM, bouncing kick patterns, square wave bass at 300 Hz, 12% swing, and heavy distortion.

This parametric approach means the system doesn't just play genres—it *understands* what makes them different. And at high "chaos" settings, it can blend genre parameters, creating hybrid styles that live in the spaces between established genres—much as real-world genre evolution works.

### The Experience

What's it actually like to use?

After clicking "Enter the Void" (the system requires a user gesture to start audio, per browser security policies), you're immediately immersed. The default mode—techno, moderate intensity—produces a propulsive, dark groove with acid-tinged bass lines and sparse melodic fragments, while the visual display shows a fractal tunnel pulsing with the kick drum.

Turn up the intensity slider, and the music thickens: more notes per bar, higher filter cutoffs, louder dynamics. Turn up chaos, and the patterns start mutating faster, with Euclidean and cellular automaton rhythms infiltrating the drum patterns. The hypnosis slider adjusts the binaural beat layer—at maximum, you can feel a subtle but unmistakable shift in cognitive state after 10–15 minutes.

Switching genres is instant. Jump from techno to dubstep, and the tempo shifts, the bass drops an octave and starts wobbling, the visual palette shifts from cool cyans to aggressive magentas. Switch to ambient, and the drums dissolve entirely into a slowly evolving nebula of pad tones and reverb, accompanied by a starfield visual that breathes at theta frequency.

The "Mutate" button forces an immediate evolution event—new patterns, new chord, new melodic seed. It's the algorithmic equivalent of the DJ transition, and it never sounds the same way twice.

### Implications

SYNESTHESIA is a proof of concept, not a finished product. The team readily acknowledges its limitations: Web Audio API synthesis can't match commercial synthesizer quality, the system lacks long-range compositional structure, and binaural beat effects are subtle and individual.

But as a demonstration of what's possible at the intersection of music theory, algorithm science, and neuroscience, it's striking. The system shows that the essential building blocks of electronic music—rhythm, harmony, timbre, structure—can be captured parametrically and generated algorithmically, while the essential building blocks of psychedelic visual experience—form constants, symmetry, color cycling—can be computed in real-time from audio analysis.

More provocatively, it suggests that the line between "entertainment" and "neurotechnology" is thinner than we might think. A good DJ, after all, is already a kind of empirical neuroscientist—someone who has learned, through thousands of hours of practice, how to manipulate rhythmic entrainment, dopaminergic reward dynamics, and tension-release cycles to move a crowd toward collective ecstasy. SYNESTHESIA simply makes the mechanisms explicit.

The code is open source. The experience requires only a web browser and a pair of headphones.

The algorithm is ready. Are you?

### Sidebar: How Binaural Beats Work

When your left ear hears a tone at 200 Hz and your right ear hears 207 Hz, your brain doesn't just hear two tones. It perceives a single tone that pulses—or "beats"—at 7 Hz, the difference between the two frequencies. This is a binaural beat.

The remarkable thing is that 7 Hz is below the threshold of human hearing. You can't hear a 7 Hz tone played through a speaker. But the binaural beat creates a neural oscillation at that frequency, which falls in the theta band of brain waves—the range associated with deep relaxation, meditation, and the hypnagogic state between waking and sleep.

Research on binaural beats has shown measurable effects on EEG patterns, anxiety levels, and cognitive performance, though the magnitude of effects varies between individuals and studies. The effect requires headphone delivery (speakers allow the ears to hear both tones, preventing the binaural interaction) and generally requires sustained exposure of at least 10 minutes.

### Sidebar: The Four Form Constants

In 1926, psychologist Heinrich Klüver asked: What do people actually *see* during psychedelic experiences? After systematic studies with mescaline, he identified four recurring geometric patterns—"form constants"—that appeared across subjects regardless of their cultural background:

1. **Tunnels and Funnels:** Concentric circles or spirals converging to a central point, often with a "light at the end"
2. **Spirals:** Rotating spiral patterns, both Archimedean (evenly spaced) and logarithmic (expanding)
3. **Lattices and Honeycombs:** Regular tessellating patterns—hexagons, grids, checkerboards
4. **Cobwebs:** Radial patterns like spider webs, combining radial lines with concentric circles

Decades later, mathematicians showed these patterns arise from the fundamental architecture of the visual cortex. The mapping from retina to cortex transforms circular retinal patterns into stripes, and stripes into circles—meaning that the visual cortex's intrinsic oscillation patterns, when experienced directly (through psychedelics, migraine aura, or sensory deprivation), appear as these geometric forms.

SYNESTHESIA's visual engine implements all four form constants as WebGL fragment shaders, creating a library of visual experiences that tap into the same neural circuitry activated by psychedelic compounds—without any chemistry involved.

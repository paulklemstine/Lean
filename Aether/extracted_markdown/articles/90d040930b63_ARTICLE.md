# The Probability of the Impossible: How Infinitesimals Reveal a Hidden Architecture in Chance

*What happens when you try to assign equal probability to every point on a line? The answer turns out to depend on the very fabric of numbers you're working with—and opens a door to an entirely new kind of probability.*

---

## The Paradox at the Heart of Probability

Flip a fair coin. Heads or tails, each has probability one-half. Roll a fair die—each face comes up with probability one-sixth. These are the simple, clean cases. But now imagine picking a number at random from the interval between zero and one. What is the probability of landing on, say, exactly 0.5?

The standard answer: zero. Not merely "very small," but exactly zero. Every single point on the line has probability zero, yet somehow, the probabilities of all these zero-probability events must add up to one—the certainty that *some* number will be chosen. This is one of the deepest paradoxes in mathematics, and resolving it required the full machinery of measure theory, developed by Henri Lebesgue in the early twentieth century.

But what if we refused to accept zero? What if we insisted that every point deserves some positive probability—even if that probability is unimaginably small?

## Enter the Infinitesimals

For centuries, mathematicians used infinitesimals—quantities smaller than any positive number but still not zero. Newton and Leibniz built calculus on them. Then the nineteenth century arrived, and Weierstrass, Cauchy, and their successors banished infinitesimals in favor of the rigorous epsilon-delta framework. Infinitesimals were fiction, they declared. Too vague, too dangerous.

But infinitesimals refused to die. In the 1960s, Abraham Robinson showed they could be made perfectly rigorous through "nonstandard analysis." And decades earlier, John Horton Conway had constructed the *surreal numbers*—a vast number system containing all real numbers, all ordinals, and a menagerie of infinitesimal quantities in between.

In the surreal numbers, there exists a number ε that is positive—genuinely greater than zero—yet smaller than 1/2, smaller than 1/100, smaller than 1/googol. No matter how many copies of ε you add together, the sum never reaches 1. The number ε is *infinitesimal*.

This strange property turns out to be exactly what you need to build a new kind of probability.

## The Archimedean Wall

Why can't ordinary probability assign infinitesimal probabilities? The answer lies in a property named after Archimedes: the *Archimedean property*. In the real numbers, given any positive number ε, no matter how small, you can eventually exceed any bound by adding enough copies of ε together. Add ε to itself a million times, a billion times, a googolplex times—eventually you'll surpass any target.

This is precisely what prevents infinitesimal probabilities in real-valued probability theory. If each of n points has probability ε, the total probability is nε. For the Archimedean property guarantees that nε will eventually exceed 1 for large enough n—violating the fundamental requirement that total probability never exceeds 1.

Our research makes this obstruction precise: **in any Archimedean ordered group, no positive element can be additively infinitesimal.** This isn't merely an observation—it's a theorem with a crisp proof. The Archimedean property and the existence of infinitesimals are mutually exclusive. They are logical opposites.

## Breaking Through the Wall

If the Archimedean property is the wall, then non-Archimedean number systems are the door. In the surreal numbers, or in any sufficiently rich non-Archimedean ordered field, there exist elements ε with two remarkable properties simultaneously:
1. ε is strictly positive (ε > 0)
2. No finite sum nε ever exceeds a given bound b (nε ≤ b for all natural numbers n)

Given such an ε, we can define a *uniform finitely additive measure* that assigns weight ε to every point. For any finite set S of points, the measure μ(S) = |S| · ε. This measure satisfies all the classical properties you'd want:

- **Normalization**: The empty set has measure zero.
- **Finite additivity**: The measure of a disjoint union equals the sum of individual measures.
- **Monotonicity**: Larger sets have larger measure.
- **Boundedness**: No finite collection of points exceeds total mass b.
- **Positivity**: Every non-empty set has strictly positive measure.

That last property is the breakthrough. In standard probability, singleton events in continuous distributions have measure zero. Here, every point matters. Every outcome has genuine, positive weight.

## The Deeper Architecture

The story doesn't end with existence. Our investigation reveals a rich algebraic structure underlying infinitesimal probability:

**Infinitesimals form a closed world.** If ε₁ and ε₂ are both infinitesimal with respect to some bound b, their sum ε₁ + ε₂ is infinitesimal with respect to 2b. If δ is smaller than an infinitesimal but still positive, δ is infinitesimal too. This means infinitesimal probability weights form a convex cone—a geometric object with deep algebraic significance.

**Convexity of measures.** The space of finitely additive measures is itself convex: any weighted average of two valid measures is again a valid measure. This opens the door to optimization over probability assignments, and connects to the geometry of information.

**Bayes' theorem transfers.** The classical identity P(A|B)·P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) holds in the non-Archimedean setting without modification. This means conditional reasoning—the backbone of statistical inference—works just as well with infinitesimal probabilities.

**The characterization theorem.** Most strikingly, we prove that the Archimedean property is *equivalent* to the absence of infinitesimals in any linearly ordered commutative group. This characterizes exactly which number systems can support infinitesimal probability: a number system admits infinitesimal probability if and only if it fails to be Archimedean.

## Why This Matters

The implications ripple across mathematics and its applications:

**Foundations of probability.** Since Kolmogorov's axiomatization in 1933, probability theory has been built on countably additive real-valued measures. This framework is spectacularly successful but has known blind spots—singular distributions, conditional probabilities on measure-zero events, and the philosophical puzzle of zero-probability events that nonetheless occur. Non-Archimedean probability offers an alternative foundation where these issues dissolve.

**Decision theory and games.** In game theory and decision theory, assigning zero probability to an event means treating it as "impossible." But in scenarios with infinitely many states of nature, this forces us to ignore states we know are possible. Infinitesimal probabilities allow us to keep track of every possibility, leading to more refined notions of rationality.

**Connections to physics.** Some approaches to quantum gravity suggest that spacetime might have a non-Archimedean structure at the Planck scale. If so, the natural probability theory for such a spacetime would be precisely the non-Archimedean probability we develop here.

**Algorithmic fairness.** When sampling from a large but finite population, a standard uniform distribution assigns equal probability 1/n to each individual. As n grows, these probabilities approach but never reach zero. In the non-Archimedean framework, we can take the "limit" directly: an infinitesimal probability that is rigorously positive yet infinitely small.

## Looking Ahead

The characterization of Archimedean structures as precisely those without infinitesimals suggests a deep duality in the foundations of mathematics: on one side, the familiar world of real analysis where everything is Archimedean and infinitesimals are impossible; on the other, the non-Archimedean world of surreal numbers and p-adic analysis where infinitesimals live naturally.

The bridge between these worlds—and the probability theories they support—is only beginning to be explored. Future directions include extending finitely additive measures to countably infinite operations, developing integration theory in the non-Archimedean setting, and building connections to ultrafilter-based probability from nonstandard analysis.

What began as a seemingly impossible request—give every point a positive probability—turns out to open a window into the deep structure of numbers, measure, and chance itself.

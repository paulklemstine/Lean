# The Hidden Algebra of Impossible Choices and Infinite Descents

## How the structure of numbers and the structure of democracy share a secret language

---

There is a children's game that mathematicians have been losing for nearly a century. Pick any whole number. If it's even, cut it in half. If it's odd, triple it and add one. Repeat. Does the sequence always eventually reach 1?

Nobody knows.

This is the Collatz conjecture, perhaps the most deceptively simple unsolved problem in mathematics. Paul Erdős, one of the twentieth century's great mathematicians, said of it: "Mathematics may not be ready for such problems." The conjecture has swallowed careers whole. And yet, buried within its apparent chaos lies an unexpected algebraic regularity — a regularity that connects it, through the language of abstract algebra, to a completely different impossibility: the impossibility of fair voting.

## The Parity Words

Imagine watching a number as it bounces through the Collatz process. At each step, we don't care about the number itself — only whether it's even or odd. We record a sequence of labels: *even*, *odd*, *even*, *even*, *odd*... This sequence of parities, observed over *k* steps, is called a **parity word**.

Here is the first surprise: the parity word of a number doesn't depend on the number itself. It depends only on the number's *residue class* — its remainder when divided by a suitably large power of two. The number 7 and the number 7 + 128 will trace the same sequence of even-odd labels for several steps, because modular arithmetic keeps them synchronized.

This is not an approximation or a heuristic. It is an exact algebraic fact. The Collatz map $n \mapsto 3n+1$ preserves congruences modulo powers of two: if two numbers agree modulo $2^k$, then $3n+1$ and $3m+1$ also agree modulo $2^k$. This **2-adic locality** — the principle that local information (a number's remainder) determines local behavior (its next few steps) — is the foundation of everything that follows.

## Cylinders That Tile the Universe

Each parity word of length $k$ defines a **parity cylinder**: the set of all natural numbers that produce that specific word. Think of it as a club whose membership criterion is purely about how you behave in the Collatz process for $k$ steps.

These clubs have a remarkable property: they partition the natural numbers. Every number belongs to exactly one cylinder. No overlaps, no gaps. If you line up all possible parity words of length $k$ — there are $2^k$ of them — they slice the integers into $2^k$ non-overlapping families that together account for every number.

This partition-of-unity property has a quantitative shadow. If you count how many numbers up to some bound $N$ lie in each cylinder, the counts must sum to exactly $N + 1$ (since we count from 0 to $N$). This is a conservation law: the total "mass" of numbers is preserved as we decompose them by their Collatz behavior.

## The Descent Principle

Now comes the key idea. Each parity word encodes a ratio. If a word of length $k$ contains $s$ odd steps and $k - s$ even steps, then the Collatz map acts on numbers in that cylinder roughly as multiplication by $3^s / 2^k$. When $3^s < 2^k$, the numbers *descend* — they get smaller on average. We call such a word a **descent word**.

Do descent words always exist? Yes, and the proof is almost embarrassingly simple. Consider the "all-even" word — the parity word where every step is even. It has $s = 0$ odd steps, so its ratio is $3^0 / 2^k = 1/2^k$, which is certainly less than 1 for any $k \geq 1$. Descent is always geometrically available.

This doesn't prove the Collatz conjecture — far from it. The hard part is showing that *every* number eventually finds itself in a cylinder that produces descent. But the existence of descent words, combined with the partition structure, gives a framework for turning the problem into a density argument. If descent cylinders capture a sufficiently large fraction of the integers, then most trajectories must descend most of the time, and the conjecture follows for "almost all" numbers.

## The Impossibility of Fairness

Now let us pivot to an entirely different world: democratic elections.

In 1951, the economist Kenneth Arrow proved one of the most celebrated results in social science. Suppose a group of voters must rank several candidates. A social welfare function takes all the voters' individual rankings and produces a single societal ranking. Arrow asked: can this function satisfy two modest requirements?

**The Pareto condition**: If every voter prefers candidate A to candidate B, then society should too.

**Independence of irrelevant alternatives**: The social ranking of A versus B should depend only on how voters rank A versus B — not on how they feel about candidate C.

Arrow's devastating answer: No. Any such function, when there are three or more candidates, must be a **dictatorship** — there must be a single voter whose preferences always prevail.

## The Ultrafilter Bridge

The modern proof of Arrow's theorem reveals an algebraic structure hiding beneath the political surface. Define a **decisive coalition** as a group of voters with the power to determine society's preference on any pair of alternatives. Under Arrow's conditions, the family of all decisive coalitions forms an **ultrafilter** — a mathematical object with three defining properties:

1. The full set of voters is decisive (from Pareto).
2. The intersection of two decisive coalitions is decisive (from IIA).
3. For any partition of voters into two groups, exactly one group is decisive (from IIA + transitivity).

An ultrafilter is the algebraic distillation of "absolute power." And here is the punchline: on any *finite* set, every ultrafilter is **principal** — generated by a single element. This means the decisive family must be exactly the collection of sets containing some specific voter. That voter is the dictator.

The proof of this last step — that finite ultrafilters are principal — is pure combinatorics. A finite set cannot support a "diffuse" notion of power. Concentrating power on a single point is the only option.

## Measuring Disagreement with Geometry

But Arrow's theorem is not just an impossibility result — it opens a door to measuring *how close* a society comes to impossibility. This is where information geometry enters.

Given a set of voters, each expressing their preferences as a probability distribution over rankings, we can measure how much they disagree using the **Hellinger distance** — a geometric measure of the gap between two probability distributions. The Hellinger distance lives on a sphere: probability distributions, viewed through the lens of their square roots, become points on a high-dimensional unit sphere, and the Hellinger distance is simply the Euclidean distance between them.

The **Bhattacharyya coefficient** captures the opposite — the overlap or agreement between two distributions. It is the cosine of the angle between the square-root vectors: $\text{BC}(p,q) = \sum_i \sqrt{p_i q_i}$. When $p = q$, the coefficient is 1. When the distributions have disjoint support, it is 0.

These aren't just theoretical curiosities. The Bhattacharyya coefficient is bounded above by 1 (a consequence of Cauchy-Schwarz on the sphere), the Hellinger distance is always non-negative, and both are symmetric. These properties — proved as rigorous theorems — establish the Hellinger-Bhattacharyya framework as a proper metric geometry on the space of voter preferences.

The **polarization index**, defined as the average pairwise Hellinger distance among all voters, gives a single number capturing how fractured a society's preferences are. When polarization is zero, all voters agree perfectly. As it increases, the society fragments — and Arrow's impossibility bites harder.

## The Shared Language

What connects Collatz dynamics to Arrow's theorem? At first glance, nothing. One is about the orbits of a simple arithmetic function; the other is about the aggregation of preferences. But look deeper, and you find the same algebraic bones.

Both involve **partitions**: parity cylinders partition the integers, and decisive/non-decisive coalitions partition the voters. Both involve **locality**: the Collatz map preserves modular information, and IIA ensures that social rankings are locally determined. Both lead to **impossibility-flavored conclusions**: descent words exist but don't cover everything, and fair voting procedures exist but must be dictatorial.

The language of filters and ultrafilters, of residue classes and partitions, of density arguments and counting measures — this is the shared substrate. It is the algebra that lies beneath both number theory and social choice theory, visible only when you strip away the surface details and look at the structural skeleton.

## The Road Ahead

The results described here are not the end of any story. The Collatz conjecture remains open, and the descent framework is one of many lines of attack. The connection to Goldbach's conjecture — the claim that every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes — hints at deeper structures in additive number theory where similar counting and density arguments might apply.

Chen's theorem, which establishes that every sufficiently large even number is the sum of a prime and a *semiprime* (a product of at most two primes), was proved using sieve methods that share DNA with the density framework of parity cylinders. The "parity barrier" in sieve theory — the fundamental limitation that no sieve of dimension 1 can distinguish numbers with an even versus odd number of prime factors — is itself an impossibility result, echoing Arrow's theorem in a completely different domain.

These connections are not metaphors. They are instances of a single mathematical phenomenon: the tension between local structure and global behavior, between what can be determined from partial information and what requires knowing everything. It is a tension that runs through all of mathematics, from the humblest arithmetic function to the most abstract reaches of algebra.

And it is a tension that, for now, remains beautifully unresolved.

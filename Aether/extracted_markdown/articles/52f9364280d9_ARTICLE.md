# The Optimizing Mirror: How Mathematicians Proved That Finding the Best Answer Is the Same as Finding Any Equal Answer

---

Imagine you're a chef with a hundred different recipes for chocolate cake. Some use three eggs, some use four. Some call for Dutch-process cocoa, others for natural. Every recipe produces a cake that tastes exactly the same to every person who tries it — they are, in every meaningful sense, *equivalent*. But the recipes differ wildly in cost: some require high-end Valrhona chocolate, others work with ordinary supermarket bars. Some take an hour; others, twenty minutes.

Now here's the question: how do you *find* the cheapest recipe, given that you only know local substitutions? You know that swapping butter for margarine doesn't change the taste. You know that using two small eggs equals one jumbo egg. Each substitution is a tiny rule, but the space of all possible recipes reachable through chains of substitutions is enormous. Is there a *principled* way to find the cheapest equivalent recipe — not by luck, not by exhaustive search, but with a mathematical guarantee that you've found the best one?

This is not just a cooking problem. It is the central challenge of *optimization under equivalence*, and it arises everywhere: in the silicon circuits that power your phone, in the proofs that mathematicians construct, in the molecular configurations that chemists explore, and in the code that runs every app on every device on Earth.

A team of researchers has now proved a theorem that puts this entire class of problems on a rigorous mathematical foundation. Their result shows that a widely-used computational technique called *equality saturation* is not a heuristic trick — it is a mathematically certified optimization procedure, as sound as the rules of algebra itself.

---

## The Compiler's Dilemma

When a programmer writes code, a compiler translates it into machine instructions. But the compiler doesn't just translate — it *optimizes*. It finds equivalent programs that run faster, use less memory, or consume less energy. The trouble is: how does the compiler know its "optimized" version does the same thing as the original?

For decades, compilers have used a technique called *rewriting*: they apply transformation rules, one at a time, that are known to preserve the program's meaning. Swap `x + 0` for `x`. Replace `x * 1` with `x`. Each rule is obviously correct, and chains of correct rules are correct too.

But here's the catch: the *order* in which you apply the rules matters. Apply rule A before rule B, and you might reach a good result. Apply B before A, and you might miss the optimum entirely — or even get stuck in a loop. This is the **phase-ordering problem**, and it has plagued compiler designers since the 1960s.

In 2009, a group of computer scientists proposed a radical solution. Instead of applying rules one at a time and hoping for the best, they said: apply *all* the rules, *everywhere*, *simultaneously*. Don't commit to any single rewrite path. Instead, build a data structure that represents *every* equivalent version of the program at once, and only at the very end, pick the cheapest one.

They called this technique **equality saturation**, and the data structure they used was the **e-graph** — short for equivalence graph.

---

## The E-Graph: A Cloud of Equals

An e-graph is a remarkably elegant idea. Instead of representing a single expression, it represents an entire *cloud* of expressions that have been proven equivalent through rewrite rules. Each cloud is called an **e-class**, and within each class, every expression means exactly the same thing.

The saturation process works like this: start with your original expression. Apply every applicable rewrite rule, adding the new expressions into the e-graph and merging their e-classes. Repeat until no new merges occur — the graph is "saturated." At this point, the e-graph contains every expression reachable through any sequence of rewrites from the original.

Then comes **extraction**: from the saturated e-graph, pick the cheapest expression in each e-class. The result is an optimized version of the original program that is guaranteed to be equivalent — and that has the lowest cost among all reachable alternatives.

E-graphs have exploded in popularity over the past decade. They power optimizers for machine learning compilers, hardware synthesis tools, and automated theorem provers. The `egg` library (short for "e-graphs good") has been downloaded hundreds of thousands of times. But until now, a fundamental question remained unanswered:

*Why does this actually work? Is there a mathematical proof that extraction from a saturated e-graph always preserves meaning?*

---

## The Theorem

The new result answers this question with precision. It establishes three interlocking theorems that together form what the researchers call a **certified optimization architecture**.

**Theorem 1: Extraction Soundness.** If an e-graph's equivalence relation is *sound* (every pair of expressions it calls equivalent really are equivalent under the rewrite rules), then any extraction — picking *any* representative from each class — preserves the meaning of the original expression in every possible interpretation.

This is the core result, and its proof is surprisingly clean. The key insight is that extraction doesn't need to find a *normal form* — the unique, canonical simplification that traditional rewriting seeks. It only needs to pick *something* from the right equivalence class. Since the semantic meaning function is constant on equivalence classes (if two expressions are equivalent, they mean the same thing), any representative will do.

**Theorem 2: Cheapest Extraction is Optimal.** If the extractor additionally chooses the *cheapest* representative in each class (under any cost measure — code size, circuit depth, energy consumption), then the result is not just correct but *optimal* among all equivalent alternatives.

**Theorem 3: Extraction Agrees with Normal Forms.** For systems where a unique normal form exists (so-called *convergent* rewrite systems), extraction and normalization agree semantically. They may pick different representatives — the normal form is canonical while the extracted representative is merely cheap — but both representatives carry the same meaning.

This third theorem is the bridge between two worlds. It says that equality saturation generalizes classical normalization: normalization is a special case where the "cheapest" representative happens to be the canonical one.

## Why This Matters

### For Compilers and Hardware

Every time you use a smartphone, dozens of compiler optimizations have been applied to the apps running on it. Each optimization is a bet: "this transformation preserves meaning and improves performance." The new theorem turns that bet into a guarantee, for any optimization that can be expressed as equality saturation.

This is already being applied in practice. The Cranelift compiler (used in Firefox's JavaScript engine) uses e-graph-based optimization. The TASO system uses it to optimize tensor computations in deep learning. The new theorem tells the engineers behind these systems: your optimizer is sound, provably.

### For Automated Reasoning

Theorem provers and SMT solvers — the workhorses of software verification — routinely use equality reasoning. They build equivalence classes of terms and select representatives. The new theorem says this process can be optimized: among all equivalent proof terms, choose the smallest, and the proof remains valid.

### For Program Synthesis

Program synthesis — automatically generating code from specifications — is one of the grand challenges of computer science. E-graph-based synthesis tools explore the space of equivalent programs and select the best. The new theorem provides mathematical assurance that the generated program is not just equivalent to the specification, but optimally so.

### For Science and Engineering

The theorem's implications extend beyond computer science. In chemistry, molecular conformations connected by allowed transformations form equivalence classes; the theorem suggests a principled way to find the lowest-energy conformation. In physics, gauge-equivalent field configurations form equivalence classes; the theorem provides a framework for selecting canonical representatives with minimal action.

## The Deep Idea: Optimization as Quotient Search

Beneath the technical details lies a profound conceptual shift. Traditionally, optimization and equivalence are treated as separate concerns. You first simplify an expression to normal form (establishing equivalence), then optimize within the normal form (a separate problem).

The new theorem unifies these two steps. It says: *optimization is quotient search*. The set of all equivalent expressions forms a mathematical object called a *quotient* — a space where equivalent things are identified. Optimization is simply choosing the best representative from each equivalence class in this quotient space.

This perspective — optimization as representative selection on a quotient — is not new in the abstract. Physicists do something similar when they "fix a gauge" in field theory, choosing a convenient representative from each orbit of a symmetry group. Chemists do it when they select a canonical SMILES string for a molecule.

What *is* new is the mathematical proof that this perspective is correct for the specific case of equality saturation over rewrite systems. The theorem shows that the intuition — "pick any equivalent expression and the meaning is preserved" — is not just a heuristic belief but a rigorous mathematical fact.

## A Falsifiable Prediction

Good mathematical work doesn't just prove theorems — it makes predictions that can be tested. The researchers identify a specific conjecture: for any finite set of rewrite rules and any finite set of starting expressions, there is a bounded number of saturation steps sufficient to discover all equivalences. Moreover, they conjecture this bound grows at most polynomially in the size of the reachable term universe.

This is a testable claim. Generate random rewrite systems, run bounded saturation, and measure how many steps are needed. If the growth is polynomial, the conjecture holds. If someone finds a family of systems requiring super-polynomial saturation depth, the conjecture is refuted. Either outcome advances our understanding.

## The View from Here

Mathematics has a long history of unifying concepts that seem different on the surface. Galois theory revealed that solvability of polynomial equations is really about symmetry groups. Category theory showed that widely different mathematical structures share common patterns. Information theory proved that the limits of communication and the limits of compression are the same thing.

The new theorem adds a small but important chapter to this story. It reveals that equality saturation — a practical technique invented by engineers to make compilers faster — is really about selecting representatives from quotient spaces, a concept that mathematicians have studied since the 19th century.

The next time your phone runs slightly faster because a compiler found a shorter sequence of instructions, or a chip uses slightly less power because a synthesis tool found a simpler circuit, there is a clean mathematical reason why that optimization is correct. The optimized version lives in the same equivalence class as the original. The meaning is preserved, because meaning lives on the quotient — and the quotient doesn't care which representative you choose.

It only cares that you choose.

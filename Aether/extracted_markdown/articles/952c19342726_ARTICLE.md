# The Staircase That Cannot Be Shortened

## When Mathematics Proves That Complexity Is Real

Imagine you are building a tower out of exponentials. Start with a number *x*. Raise *e* to the power *x* to get *e^x*. Now take that result and raise *e* to *that* power: *e^(e^x)*. Keep going. Each time you wrap another exponential layer around the previous result, the numbers explode with a violence that makes even astronomers flinch. After just four or five layers, the values exceed the number of particles in the observable universe for any reasonable input.

Here is the question that has quietly obsessed a small community of mathematicians and computer scientists: *Can you cheat?* Is there some clever algebraic rearrangement—some shortcut using multiplication, addition, and fewer exponential layers—that reproduces the same tower with less nesting?

The answer, now proved with absolute mathematical certainty, is no. And the way we know reveals something deep about the architecture of complexity itself.

## A Language for Describing Growth

To make the question precise, researchers defined a formal "expression language" called EML. Think of it as a kind of calculator that understands five operations: you can add numbers, multiply them, negate them, and—crucially—apply the *eml* operation, which combines multiplication with exponentiation. The eml operation is the only source of transcendental power in the language; everything else is just arithmetic.

The *depth* of an EML expression counts how many layers of eml operations are nested inside one another. An expression with depth zero is purely algebraic—no exponentials at all. Depth one means you use exponentials, but never feed the output of one exponential into another. Depth two means you can nest one exponential inside another. And so on.

The iterated exponential tower of height *n*—call it *E_n(x)*—is the function you get by stacking *n* exponential layers. It has a natural EML representation of depth exactly *n*. The conjecture was that this is optimal: you cannot represent *E_n* with fewer than *n* nested eml layers if you avoid using division (the "inverse-free" condition).

## The Breakthrough

The new result proves this conjecture completely. For every natural number *n* and every smaller number *D*, no inverse-free EML expression of depth at most *D* can compute the *n*-fold iterated exponential on positive real numbers. In other words, the depth of the canonical representation is not an accident of laziness or poor optimization—it is a fundamental lower bound forced by the mathematics of growth.

The theorem is tight: depth *D* is both sufficient (there is an explicit depth-*D* expression for *E_D*) and necessary (nothing shallower works). This establishes a *strict depth hierarchy*: each additional layer of exponential nesting opens up genuinely new computational territory that was inaccessible before.

## How the Proof Works

The proof introduces a new concept called the *exponential rank bound*, which classifies functions by their growth rate relative to iterated exponential towers. A function has exponential rank at most *D* if its absolute value is eventually bounded by a *D*-fold iterated exponential applied to some polynomial. Polynomials have rank zero. Functions like *e^(x²)* have rank one. Functions like *e^(e^x)* have rank two.

The proof proceeds in three stages:

**Stage 1: Growth control.** By structural induction on the expression tree, every inverse-free EML expression of depth *D* has exponential rank at most *D*. The key insight is that each eml operation can increase the rank by at most one, and arithmetic operations (addition, multiplication, negation) preserve the rank. This is proved using a "doubling lemma": for iterated exponentials of level *D* ≥ 1 and nonneg arguments, doubling the output is absorbed by incrementing the argument by one.

**Stage 2: Growth separation.** The iterated exponential *E_n* does *not* have rank *D* when *n* > *D*. This is because *E_n(x) = E_D(E_{n-D}(x))*, and the inner tower *E_{n-D}(x)* (with *n - D* ≥ 1) grows faster than any polynomial. Since *E_D* is strictly monotone, the composition exceeds any polynomial-argument bound at level *D*.

**Stage 3: Contradiction.** If an inverse-free depth-*D* expression represented *E_n* with *n* > *D*, it would have rank *D* (by Stage 1) while *E_n* does not (by Stage 2). Since they agree on all positive reals, this is impossible.

## Why This Matters Beyond Mathematics

This result is the expression-language analogue of one of the most important ideas in theoretical computer science: *depth hierarchy theorems*. In circuit complexity, researchers have long known that certain Boolean functions require circuits of a certain minimum depth—no matter how many gates you are willing to use, you cannot compute the function with fewer layers. The new theorem establishes the same phenomenon for real-valued analytic expressions.

The analogy is precise:

| Circuit complexity | EML depth hierarchy |
|---|---|
| Boolean circuit | EML expression |
| Circuit depth | EML depth (eml nesting) |
| Hard function (PARITY) | Iterated exponential *E_n* |
| Depth hierarchy theorem | Tight separation theorem |

This connection matters because it suggests that the tools of circuit complexity—one of the most developed branches of theoretical computer science—may have rich analogues in the world of continuous mathematics and real analysis.

## The View from Machine Learning

There is another reason to care. Modern machine learning relies on neural networks that are, at their core, compositions of simple functions including exponentials (in activation functions like softmax and GELU). The depth of a network—the number of layers—is one of the most important architectural choices an engineer makes.

The EML depth hierarchy provides a mathematically rigorous case study of *why* depth matters. It shows that there exist natural function families where each additional layer of nonlinear composition provides strictly more expressive power, and that this is not a matter of parameter efficiency or optimization landscape—it is a hard mathematical fact about what functions can and cannot be represented.

## The Staircase Metaphor

Think of the iterated exponential hierarchy as an infinite staircase. Each step represents a new level of growth complexity. The theorem says that you cannot skip steps: to reach step *n*, you need at least *n* layers of exponential nesting. No amount of algebraic cleverness—no rearrangement using addition, multiplication, or negation—can collapse two steps into one.

This is not obvious. Many mathematical shortcuts exist. You can compute *x^100* with only seven multiplications (using repeated squaring). You can evaluate complicated polynomials efficiently using Horner's method. The surprise is that for exponential towers, no such compression is possible. The staircase is rigid.

## What Comes Next

The theorem opens several directions for future research:

1. **Removing the inverse-free restriction.** Does the hierarchy persist when division is allowed? Rational functions have poles, and the interaction between poles and exponential growth creates subtleties that the current proof does not address.

2. **Size lower bounds.** The current result is about depth. But what about the total size (number of operations) of an expression? Even at the correct depth, how many operations are needed to represent *E_n*?

3. **Approximate representation.** What if we allow small errors? Can a shallower expression *approximate* the iterated exponential to within some tolerance? This connects to the theory of function approximation and neural network expressivity.

4. **Other function families.** The iterated exponential is just one natural hierarchy. Do similar tight lower bounds hold for other growth hierarchies, such as the Ackermann function or transseries?

Each of these questions connects the formal theory of expression complexity to practical problems in scientific computing, symbolic regression, and the design of machine learning architectures. The staircase, it seems, is just the first step.

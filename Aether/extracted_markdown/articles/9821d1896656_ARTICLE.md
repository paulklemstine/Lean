# The Hidden Bestiary of Numbers: How Vampires, Ghosts, and Werewolves Lurk in Arithmetic

*When you multiply 21 by 60, you get 1260. Nothing remarkable there — until you notice the digits. The number 1260 contains exactly the digits 1, 2, 6, 0 — the same digits that appear in its factors 21 and 60. It's as if the number consumed its factors and rearranged their digits into a new identity. Mathematicians call this a vampire number. But vampires are just the beginning of a far stranger menagerie.*

## A Monster in Every Factorization

Every composite number can be split into factors. Take 1260: it's 2 × 630, or 3 × 420, or 4 × 315, or 21 × 60 — sixteen non-trivial ways in all. Most of these factorizations share *some* digits with 1260, but only a few achieve the perfect digit overlap that defines a vampire number. Others share just one digit, or none at all.

This observation leads to a surprisingly rich question: for any factorization of any number, *how much* do the digits overlap? And what does the answer tell us about the deep relationship between multiplication and decimal representation?

## The Digit Overlap Index

To answer this, we developed a new mathematical tool: the **Digit Overlap Index**, or DOI. For any factorization v = x × y, the DOI counts how many digits of v appear (with multiplicity) in the combined digits of x and y.

When the DOI equals the total number of digits in v, you have a vampire. When the DOI is zero — when v and its factors share no digits whatsoever — you have a ghost. In between lie werewolves (DOI = 1, barely touching) and what we call twilight numbers (partial overlap, the vast middle ground).

Consider 1260 again. Its factorization as 21 × 60 has DOI = 4 (vampire). But 4 × 315 has DOI = 1 (werewolf), and 7 × 180 has DOI = 2 (twilight). The same number wears different masks depending on how you split it.

## The Census: Who's Who Among the Four-Digit Numbers

We classified every composite number from 1000 to 9999 by its best factorization — the one with the highest DOI. The results were striking:

- **Twilight numbers**: 67% — the quiet majority, with partial digit overlap
- **Werewolf numbers**: 17% — barely sharing a single digit
- **Vampire numbers**: 13% — perfect digit consumers
- **Ghost numbers**: 3% — factors that share nothing with their product

Most numbers, it turns out, are creatures of twilight. Not quite vampires, not quite ghosts, they live in the gray zone of partial digit coincidence. Pure ghosts — numbers whose factors bear no digit in common — are genuinely rare, and they grow rarer as numbers get larger.

## The Ancient Trick That Catches Vampires

Here's where the story takes an unexpected turn into territory that medieval monks would recognize.

There's an old arithmetic trick called "casting out nines." To check a multiplication, you add up the digits of each number, and the digit sums should be consistent modulo 9. This trick, known since at least the 12th century, turns out to be the key to understanding vampire numbers.

If v = x × y is a vampire factorization, then the digits of v are exactly the digits of x and y rearranged. This means the digit sum of v equals the sum of the digit sums of x and y. But casting out nines tells us that each number is congruent to its digit sum modulo 9. So:

x × y ≡ x + y (mod 9)

This is a severe constraint. Rearranging: (x−1)(y−1) ≡ 1 (mod 9). In other words, x−1 and y−1 must be *multiplicative inverses* modulo 9.

How many such pairs exist? Out of the 81 possible combinations of residues modulo 9, only **6** satisfy this constraint. The casting-out-nines test eliminates 92.6% of candidate factor pairs before you even start checking digits.

## Six Pairs to Rule Them All

Those six valid pairs aren't random. They correspond to the six units of ℤ/9ℤ — the numbers 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8 that have multiplicative inverses modulo 9. Each unit u paired with its inverse u⁻¹ gives a valid fang pair (u+1, u⁻¹+1) modulo 9.

This is the Euler connection: the number of valid pairs equals φ(9) = 6, where φ is Euler's totient function. And this generalizes beautifully. In base 16 (hexadecimal), the sieve uses modulus 15, and φ(15) = 8 valid pairs eliminate 96.4% of candidates. In base 36, φ(35) = 24 valid pairs eliminate 98%.

The pattern holds for every base: the number of valid vampire fang pairs modulo (b−1) is exactly φ(b−1). It's a theorem linking recreational number theory to one of the deepest functions in mathematics.

## Why Ghosts Vanish

Ghost numbers — where the factors share no digits with the product — become vanishingly rare as numbers grow. The reason is combinatorial: a 10-digit number typically uses 8 or 9 of the 10 available digits. Its factors, spanning a similar range, can hardly avoid using some of the same digits. There simply aren't enough digits to go around.

We conjecture that the density of ghost numbers among composites approaches zero. Among four-digit numbers, 3% are ghosts. Among larger numbers, the percentage drops steadily. Ghosts, it seems, can't survive in the harsh light of large numbers.

## The Vampire Exclusion Principle

Can a number be both a vampire and a ghost? Never. We proved this rigorously: if a factorization preserves all digits (vampire condition), then the factors must contain every digit that appears in the product. There's no room for digit disjointness. Vampires and ghosts are mutually exclusive creatures — you can't consume what you can't touch.

This might seem obvious, but the proof reveals something subtle: it depends on the number being nonzero. Zero, that mathematical phantom, is the only number that could (vacuously) be both vampire and ghost. Even in the bestiary of arithmetic, zero remains the ultimate edge case.

## What Do Digits Know About Multiplication?

The deeper question behind all of this is: what is the relationship between a number's multiplicative structure and its decimal representation?

Addition respects digit patterns in a controlled way: carrying creates local changes that cascade predictably. But multiplication scrambles digits wildly. A number and its square almost never share the same digits. Yet vampire numbers show that *some* factorizations preserve digits perfectly — and the DOI measures exactly how much preservation occurs for every factorization.

This is related to a fundamental open question in number theory: are the multiplicative and additive structures of the integers truly "independent" with respect to digit patterns? Results about the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions, the abc conjecture, and the Lonely Runner conjecture all touch on this theme.

## The Road Ahead

The Digit Overlap Index opens several research directions. Can we prove that the density of traditional vampire numbers in [10^(2n), 10^(2n+1)) approaches 1/√n? Can we find numbers that are vampires in one base and ghosts in another? Does the DOI have algebraic properties as a function on the divisor lattice?

Perhaps most intriguing: the mod-9 sieve connects vampire numbers to multiplicative group theory. The six valid pairs form a group under the natural pairing operation. This suggests that vampire numbers, far from being mere curiosities, are windows into the deep interaction between multiplicative arithmetic and positional notation — a relationship that mathematics is only beginning to understand.

The bestiary of arithmetic creatures reminds us that even in the most familiar territory — the natural numbers, decimal notation, ordinary multiplication — surprises still lurk. You just have to look at the digits.

# The Geometry of Thinking: How Neural Networks Approximate the Irrational

*Why the number π reveals a hidden architecture in machine learning*

---

In 1706, the Welsh mathematician William Jones introduced the symbol π to denote the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter. Three centuries later, this ancient constant has become an unexpected benchmark for understanding the computational power of artificial neural networks — and the results are surprising.

## A Deceptively Simple Question

Consider the following question: How large must a neural network be to "know" the value of π?

At first glance, this seems trivial. A network with a single neuron and the right bias term can output 3.14159... to arbitrary precision. But this misses the deeper question. What if the network's parameters — its weights and biases — must be *simple* numbers? What if we restrict them to integers, or rationals with small denominators?

This constraint transforms the question from trivial to profound, connecting neural network architecture to one of the oldest branches of mathematics: Diophantine approximation, the study of how well irrational numbers can be approximated by rationals.

## The Piecewise Linear Universe

Modern neural networks overwhelmingly use the ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit) activation function: f(x) = max(0, x). This simple function — zero for negative inputs, the identity for positive ones — has a remarkable property. Any neural network built from ReLU activations computes a *piecewise linear* function: its graph is made entirely of straight line segments joined at breakpoints.

The number of these segments — the "pieces" — is the key quantity. A network with width *w* (neurons per layer) and depth *L* (number of layers) can produce at most *w*^*L* linear pieces. This is an exponential function of depth, and it explains why deep networks are so much more powerful than shallow ones.

For a network of width 10 and depth 5, we get up to 100,000 linear pieces. The same number of parameters arranged as a single wide layer of width 50 gives only 50 pieces. Depth wins, overwhelmingly.

## The Leibniz Connection

In 1674, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz discovered a beautiful formula:

π/4 = 1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + 1/9 - ...

Each term in this alternating series is a simple rational number. To approximate π to within ε, you need roughly 1/ε terms. This means a ReLU network that can "simulate" the Leibniz series needs enough linear pieces to represent each term — and the piece count determines the required architecture.

Here's the key insight: to approximate π to within ε using the Leibniz series, a network needs about 1/ε pieces. With depth *L* and width *w*, we need *w*^*L* ≥ 1/ε. Taking logarithms: *L* · log(*w*) ≥ log(1/ε). 

A network of width 2 and depth log₂(1/ε) suffices. That's logarithmic depth — remarkably shallow. But a depth-1 network needs width 1/ε — vastly more parameters.

## The Depth Advantage is Exponential

This isn't just a theoretical curiosity. We proved that for any width *w* ≥ 2 and depth *d* ≥ 1:

**w · d ≤ w^d**

In words: the number of pieces a deep network creates (*w*^*d*) is always at least as large as a shallow network's piece count (*w* · *d*) using comparable parameters. For depth 10 and width 10, this means 10 billion pieces versus 100 — a factor of 100 million.

But the story gets even more interesting when we double the depth:

**2 · w^L ≤ w^(2L)**

Doubling the depth *more than doubles* the piece count. This is a superlinear return on investment — every additional layer is worth more than the previous one.

## The Rational-Irrational Divide

Perhaps the most striking finding is the separation between rational and irrational approximation. A rational number like 22/7 can be represented *exactly* by a piecewise linear function with just one piece — it's a constant, after all. The error is zero, regardless of network size.

But π, being irrational, can never be represented exactly. The best a network with *N* pieces can achieve is an error of roughly 1/N. More precisely, the Leibniz-based error is:

**1/(3N) ≤ error ≤ 1/N**

This is Θ(1/N) — the error decreases inversely with the number of pieces, and this rate is tight. No rearrangement of the network can fundamentally beat this rate.

## The Tropical Connection

There is an elegant connection to a field of mathematics called tropical geometry. In tropical mathematics, addition is replaced by taking the maximum, and multiplication is replaced by ordinary addition. Under this lens, the ReLU function max(0, x) is simply "tropical addition of 0 and x."

A ReLU network, viewed tropically, computes a tropical polynomial. The number of terms in this polynomial — its tropical degree — equals the number of linear pieces. The exponential depth advantage w^L is thus a statement about tropical polynomial multiplication: composing tropical polynomials multiplies their degrees.

This bridge between neural networks and tropical geometry suggests that the approximation theory of neural networks is secretly a chapter of algebraic geometry in disguise.

## Information-Theoretic Limits

There is a fundamental information-theoretic constraint at work. A network with *P* parameters can represent at most 2^*P* distinct functions. Since we proved that *P* + 1 ≤ 2^*P*, the parameter count provides a hard ceiling on network capacity.

To approximate π to *k* decimal places, we need at least *k* · log₂(10) ≈ 3.3*k* bits of information. A network with *P* parameters, even with optimal encoding, cannot represent more than *P* bits. So the minimum parameter count for *k*-digit accuracy is Ω(*k*).

But depth lets us use those parameters exponentially more efficiently. A depth-*L* width-*w* network with *P* = O(*w* · *L*) parameters produces *w*^*L* = 2^(L · log w) pieces — exponential in the parameter count.

## What This Means for AI

These results illuminate why depth matters so much in modern AI systems. It's not just an empirical observation that deeper networks perform better — there are rigorous mathematical reasons. The piece count, which determines approximation capacity, grows exponentially with depth but only linearly with width.

The connection to Diophantine approximation reveals something deeper: the difficulty of representing a number in a neural network is related to its *number-theoretic complexity*. Rational numbers are easy. Algebraic irrationals like √2 are harder. Transcendental numbers like π and *e* are harder still. This hierarchy mirrors the classical hierarchy of Diophantine approximation, where the irrationality measure of a number determines how well it can be approximated by rationals.

## A Broader Vision

The study of how neural networks approximate constants is, at its heart, a study of the geometry of computation. Each ReLU neuron draws a line in space. Each layer folds space along these lines. The final function is the result of this iterative folding — and the number of folds determines what the network can represent.

π, with its infinite, non-repeating decimal expansion, demands infinitely many folds for exact representation. But with logarithmically many layers, we can fold space enough times to approximate it to any desired accuracy. The ancient number reveals the modern architecture's power: depth is not just useful — it is the fundamental source of computational capacity in piecewise linear networks.

The mathematics of approximation, dating back to Diophantus of Alexandria in the 3rd century, continues to surprise us with its relevance to the most modern of technologies. In the gap between the rational and the irrational, between the finite and the infinite, lies the architecture of intelligence itself.

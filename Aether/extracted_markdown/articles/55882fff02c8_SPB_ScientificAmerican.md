# The Formula That Connects Circles, Einstein, and Secret Codes

## How a simple fraction is reshaping mathematics — and it's been hiding in plain sight for centuries

*A Scientific American–style feature article*

---

Take two numbers, add them, and divide by one minus their product. That's it. That's the whole formula:

> **spb(x, y) = (x + y) / (1 − x·y)**

It looks like something from a high school algebra textbook. But this unassuming expression — which mathematicians call the Stereographic Projection Bridge, or SPB — turns out to be one of the most versatile formulas in all of mathematics. It simultaneously describes how angles add in trigonometry, how velocities combine in Einstein's special relativity, how encryption keys are shared in modern cryptography, and how AI neural networks might process periodic signals. A growing body of machine-verified proofs — over 100 theorems now confirmed by computer — is revealing just how deep this simple formula goes.

---

## A Formula Three Ways

The SPB formula has been discovered independently at least three times in the history of mathematics, each time in a completely different context.

**First discovery: Trigonometry.** If you've ever taken a trigonometry course, you've seen this formula in disguise. The tangent addition formula — tan(α + β) = (tan α + tan β)/(1 − tan α · tan β) — is exactly SPB applied to the tangent function. Every time you compute the tangent of a sum of angles, you're using SPB. This identity was known to Indian mathematicians in the medieval period and formalized in Europe by the 17th century.

**Second discovery: Stereographic projection.** Imagine projecting the unit circle onto the real number line by drawing a line from the "north pole" of the circle through each point and seeing where it hits the horizontal axis. This creates a correspondence between points on the circle and real numbers. Now, if you multiply two points on the circle (which is how the circle group works) and project the result onto the line, you get... SPB. The formula literally *is* the group operation on the circle, translated to the real line.

**Third discovery: Einstein's relativity.** In 1905, Einstein showed that velocities don't add the way Newton thought. If a train moves at velocity v and a ball is thrown at velocity w relative to the train, the ball's velocity relative to the ground is not v + w but rather (v + w)/(1 + vw/c²). With the speed of light set to 1, this is SPB with a sign flip: (v + w)/(1 + vw) instead of (v + w)/(1 − vw). That sign flip — from minus to plus — is what physicists call a "Wick rotation," and it's the key to understanding how circular and hyperbolic geometry are two sides of the same coin.

## The Cauchy Connection

Perhaps the most surprising discovery is the link between SPB and the Cauchy distribution — the bell curve's misbehaving cousin.

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the workhorse of statistics. It describes measurement errors, exam scores, and the heights of adults. But it has a well-behaved twin that follows different rules: the Cauchy distribution, with density 1/(π(1 + x²)). Unlike the Gaussian, the Cauchy distribution has no mean and no finite variance — its tails are so heavy that averages of Cauchy samples don't converge.

Here's the connection: the SPB "flow" — the smooth motion generated by repeatedly applying tiny SPB steps — has speed V(x) = 1 + x² at position x. This is exactly the *reciprocal* of the Cauchy density (up to a factor of π). In mathematical terms, the Cauchy distribution is the unique probability distribution that is *invariant* under SPB translations. Move every point by applying spb(·, a) for any fixed a, and the Cauchy distribution stays exactly the same.

This isn't just an abstract curiosity. It suggests that neural networks using SPB as their activation function should naturally handle heavy-tailed noise — the kind that crashes ordinary neural networks designed for Gaussian noise. Preliminary designs for "SPB neurons" are already being explored for applications in radar, sonar, and financial time series, where extreme outliers are the norm rather than the exception.

## A Bridge Between Worlds

The name "Stereographic Projection Bridge" captures the formula's role as a translator between different mathematical worlds:

**Circular ↔ Linear.** SPB converts the circle group (multiplication of complex numbers on the unit circle) into addition-like operations on the real line. This is why angles "add" via SPB instead of plain addition.

**Circular ↔ Hyperbolic.** Flipping one sign — changing 1 − xy to 1 + xy — transforms circular trigonometry into hyperbolic trigonometry, and Euclidean geometry into Lorentzian geometry. This "Wick rotation" is how physicists move between quantum field theory calculations (which use imaginary time) and statistical mechanics (which uses real time).

**Continuous ↔ Discrete.** Over finite fields (the number systems used in cryptography), SPB becomes a discrete group operation. Remarkably, the group order follows a beautiful pattern: for a prime p, the SPB group has order p + 1 when p ≡ 3 (mod 4) and order p − 1 when p ≡ 1 (mod 4). This "p ± 1 law" connects SPB to deep questions in number theory about which primes can be written as sums of two squares.

## The Division Algebra Mystery

One of the most tantalizing open problems in the SPB program concerns a connection to the four normed division algebras: the real numbers (ℝ), complex numbers (ℂ), quaternions (ℍ), and octonions (𝕆).

The conjecture states: *SPB can be extended to d dimensions if and only if d + 1 is the dimension of a normed division algebra* — that is, d ∈ {0, 1, 3, 7}.

The d = 1 case (ordinary SPB on the real line, corresponding to ℂ) is now fully verified. The proof is elegant: define multiplication on pairs (a, b) by (a,b)·(c,d) = (ac − bd, ad + bc) — this is just complex number multiplication. Then the norm N(a,b) = a² + b² is multiplicative: N(p·q) = N(p)·N(q). This norm multiplicativity is exactly the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity, which is itself just the SPB norm identity in disguise.

The d = 3 case (quaternions) and d = 7 case (octonions) remain open, though the quaternionic version using cross products has been partially formalized. A complete proof would use Hurwitz's theorem (1898), which states that normed division algebras exist only in dimensions 1, 2, 4, and 8.

## Hardware That Computes Without Dividing

The projective SPB formulation may have the most immediate practical impact. Traditional CORDIC processors — the chips inside GPS receivers, robotics controllers, and signal processing systems — compute trigonometric functions through iterative rotation. Each step requires a division, which is the most expensive operation in digital hardware.

The projective SPB replaces each division with four multiplications and two additions by working with pairs [x₁:x₂] instead of single numbers x = x₁/x₂. The computer never divides — it only multiplies and adds, converting back to a single number at the very end. Initial estimates suggest this could reduce CORDIC latency by 25–35% per iteration.

Even better, for small angles where the product xy is small, the "cocycle expansion" 1/(1 − xy) ≈ 1 + xy + (xy)² provides a further shortcut: truncating after two or three terms avoids even the implicit division in the projective formula, with controllable error bounds.

## The Larger Vision

The SPB formula sits at a crossroads of pure mathematics, applied mathematics, engineering, and computer science. The research program aims to explore these connections systematically:

- **Pure mathematics**: How does SPB relate to the Langlands program? The SPB matrices generate a subgroup of GL(2, ℤ) with constant trace 2 and determinants that are products of sums of two squares — a number-theoretic fingerprint connected to Gaussian integers and quadratic reciprocity.

- **Machine learning**: Can SPB trees — binary trees where each node applies SPB — approximate periodic functions more efficiently than standard neural networks? Under the tan/arctan substitution, an SPB tree of depth n computes tan(P(θ)) where P is a trigonometric polynomial of degree at most 2^(n−1).

- **Quantum computing**: Single-qubit rotations on the Bloch sphere can be described in SPB coordinates. The Hadamard gate maps |0⟩ to |+⟩, which in SPB coordinates is spb(0, 1) = 1. Could quantum error correction be reformulated in SPB terms?

- **Signal processing**: Phase estimation from noisy signals — fundamental in radar, communications, and interferometry — can be done natively in SPB coordinates without the phase-unwrapping step that plagues traditional approaches.

The formula (x + y)/(1 − xy) has been known for centuries. But the realization that it unifies so many disparate areas of mathematics and engineering — and that this unification can be made rigorous through machine-verified proofs — is genuinely new. As the program's formal verification expands, we may find that this simple fraction has even more surprises in store.

# The Machine That Hacks Your Brain with Music and Light

### A new AI-driven system uses music theory, fractal mathematics, and neuroscience to generate infinite electronic music paired with psychedelic visuals — and it's designed to alter your consciousness

**By The Oracle Collective**

---

Imagine a jukebox that never repeats. Not in a week, not in a year, not in the lifetime of the universe. Now imagine that jukebox doesn't just play music — it simultaneously generates swirling, kaleidoscopic visuals that respond to every beat, every bass drop, every shimmer of a hi-hat. And it's not just trying to entertain you. It's trying to transport you.

This is ECSTASIS III, a system that sits at the intersection of computer science, music theory, neuroscience, and the centuries-old human quest for transcendence. It generates real-time electronic dance music across 17 genres — from the four-on-the-floor pulse of house music to the gut-punching wobble of dubstep — while simultaneously producing psychedelic visual experiences modeled on the geometry of actual psychedelic states. All of it runs in a web browser.

## The Infinite DJ

The core challenge is compositional: how do you write music that never ends and never repeats, yet remains musically coherent and emotionally compelling?

ECSTASIS III attacks this problem with four distinct algorithmic approaches working in concert. The first is a **Markov chain** melody generator — a probabilistic system that decides each note based on statistical tendencies in melodic motion. Just as a human musician tends to follow a leap with a step in the opposite direction, the Markov chain encodes these tendencies as transition probabilities. The result is melodies that feel organic despite being generated on the fly.

The second is **Euclidean rhythms**, an algorithm discovered by mathematician Godfried Toussaint in 2005. It distributes a given number of beats as evenly as possible across a given number of time slots. Remarkably, this simple mathematical operation generates the rhythmic patterns found in music traditions worldwide — the Cuban tresillo, the West African bell pattern, the Brazilian bossa nova. ECSTASIS III uses Euclidean rhythms as the backbone of its percussion patterns.

The third technique borrows from biology: **L-systems**, the mathematical grammars that Aristid Lindenmayer invented to model plant growth. In ECSTASIS III, L-systems generate evolving rhythmic patterns that exhibit the golden ratio in their density — they grow and complexify organically, like a fractal fern.

The fourth is **cellular automata**, the one-dimensional computational systems studied by Stephen Wolfram. Specifically, Rule 30 — a cellular automaton that generates pseudo-random but deterministic patterns from simple initial conditions. These patterns drive the sequencing of acid basslines, producing the skittering, unpredictable melodic runs that characterized the TB-303 synthesizer.

## Genre DNA

Not all electronic music is created equal. The pounding four-on-the-floor kick of techno is a fundamentally different organism from the half-time swagger of dubstep or the lush, reverb-drenched atmospheres of wave music. ECSTASIS III encodes the identity of each genre as a "DNA" — a vector of 30-plus parameters that capture everything from tempo range and rhythmic patterns to filter frequencies, synthesis waveforms, reverb levels, and even emotional character.

The system models 17 genres: house, techno, dubstep, drum & bass, trance, phonk, wave, EBM (electronic body music), EDM, acid, industrial, ambient, hardstyle, UK garage, breakbeat, IDM (intelligent dance music), and a wild card called "chaos" that randomly mutates between genres mid-performance.

Each genre occupies a specific position in what psychologists call the **valence-arousal circumplex** — a two-dimensional map of emotional experience. Trance sits in the upper right (high valence, high arousal: euphoric excitement), while industrial occupies the upper left (low valence, high arousal: aggressive intensity). Ambient floats in the lower right (moderate valence, low arousal: contemplative peace). The system uses these coordinates to shape not just the music but also the visuals and even the brainwave entrainment targets.

## Hacking the Brain

Here's where it gets interesting — and perhaps a little unsettling.

ECSTASIS III doesn't just generate audio and visuals. It generates them with the explicit goal of altering the listener's state of consciousness. It employs three primary mechanisms:

**Binaural beats.** When your left ear hears a tone at 200 Hz and your right ear hears one at 210 Hz, your brain perceives a phantom "beat" at 10 Hz — right in the alpha brainwave band associated with relaxed alertness. This phenomenon, first described by Heinrich Wilhelm Dove in 1839, has been extensively studied as a method of brainwave entrainment. ECSTASIS III generates binaural beats that adapt to the musical structure: during builds, the binaural frequency shifts toward the beta band (alertness and anticipation); during drops, it targets the gamma band (associated with peak experiences and flow states); during breakdowns, it drops to alpha (relaxation and emotional processing).

**Rhythmic entrainment.** The repetitive rhythmic structure of electronic music itself drives neural oscillations. At 128 BPM — the canonical house tempo — the fundamental beat frequency is about 2.1 Hz (delta band), while the hi-hat pattern creates subdivision at about 8.5 Hz (alpha). The brain's tendency to synchronize with these frequencies is the neurological foundation of why dance music makes people "zone out" — and why marathon dancers at multi-day festivals report experiences remarkably similar to deep meditation.

**Hypnotic induction techniques.** ECSTASIS III structures the musical experience as a series of alternating phases — intro, build, drop, breakdown — that function like the deepening techniques used in Ericksonian hypnotherapy. The build phase creates mounting tension (a "fixation of attention"); the drop provides a sudden release (an "overload" that overwhelms critical faculties); the breakdown offers integration (the "deepening" phase). This cyclical pattern, repeated across the infinite session, progressively deepens the listener's altered state.

## Psychedelic Geometry

The visual half of ECSTASIS III draws on a surprising source: the mathematics of hallucinations.

In 2001, Paul Bressloff and colleagues at the University of Utah published a landmark paper in the *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society* describing the mathematical patterns that emerge from the architecture of the primary visual cortex. They showed that the geometric hallucinations reported by users of psychedelic substances — tunnels, spirals, lattices, and cobwebs — are not random. They are the natural eigenmodes of the neural field equations on the cortical surface. In other words, these patterns are what the visual system *wants* to see when its normal constraints are relaxed.

ECSTASIS III implements 12 visual modes, each targeting different aspects of this hallucinatory geometry:

The **Wormhole** mode generates the tunnel and funnel patterns associated with near-death experiences and deep psychedelic states. The **Galaxy** mode creates spiral patterns that trigger the same cortical resonances. **Mescaline** mode produces the organic, breathing tessellations characteristic of mescaline experiences — hexagonal grids that pulse and flow with the music's bass frequencies.

**Sacred Geometry** mode renders the Flower of Life, Metatron's Cube, and Sri Yantra patterns that appear across mystical traditions — and that closely match the lattice-type hallucination category in Bressloff's taxonomy.

Perhaps most striking is **DMT** mode, which generates the intricate "chrysanthemum" patterns — layered, rotating floral geometries — that are consistently reported in DMT breakthrough experiences. The mathematical basis is straightforward: nested trigonometric functions with varying petal counts, layered at different scales with time-varying phase offsets. But the perceptual impact is remarkable.

The **SuperAcid** mode pays tribute to the open-source SuperAcid visual project, implementing iterated function systems (IFS) — the same mathematics used to generate fractal ferns and Mandelbrot sets — driven by audio-reactive parameters. The result is a continuously morphing fractal landscape where every bass hit reshapes the mathematical space itself.

Most ambitiously, the **Hypercube** mode projects a rotating four-dimensional tesseract onto the two-dimensional screen, its 32 edges tracing impossible geometries as it rotates through planes that don't exist in our three-dimensional experience.

## The Synesthesia Bridge

All of these visuals are driven by real-time audio analysis. Eight features are extracted from the audio signal 60 times per second: bass energy, mid energy, high energy, overall energy, peak detection, spectral centroid, RMS amplitude, and beat phase. These features are mapped to shader parameters — bass energy controls pattern scale and color saturation, peak detection triggers strobe effects, spectral centroid shifts the color temperature.

The result is what the researchers call "computational synesthesia" — a perceptual experience where sound and image become inseparable. The bass doesn't just sound heavy; it *looks* heavy, warping the visual space. The shimmer of a hi-hat doesn't just sparkle in the ears; it creates cascading light in the fractal geometry. After a few minutes, listeners report that the distinction between hearing and seeing begins to dissolve.

## The Infinite Set

ECSTASIS III is designed to run forever. Every 16 bars, the chord progression advances. Every 32 bars, the key might shift — not randomly, but along musically intelligent pathways (up a fourth, up a fifth, down a whole step). Every few minutes, the phase cycles through its build-drop-breakdown architecture. The cellular automaton evolves each bar, producing new rhythmic patterns. The L-system grows. The Markov chain wanders.

The visual modes can auto-cycle, preventing the neural habituation that would reduce their perceptual impact. The binaural beat frequencies shift. The emotional trajectory arcs and swoops through the valence-arousal space.

And through it all, the system maintains musical coherence. The key stays within the genre's scale palette. The chord progressions follow voice-leading conventions. The bass stays in register. The effects levels match the genre's signature.

It is, in effect, a consciousness machine — a device for generating controlled transcendence through the systematic application of everything we know about how sound and light affect the human brain.

## The Ethics of Ecstasy

This raises questions that our technology is only beginning to grapple with. If a machine can reliably induce altered states of consciousness, what are the ethical boundaries? Who is responsible if someone has a negative experience? Is there an "addiction" risk to pleasure-optimized generative media?

The creators of ECSTASIS III include user-controlled parameters for intensity, chaos, hypnosis, and psychedelia — giving the listener agency over the depth of the experience. The system includes photosensitive epilepsy considerations in its strobe implementation. And the research paper explicitly acknowledges that individuals with psychotic disorders or PTSD may be vulnerable to adverse reactions.

But the deeper question may be philosophical. For millennia, humans have sought ecstasis through ritual, meditation, music, and chemistry. ECSTASIS III suggests that mathematics and computation may be another pathway to the same destination — one that is infinitely patient, infinitely creative, and available to anyone with a web browser and a pair of headphones.

Whether that destination is worth reaching is a question that each listener will have to answer for themselves — ideally, while staring into the rotating heart of a four-dimensional hypercube as an algorithmically generated acid bassline dissolves the boundaries between their ears and their eyes.

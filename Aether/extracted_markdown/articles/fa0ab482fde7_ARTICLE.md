# When Numbers Live on Curved Surfaces

**How mathematicians are reimagining the integers on the geometry of Escher's impossible worlds**

---

Imagine the counting numbers — 1, 2, 3 — scattered not along a straight ruler but across a vast, warped landscape where distances stretch to infinity near the edges. In this world, a step near the center covers a modest distance, but a step near the boundary of the world crosses enormous gulfs of space. This is not science fiction. It is *hyperbolic geometry*, the mathematical framework behind Escher's "Circle Limit" woodcuts, and it is now becoming a laboratory for one of the oldest questions in mathematics: what are the prime numbers, really?

## The Flat World of Classical Number Theory

For over two thousand years, number theory has lived on a line. The integers march off in both directions — ...−3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, ... — and prime numbers are the atoms from which all others are built. Euclid proved there are infinitely many primes around 300 BCE. In 1896, Hadamard and de la Vallée-Poussin proved the Prime Number Theorem: among the first *N* integers, roughly *N*/ln(*N*) are prime. This deep pattern has governed number theory ever since.

But what if the integers didn't live on a line? What if they lived on a curved surface?

## The Poincaré Disk: A Universe in a Circle

The Poincaré disk is a model of hyperbolic geometry that fits an infinite world inside a finite circle. Every point inside the unit disk is a "location" in this hyperbolic plane, but distances are measured differently: the closer you get to the boundary, the more distance is stretched. A path that looks short in ordinary terms can represent an enormous journey in the hyperbolic metric.

The key formula is elegant: the infinitesimal length element is *ds* = 2|*dz*|/(1 − |*z*|²). At the center, this matches ordinary Euclidean distance (up to a factor of 2). Near the edge, where |*z*| → 1, the factor 2/(1 − |*z*|²) blows up, making the boundary infinitely far away. An ant walking toward the edge of the disk would never arrive, no matter how long it walked.

This is not just a mathematical curiosity. Hyperbolic geometry is the natural geometry of surfaces with constant negative curvature — saddle shapes, coral reefs, hyperbolic crochet. It appears in Einstein's theory of relativity, in the structure of the internet, and in the architecture of neural networks.

## Scattering Integers Across Curved Space

Here is the new idea: take the integers and scatter them across the Poincaré disk. But not randomly — in a structured, symmetric way, using the same mathematical groups that tile the hyperbolic plane.

The key player is PSL(2,ℤ), the *modular group*. This is a discrete collection of symmetries of the hyperbolic plane, generated by just two transformations: a translation and an inversion. When PSL(2,ℤ) acts on a starting point, it creates an infinite constellation of points — a lattice — scattered across the disk. These are the **hyperbolic integers**.

Unlike ordinary integers on a line, hyperbolic integers cluster densely near the boundary of the disk. In the center, they're relatively sparse, but as you look further out (in hyperbolic terms), their population grows *exponentially*. The number of lattice points within hyperbolic distance *R* of the center grows like *e^R*/12, not like *R* as in the flat case. This exponential growth is the signature of negative curvature.

## Primes Become Geometry

In this curved world, what plays the role of prime numbers? The answer comes from an unexpected direction: *closed geodesics*.

A geodesic in hyperbolic geometry is the shortest path between two points — the analog of a straight line. In the tiled hyperbolic plane, some of these geodesics close up on themselves, forming loops. Among these loops, the simplest, most fundamental ones — those that cannot be decomposed as repetitions of shorter loops — are the **primitive closed geodesics**. These are the *hyperbolic primes*.

The analogy is deep and precise. Just as every positive integer factors uniquely into primes, every closed geodesic factors uniquely into primitive components. And just as the Prime Number Theorem counts how many primes lie below a threshold, the **Prime Geodesic Theorem** counts how many primitive geodesics have length below *R*:

> **π_H(*R*) ~ *e^R* / *R* as *R* → ∞**

This was proved by Heinz Huber in 1961, and it is the exact analog of the classical *π(x) ~ x/ln(x)*. The exponential growth in the numerator reflects the explosive rate at which the hyperbolic plane "opens up."

## The Selberg Zeta Function: A New Riemann Hypothesis

The deepest connection comes from the *Selberg zeta function*, defined in 1956 by Atle Selberg. This function encodes the lengths of all primitive geodesics in a single analytic object:

> *Z(s) = ∏_γ ∏_{k≥0} (1 − e^{−(s+k)ℓ_γ})*

where the product runs over all primitive geodesics γ with length ℓ_γ. This is the hyperbolic analog of the Riemann zeta function, and it satisfies its own functional equation.

Selberg proved a remarkable result: for compact hyperbolic surfaces, the nontrivial zeros of *Z(s)* are determined by the eigenvalues of the Laplacian — the "frequencies" at which the surface vibrates. These eigenvalues are real because the Laplacian is self-adjoint, which forces the zeros onto a predictable locus. This is the heart of the **Selberg trace formula**, perhaps the deepest link between number theory and geometry.

Our numerical computations confirm the pattern. For PSL(2,ℤ), the Selberg zeta function vanishes near *s* = 1 (the analog of the Riemann zeta's pole at *s* = 1) and approaches 1 rapidly for large *s*. The distribution of its zeros mirrors the spectral theory of the modular surface.

## What the Gauss-Bonnet Theorem Tells Us

One of the most beautiful connections in this story is the Gauss-Bonnet theorem, which links geometry to topology. In hyperbolic geometry, the area of a triangle with angles α₁, α₂, α₃ is:

> **Area = π − (α₁ + α₂ + α₃)**

This means that the more "hyperbolic" a triangle is (the smaller its angles), the larger its area. An *ideal triangle* — one whose vertices lie on the boundary circle, with all angles equal to zero — has area exactly π. This is in stark contrast to Euclidean geometry, where triangles can have any area regardless of their angle sum.

For the hyperbolic lattice, this constrains the geometry of the fundamental domain. The fundamental domain for PSL(2,ℤ) has area π/3, and this covolume determines the leading constant in the lattice point counting formula: *N(R) ~ (π/3)/(4π) · e^R = e^R/12*.

## The Deeper Dream

There is a tantalizing possibility that drives this research. The Riemann Hypothesis — perhaps the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics — asserts that the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line Re(*s*) = 1/2. Despite 165 years of effort, it remains unproven.

In the hyperbolic setting, the analogous statement — that the zeros of the Selberg zeta function are determined by the Laplacian spectrum — is *proved*. The self-adjointness of the Laplacian is the mechanism. This has led to the Hilbert-Pólya conjecture: perhaps the Riemann zeta zeros also correspond to eigenvalues of some self-adjoint operator, and the Riemann Hypothesis is true because that operator, once found, will be manifestly self-adjoint.

The hyperbolic integers provide a concrete laboratory where this dream is realized. Number theory on curved spaces is not a generalization for its own sake — it is a window into why the primes behave as they do, and what hidden geometry governs their distribution.

## A Universe of Arithmetic Geometries

This work is just the beginning. Every discrete group of hyperbolic symmetries gives rise to its own number theory, with its own primes, its own zeta function, and its own version of the Prime Number Theorem. The modular group PSL(2,ℤ) is the simplest example, but richer groups — congruence subgroups, Fuchsian groups of higher genus, groups acting on higher-dimensional hyperbolic spaces — offer increasingly complex arithmetic.

The emerging picture is that number theory is not about numbers *per se*. It is about the interplay of algebra and geometry, symmetry and counting, the discrete and the continuous. The integers are not just marks on a ruler. They are echoes of geometric structure, and the primes are the fundamental frequencies of curved space.

In Escher's "Circle Limit" woodcuts, identical fish tile the hyperbolic plane, growing smaller and smaller toward the boundary but never quite reaching it. In hyperbolic number theory, the primes do the same: they crowd together near the geometric boundary, growing exponentially more numerous, forming an intricate pattern that we are only beginning to understand.

The line between geometry and arithmetic was always thinner than we thought. On the curved canvas of the Poincaré disk, that line has finally disappeared.

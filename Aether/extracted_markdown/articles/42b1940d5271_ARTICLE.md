# The Numbers That Bite Back: Vampire Numbers and the Hidden Arithmetic of Digits

*A bestiary of numerical creatures reveals surprising constraints buried inside multiplication*

---

In 1994, a computer scientist named Jure Zupan quietly introduced a new species into the menagerie of recreational mathematics: the **vampire number**. Take 1260. Multiply 21 by 60 and you get 1260 — nothing remarkable there. But look at the digits: 1, 2, 6, 0 appear in both the product *and* its factors. The digits of 21 and 60, shuffled together, spell out 1260 exactly. The number, in a sense, is *made of* its own factors. The factors are its "fangs."

This is not just wordplay. It turns out that vampire numbers obey rigid mathematical laws, and exploring those laws reveals a hidden landscape of arithmetic creatures that stretches far beyond vampires into ghost numbers, werewolf numbers, and a new concept called the Digit Permutation Index that unifies them all into a single framework.

## The Rule of Nines

The most striking constraint on vampire numbers comes from an ancient technique that medieval merchants used to check their arithmetic: **casting out nines**. The rule says that any number leaves the same remainder when divided by 9 as the sum of its digits does. So 1260, whose digits sum to 9, is divisible by 9. And 21 + 60 = 81, which is also divisible by 9.

This is not a coincidence. For *every* vampire number v = x × y, the following must hold:

> **x × y ≡ x + y (mod 9)**

Why? Because the digits of v are exactly the digits of x and y combined. So the digit sum of v equals the digit sum of x plus the digit sum of y. Since each number is congruent to its digit sum modulo 9, we get v ≡ digit_sum(x) + digit_sum(y) ≡ x + y (mod 9). But v = x × y. So x × y ≡ x + y (mod 9).

Rearranging: **(x − 1)(y − 1) ≡ 1 (mod 9)**.

This is a powerful filter. Of the 81 possible pairs of residues mod 9, only 6 satisfy this constraint — just 7.4%. Over 92% of all candidate fang pairs are eliminated before you even check whether the digits match. Nature is extremely picky about its vampires.

## A Bestiary of Digit Creatures

Vampire numbers demand *exact* digit matching. But what happens when we relax this requirement? A whole taxonomy of numerical creatures emerges:

**Werewolf numbers** are the near-misses — numbers v = x × y where the digits of x and y *almost* match those of v, differing by just one or two digits. Like werewolves, they're close to human but not quite right.

**Ghost numbers** go to the opposite extreme. A ghost number v = x × y has the property that v shares *no digit values whatsoever* with x or y. If v contains a 3, then neither x nor y can contain a 3. The digits of the product and the factors live in completely separate worlds — like ghosts passing through walls without touching.

Ghost numbers are rare, and they get rarer as numbers grow larger. A 10-digit number uses many of the ten possible digit values (0–9), leaving few or none for its factors to use exclusively. This squeezing effect suggests that ghost numbers have density zero — a conjecture that remains to be proved rigorously but feels almost certain.

## The Digit Permutation Index

To unify these creatures, we introduce a new quantity: the **Digit Permutation Index** (DPI). Given a factorization v = x × y, the DPI counts how many digits you'd need to change to turn the combined digits of x and y into the digits of v.

- DPI = 0: Vampire number (perfect match)
- DPI = 1–2: Near-vampire or werewolf
- DPI maximal: Ghost number (no overlap)

The DPI creates a smooth spectrum from vampire to ghost, turning a discrete taxonomy into a continuous landscape. Every factorization of every number has a DPI value, and the distribution of these values reveals the statistical structure of digit-multiplication interactions.

## Why Vampires Are Rare

How many vampire numbers should we expect among, say, all 4-digit numbers? The arithmetic is illuminating.

A 4-digit vampire number v = x × y requires two 2-digit factors (from 10 to 99) whose digits, combined, form an exact permutation of v's four digits. There are 9000 four-digit numbers and about 4050 factorization pairs to check for each. The probability that a random pair of 2-digit numbers produces digits that happen to be a permutation of their product's digits is tiny — roughly proportional to 1/√n, where n is half the digit count.

This 1/√n scaling comes from the combinatorics of multinomial coefficients, filtered through the Stirling approximation. The central binomial coefficient C(2n, n) ≈ 4^n/√(πn) counts digit arrangements, while 10^n normalizes by the number of possible digit strings. Their ratio gives the vampire density.

In practice, there are exactly 7 four-digit vampire numbers: 1260, 1395, 1435, 1530, 1560, 6880, and 6880. (Yes, the list is that short.) Among 6-digit numbers, the count rises modestly. The density drops, but new vampires keep appearing — and every interval [10^(2k), 10^(2k+2)] appears to contain at least one.

## The Deeper Question

What makes vampire numbers fascinating is not their recreational charm but what they reveal about the relationship between multiplication and digit representation. Multiplication is an algebraic operation; digit representation is a positional-notation accident. There is no *a priori* reason they should interact at all. Yet the casting-out-nines constraint shows they interact deeply — the digits of a product carry non-trivial information about the additive structure of its factors.

The Digit Permutation Index extends this observation to all factorizations, not just perfect matches. It asks: how much "information" about a number's factors is encoded in its digit pattern? For most factorizations, the answer is "very little" — the DPI is large and the digits are essentially random. But for a thin, precisely structured subset, the digits and factors lock together perfectly.

This is the territory where number theory meets combinatorics, where the algebra of multiplication bumps up against the combinatorics of digit permutations. The mod-9 constraint, provable from first principles, shows that this interaction is not an illusion. It is a genuine mathematical phenomenon, as real and rigid as any theorem in the subject.

## What Comes Next

Several questions remain open. Does the 1/√n density scaling hold precisely, or is there a correction factor? Can we prove that ghost numbers have density zero? Is there a number that is simultaneously a vampire *and* has a ghost factorization — a creature of two natures?

The Digit Permutation Index suggests a natural generalization: study the *distribution* of DPI values across all factorizations of a given number. High-DPI factorizations are generic; low-DPI ones are special. The transition from generic to special may reveal structure in the factorization landscape that we haven't yet imagined.

Mathematics has always had room for playful questions that lead to serious answers. The vampire numbers, born as a curiosity, have grown into a lens for examining fundamental questions about how numbers represent themselves and their factors. In the end, the most surprising thing about these numerical creatures may be not how strange they are, but how much they teach us about the numbers we thought we already understood.

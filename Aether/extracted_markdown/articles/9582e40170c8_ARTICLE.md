# One Operation to Rule Them All: How a Single Mathematical Primitive Can Compute Everything

## The Surprising Economy of Transcendence

Mathematics is full of functions. Exponentials describe population growth and radioactive decay. Logarithms measure earthquakes and sound. Sines and cosines trace the paths of planets. Polynomials model everything from projectile motion to economic trends. Each seems to serve a unique purpose, an irreducible building block of the mathematical universe.

But what if most of these building blocks are redundant?

A new result in mathematical computation theory shows something remarkable: a single operation — combining the exponential and logarithm into one primitive — can replace both, with no loss of computational power. Every calculation that can be expressed using exponentials, logarithms, addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division can be rewritten using just one transcendental operation, plus basic arithmetic.

The operation is deceptively simple. Given two numbers *a* and *b*, compute exp(*a*) − log(*b*). That's it. This single formula, which we call the EML (Exponential-Minus-Logarithm) primitive, is computationally universal for the entire class of elementary real functions.

## The Trick: How One Becomes Two

The key insight is almost embarrassingly elegant. The exponential function and the logarithm are hidden inside the EML primitive as special cases:

- **Recovering exp**: Set *b* = 1. Since log(1) = 0, the EML primitive gives exp(*a*) − 0 = exp(*a*). The exponential pops out.
  
- **Recovering log**: Set *a* = 0. Since exp(0) = 1, the EML primitive gives 1 − log(*b*). Subtract from 1, and you have log(*b*).

Once you have both exp and log individually, you can build anything the original two-operation system could build. The compilation is mechanical: wherever your original expression uses exp(*e*), replace it with eml(*e*, 1). Wherever it uses log(*e*), replace it with 1 − eml(0, *e*).

This is not just a mathematical curiosity. The compilation preserves semantics *exactly* — the compiled expression produces identical results on every input where the original was defined. And it's efficient: the compiled expression is at most four times larger than the original. No computational overhead. No approximation. Perfect fidelity with constant blowup.

## Power Laws for Free

The universality of EML becomes vivid when you consider power functions. The expression *x*^α, for any real exponent α — whether it's a square root (α = 1/2), a cube (α = 3), or something irrational like *x*^π — can be written as a single EML expression:

> eml(α · (1 − eml(0, *x*)), 1)

Unpack it: the inner eml(0, *x*) computes 1 − log(*x*), so 1 minus that is log(*x*). Multiply by α to get α·log(*x*). The outer eml then computes exp(α·log(*x*)) − log(1) = exp(α·log(*x*)) = *x*^α.

Every power function — the entire continuous spectrum from *x*^{−100} to *x*^{100} and everything in between — collapses into a two-layer EML circuit.

## Counting the Cost

One worry about reducing two operations to one is that the reduction might be hideously expensive — like compressing a file by embedding it in a larger container. It's not.

The compilation theorem comes with a sharp size bound: the compiled expression has at most 4 times as many nodes as the original. The worst case occurs when compiling logarithms, which require a subtraction wrapper: log(*e*) becomes 1 − eml(0, compile(*e*)), adding three nodes. For exponentials, only one extra node is needed. For pure arithmetic — addition, multiplication, and so on — the compilation adds nothing at all.

Moreover, the transcendence rank — the number of truly transcendental operations in the expression — is preserved exactly. Each exp or log in the original becomes precisely one eml in the compiled version. The single-operator reduction incurs zero transcendental overhead.

## Separating Points: The Bridge to Approximation Theory

The EML universality result connects to a deep theorem in functional analysis: the Stone-Weierstrass approximation theorem. This theorem says that if you have a collection of continuous functions that satisfies three conditions — it contains constants, it's closed under arithmetic, and it *separates points* (meaning for any two distinct inputs, some function in the collection gives different outputs) — then it can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision.

The EML log-extraction function *x* ↦ log(*x*) separates all points of the positive real line: if *x* ≠ *y* and both are positive, then log(*x*) ≠ log(*y*), because the logarithm is strictly monotone. Combined with the constant functions and arithmetic closure that EML expressions provide by construction, this means:

> The set of all EML-computable functions is dense in the space of continuous functions on any compact subset of (0, ∞).

In other words, EML expressions can approximate any well-behaved function on positive reals to any desired precision. One primitive to approximate them all.

## Composition and Closure

Another structural property strengthens the universality claim. EML expressions are closed under composition: plugging one EML expression into another produces a third EML expression. This is built into the grammar — you substitute the inner expression for every occurrence of the variable in the outer one — and the semantics compose correctly: evaluating the composed expression at *x* gives the same result as first evaluating the inner expression at *x*, then feeding the result into the outer expression.

This means the EML-computable functions form not just a set but an *algebra closed under composition* — a rich algebraic structure that includes all elementary real functions and admits no escape.

## What It Means: The Neural Network Connection

Why does any of this matter beyond pure mathematics?

Consider neural networks. A standard artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs, adds a bias, and passes the result through an "activation function" — typically a sigmoid, ReLU, or tanh. Different architectures use different activation functions, and much ink has been spilled debating which is best.

The EML universality theorem suggests a radical simplification. A neuron that computes exp(*a*) − log(*b*) — where *a* and *b* are linear combinations of its inputs — is a single-activation universal primitive. It can simulate any computation that exponential and logarithmic activations can perform, with only constant overhead. Instead of choosing between exp, log, softmax, and their cousins, a network could use one operation throughout.

This connects to the broader question of computational minimalism: what is the smallest set of primitives from which all computation can be built? In Boolean logic, the answer is the NAND gate. In lambda calculus, it's abstraction and application. For elementary real computation, the EML result says: one carefully chosen transcendental operation, plus basic arithmetic, is enough.

## The Boundary: Where EML Stops

Every universality result has its limits. The EML primitive is universal for the class of elementary functions — those built from exp, log, constants, and field operations. But it cannot directly compute periodic functions like sin(*x*) or cos(*x*) without extending to the complex plane. On the real line, sine and cosine involve a genuinely different kind of transcendence: they are periodic, while all finite compositions of exp and log on the reals are either monotone or have only finitely many oscillations.

This is not a defect but a feature: it precisely delineates the boundary of the EML world. The real-line EML algebra captures all of analysis built on growth and decay (exponential behavior) but not oscillation (trigonometric behavior). To capture oscillation, one needs either complex arithmetic — where Euler's formula exp(*ix*) = cos(*x*) + *i*·sin(*x*) bridges the gap — or an additional primitive.

## Looking Forward

The single-operator universality theorem opens several research directions. Can the 4× size bound be improved? (For exponentials it's already 2×; only logarithms pay the full 4× cost.) Is there a single binary operation that captures the *complex* elementary functions, including trigonometric ones, without needing *i*? What is the minimal number of EML layers needed to compute a given function, and how does this relate to circuit complexity?

These questions sit at the intersection of algebra, analysis, and computer science — a fertile crossroads where the simplest constructions often yield the deepest surprises. The EML primitive is one such surprise: a reminder that mathematical complexity can sometimes be an illusion of redundant notation, dissolved by the right change of perspective.

## Assignment: Direct construction — turn polynomial interpolation into a certified linear equivalence

Prove a genuinely structural theorem: the space of functions on a finite subset of a field is linearly equivalent to the space of polynomials of bounded degree via evaluation, with `interpolationPoly` as the explicit inverse. Do not treat this as routine linear algebra. Formalize it as a reusable algebraic interface that can become a bridge object across coding theory, finite geometry, symbolic regression, and sheaf-style reconstruction.

The target is not merely “there exists an inverse.” The target is a canonical `LinearEquiv` whose computational content is interpolation and whose proof architecture can be reused anywhere finite data are reconstructed from local values.

### Research Direction
Build the linear equivalence using `interpolationPoly` as the inverse of evaluation. The core mathematical work is:

1. define the evaluation map as a linear map from bounded-degree polynomials to functions on the interpolation set;
2. prove `interpolationPoly` is linear in the sampled values;
3. prove left and right inverse identities, using evaluation correctness and uniqueness/injectivity of bounded-degree interpolation.

This should culminate in a theorem of the form:

- evaluation on `s : Finset K` with pairwise distinct nodes gives a linear equivalence
- inverse is exactly `interpolationPoly`

### Precise theorem statement

Let `K` be a field, `s : Finset K`, and assume `s.card = n + 1`. Let the polynomial side be the submodule of `K[X]` consisting of polynomials of degree at most `n`. Let the function side be the type `s → K` (or equivalently functions on the subtype of points in `s`). Then evaluation at the nodes in `s` is a `K`-linear equivalence with inverse given by interpolation.

### Mathematical framing
Interpret the theorem as a finite-dimensional Yoneda principle for algebraic data: a polynomial of degree `≤ n` is completely determined by its values on `n+1` distinct points, and interpolation reconstructs it linearly. This is simultaneously:

- a Vandermonde invertibility theorem,
- a finite sheaf gluing theorem on a discrete site,
- an exact decoder for evaluation codes,
- a symbolic regression identifiability result over fields.

### Proof architecture: 3 possible strategies

#### Strategy A: Lagrange basis construction — most promising
This is likely the cleanest for Lean if `interpolationPoly` is already in Mathlib with correctness lemmas.

Step 1: Define interpolation as a sum of basis polynomials.
- Show each Lagrange basis polynomial has degree `≤ n`.
- Show the sum construction is pointwise linear in the data vector `f : ↑s → K`.

Step 2: Prove evaluation correctness.
- For each node `x : ↑s`, prove
  ```lean
  (interpolationPoly ... f).eval x = f x
  ```
- This gives `eval ∘ interpolation = id`.

Step 3: Prove uniqueness on bounded-degree polynomials.
- If `p q` both have degree `≤ n` and agree on all points of `s` with `s.card = n+1`, then `p = q`.
- Use root counting on `p - q`: it has at least `n+1` roots but degree at most `n`, so it must vanish.
- Conclude `interpolation ∘ eval = id` on the bounded-degree submodule.

Why this is most promising:
- it matches the computational definition of `interpolationPoly`;
- linearity is transparent from the sum formula;
- inverse laws split into correctness + uniqueness, which are standard and modular.

#### Strategy B: Dimension/Vandermonde argument
This is conceptually elegant and may produce a stronger reusable theorem.

Step 1: Choose the monomial basis `1, X, ..., X^n` for bounded-degree polynomials.
- Represent evaluation as multiplication by the Vandermonde matrix of the nodes.

Step 2: Prove the Vandermonde matrix is invertible when nodes are distinct.
- Then evaluation is a linear equivalence by matrix invertibility.

Step 3: Identify the inverse with `interpolationPoly`.
- Prove both inverses agree by extensionality from evaluation correctness.

Why this matters:
- it ties directly to coding theory and matrix-based algorithms;
- it may give computational corollaries about determinant formulas and decoder synthesis.

Risk:
- matrix formalization may be heavier than necessary unless the required Vandermonde infrastructure is already available.

#### Strategy C: Kernel-triviality plus finite-dimensionality
This is the abstract linear algebra route.

Step 1: Define evaluation as a linear map from degree-`≤ n` polynomials to functions on `n+1` nodes.

Step 2: Prove injectivity via root counting.
- Kernel consists only of zero polynomial.

Step 3: Use equal finite dimensions to deduce bijectivity.
- Then show `interpolationPoly` realizes the inverse by evaluation correctness.

Why it is attractive:
- conceptually minimal;
- may avoid explicit sum-linearity proofs if surjectivity is deduced abstractly.

Risk:
- dimension computations for the bounded-degree submodule may take more Lean overhead than the direct explicit inverse route.

### Recommended plan

### Lean engineering guidance
- If a direct `Finset`-subtype formulation becomes cumbersome, switch early to an indexed family `v : Fin (n+1) → K` with injective nodes. This often simplifies:
  - cardinal bookkeeping,
  - extensionality,
  - finite sums,
  - matrix/Vandermonde alternatives.
- Use `Subtype.ext` and polynomial extensionality carefully.
- If `natDegree` causes friction at zero, consider `degree ≤` formulations with `WithBot ℕ`, then derive `natDegree` corollaries.
- Package bounded-degree polynomials as a subtype first; only later upgrade to a `Submodule` if closure proofs are manageable.
- Prefer proving `LeftInverse` and `RightInverse` separately before building `LinearEquiv.ofBijective` or a direct `LinearEquiv`.

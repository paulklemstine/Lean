### 1. Parallel β-reduction
A relation reducing all redexes in one synchronized step. This is the most promising route to confluence.

Suggested Lean shape:

## Precise Theorem Targets

You must prove at least 3 substantial theorems. The following are the priority targets.

### Theorem 1: Diamond property for parallel β-reduction
This is the engine behind Church–Rosser.

Mathematical statement:
For all lambda terms `t u v`, if `t ⇛ u` and `t ⇛ v`, then there exists `w` such that `u ⇛ w` and `v ⇛ w`.

Suggested Lean 4 type signature:
```lean
theorem parBeta_diamond :
  ∀ {t u v : Lam}, ParBeta t u → ParBeta t v →
    ∃ w, ParBeta u w ∧ ParBeta v w
```

Why this matters:
This is not just a local lemma. It is the structural synchronization principle showing that non-deterministic β-contraction admits a canonical common expansion. It is the gateway to confluence without quotienting syntax or relying on an evaluator.

Proof strategy options:
1. **Induction on the first derivation, dependent inversion on the second**  
   - Induct on `ParBeta t u`.  
   - `rcases` the second derivation by matching source term shape.  
   - The β/β case is the heart: prove compatibility of substitution with parallel reduction.
2. **Complete developments strategy**  
   - Define the complete development `dev : Lam → Lam`.  
   - Show every parallel reduct reduces again to `dev t`.  
   - Conclude diamond with witness `dev t`.  
   This is conceptually cleaner and may scale better.
3. **Residual-theoretic route**  
   - Formalize redex residuals under contraction.  
   - More difficult, but would be a breakthrough-grade reusable rewriting framework.

Most promising: **Strategy 2**, if you can define complete developments cleanly in de Bruijn syntax. It compresses the diamond proof into a canonical target argument and makes later normalization proofs conceptually cleaner.

### Theorem 2: Church–Rosser / confluence of β-reduction
Mathematical statement:
If `t →* u` and `t →* v`, then there exists `w` such that `u →* w` and `v →* w`.

Suggested Lean 4 type signature:
```lean
theorem beta_confluent :
  ∀ {t u v : Lam}, ReflTransGen Beta t u → ReflTransGen Beta t v →
    ∃ w, ReflTransGen Beta u w ∧ ReflTransGen Beta v w
```

or, if you define reflexive-transitive closure notation:
```lean
theorem church_rosser :
  ∀ {t u v : Lam}, t -β→* u → t -β→* v →
    ∃ w, u -β→* w ∧ v -β→* w
```

Why this is a breakthrough:
Church–Rosser is the theorem that makes computation in lambda calculus mathematically coherent. In formal verification terms, it certifies consistency of normalization outcomes whenever normal forms exist. In rewriting theory, it turns β-reduction from a syntactic relation into a deterministic semantic process modulo reduction history.

Proof strategy options:
1. **Parallel reduction sandwich**
   - Show `Beta ⊆ ParBeta`.
   - Show `ParBeta ⊆ Beta*`.
   - Lift `parBeta_diamond` to confluence of `ParBeta*`.
   - Transfer confluence to `Beta*`.
2. **Newman-style route**
   - Impossible globally because β is not strongly normalizing on untyped terms.
   - But useful locally on typed fragments; do not use this for the main theorem.
3. **Tait–Martin-Löf strip lemma**
   - Define a strip/commutation lemma between one-step β and parallel β.
   - Derive confluence by commuting reductions.
   Elegant, but more technically delicate.

Most promising: **Strategy 1**. It is canonical, robust, and aligns with the best reusable formalization architecture.

### Theorem 3: Strong normalization for simply-typed lambda calculus
Mathematical statement:
Every well-typed simply-typed lambda term admits no infinite β-reduction sequence.

Suggested Lean 4 type signature:
```lean
theorem stlc_strong_normalization :
  ∀ {Γ A} (t : STm Γ A), SN Beta t
```

If you define typed reduction:
```lean
theorem stlc_strong_normalization :
  ∀ {Γ A} (t : STm Γ A), Acc TypedBeta t
```

Why this matters:
This theorem is the formal hinge between syntax and logic. It yields consistency, canonicity, decidable normalization procedures, and a basis for certified compilation. In proof theory, it is the finite-energy law of computation: types prohibit infinite computational descent.

Proof strategy options:
1. **Tait reducibility / logical relations**
   - Define reducibility by recursion on types.
   - Prove every reducible term is strongly normalizing.
   - Prove the fundamental theorem: every well-typed term under a reducible substitution is reducible.
   - Instantiate with identity substitution.
2. **Hereditary substitution**
   - Define normalization by type-directed substitution.
   - Extract strong normalization from totality.
   - Elegant computationally, but implementation-heavy.
3. **Semantic interpretation into well-founded measures**
   - Interpret terms into a well-founded semantic domain.
   - Likely too ambitious unless you already have a semantic library to reuse.

Most promising: **Strategy 1**. It is the classical theorem, scales to richer calculi, and will force a genuinely deep Lean development using induction over typing derivations and environments.

### Theorem 4: Uniqueness of normal forms as a corollary of confluence
Mathematical statement:
If `u` and `v` are β-normal forms reachable from `t`, then `u = v`.

Suggested Lean 4 type signature:
```lean
theorem normal_form_unique_of_reachable
  {t u v : Lam}
  (hu : ReflTransGen Beta t u)
  (hv : ReflTransGen Beta t v)
  (nfu : NormalForm Beta u)
  (nfv : NormalForm Beta v) :
  u = v
```

Why it matters:
This is the certified determinism theorem for symbolic evaluation. It converts Church–Rosser into a practical uniqueness guarantee for normalization algorithms and proof assistants.

Proof sketch:
- Use `beta_confluent` to obtain a common reduct `w`.
- Since `u` and `v` are normal, any reachable `w` must equal both.
- Conclude `u = v`.

This theorem is not the main breakthrough, but it is an essential high-value corollary that turns abstract confluence into algorithmic trust.

### Theorem 5: Finite Böhm approximants are invariant under β-conversion
Mathematical statement:
If `t` and `u` are β-convertible, then for every truncation depth `n`, their Böhm approximants at depth `n` coincide, under a suitable head-normalizing side condition or partiality convention.

Suggested Lean 4 type signature:
```lean
theorem bohmApprox_beta_invariant
  {t u : Lam} :
  BetaEq t u →
  ∀ n, bohmApprox n t = bohmApprox n u
```

If full invariance is too strong, use a head-normalizing fragment:
```lean
theorem bohmApprox_beta_invariant_hnf
  {t u : Lam} :
  BetaEq t u → HeadNormalizing t →
  ∀ n, bohmApprox n t = bohmApprox n u
```

Why this matters:
This moves the development beyond finite rewriting into infinitary semantics. Böhm trees are where lambda calculus stops being just a reduction system and becomes a semantic universe. Even finite approximants are enough to separate terms observationally and support computational experiments on inequivalence.

Proof strategy:
1. Prove invariance under one-step β using head reduction compatibility.
2. Lift to β-convertibility by closure.
3. For non-head-normalizing terms, map approximants to bottom and prove stability.

This theorem is highly nontrivial and field-opening because it turns semantic approximants into certified observational invariants.

### Strategy B: Semantic route for strong normalization

2. Prove β-invariance on the head-normalizing fragment or under β-equivalence with bottom for divergence.
3. Use the approximants to exhibit separations and formulate computational undecidability hypotheses.

Why this is best:
It creates a bridge from rewriting to semantic observables and gives you executable experiments in `demo.py`.

## Falsifiable Conjecture With Clear Computational Test

You must state at least one conjecture with a concrete disproof protocol.

### Conjecture 1: Böhm approximant separation depth predicts β-inequivalence
For closed terms of size at most `N`, if `t` and `u` are not βη-equivalent, then there exists `n ≤ c*N` such that `bohmApprox n t ≠ bohmApprox n u`.

Suggested formal statement in prose:
There exists a linear bound on finite Böhm-tree depth needed to witness inequivalence among closed terms of bounded size.

Computational test:
- Enumerate closed terms up to size `N`.
- Compute approximants up to depth `c*N`.
- Search for a counterexample pair inequivalent but indistinguishable up to that depth.
- A single counterexample falsifies the conjecture.

### Conjecture 2: Reduction tree branching is subexponential on simply-typed terms
For closed STLC terms of size `n`, the number of distinct β-reducts reachable within depth `n` grows strictly slower than `2^n`.

Computational test:
- Enumerate simply-typed closed terms up to size `n`.
- Compute `reductsUpToDepth t n`.
- Compare against `2^n`.
- Any family meeting the exponential envelope falsifies the claim.

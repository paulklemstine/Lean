## Abstract

We give an unconditional, elementary treatment of the cyclotomic case of Artin reciprocity, and a structural analysis of the data required by the existence theorem for Hilbert class fields.

For the first part, let $A$ be a commutative ring and $\zeta \in A$. We prove that any ring endomorphism $\sigma$ of $A$ satisfying $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$, for a prime $p$, satisfies the *Frobenius congruence* $\sigma(x) \equiv x^{p} \pmod{pA}$ for all $x$ in the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ generated by $\zeta$. Conversely, if $S$ is a domain, $\zeta \in S$ satisfies $\zeta^{n} = 1$, $Q \subset S$ is a prime with residue cardinality $p$ over a base ring $R$, and $n \notin Q$, then any arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ sends $\zeta$ to $\zeta^{p}$ *on the nose*. Consequently, for $S = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ monogenic over $\mathbb{Z}$ with $p \in Q$ and $n \notin Q$, an $R$-algebra endomorphism is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ **if and only if** it is the power map $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{p}$; and the arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ is unique **with no unramifiedness hypothesis**. Specialising to the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})$, we show that the canonical isomorphism $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^{\times} \simeq \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})/\mathbb{Q})$ evaluated at the class of a prime $p \nmid n$ restricts on the order $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ to the arithmetic Frobenius at every prime of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ above $p$, and is the only such endomorphism.

For the second part, we isolate the existence assertion for Hilbert class fields as a bundled datum — a finite Galois extension $H/K$, unramified at all finite primes, with an isomorphism $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \simeq \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$ — and derive its unconditional consequences: the degree equals the class number; the Galois group is abelian; the datum is trivial exactly when $h_{K}=1$; any two data over the same base have equal degree; and the datum exists with $H = K$ whenever $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is a principal ideal ring. As a consistency test we prove, via the different ideal and the Minkowski discriminant bound, that a number field unramified at every finite prime over $\mathbb{Z}$ equals $\mathbb{Q}$, whence every Hilbert class field datum over $\mathbb{Q}$ is trivial.

The overall conclusion is a sharp separation: the cyclotomic entry of the reciprocity dictionary is a congruence-theoretic fact requiring no ramification theory, while the difficulty of class field theory concentrates entirely in the existence of extensions with prescribed ramification and Galois group.

**Keywords:** cyclotomic field, arithmetic Frobenius, Artin reciprocity, Hilbert class field, ideal class group, different ideal, Minkowski bound, monogenic ring.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The two halves of class field theory

Class field theory asserts a dictionary between the arithmetic of a number field $K$ and the abelian extensions of $K$. Its two halves are of very different characters:

1. **Reciprocity.** Given an abelian extension $H/K$ unramified at a prime $\mathfrak{p}$, there is a canonical element $\left(\frac{H/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right) \in \operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$ — the Artin symbol — characterised by a congruence, and the assignment $\mathfrak{p} \mapsto \left(\frac{H/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)$ extends to a surjection from a ray class group.
2. **Existence.** Every open subgroup of finite index in a suitable idèle class group is the norm group of an abelian extension. The distinguished case is the **Hilbert class field**: a finite extension $H/K$, unramified at every prime, with $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \simeq \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$.

The archetype for (1) is the cyclotomic case over $\mathbb{Q}$: the Galois group of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})/\mathbb{Q}$ is $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^{\times}$ via $m \mapsto (\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{m})$, and the Artin symbol at a prime $p \nmid n$ is the class of $p$. This paper asks what that archetype actually costs, and — separately — what one can say about a Hilbert class field before constructing it.

### 1.2 Results

Our first group of results (Section 3) shows that the cyclotomic archetype is a theorem about congruences in commutative rings. It requires neither Galois theory nor ramification theory, and it holds in a generality that includes non-maximal orders and ramified primes:

- **Theorem A (Frobenius congruence).** For a commutative ring $A$, a prime $p$, an element $\zeta \in A$, and a ring endomorphism $\sigma$ of $A$ with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$: for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq A$ one has $\sigma(x) - x^{p} \in pA$.
- **Theorem B (Frobenius determines the generator).** For a domain $S$, an element $\zeta$ with $\zeta^{n} = 1$, and a prime $Q \subseteq S$ with residue cardinality $p$ over the base and $n \notin Q$: every arithmetic Frobenius $\sigma$ at $Q$ satisfies $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$ exactly.
- **Theorem C (Characterisation and rigidity).** For $S$ a domain with $S = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$, $\zeta^{n} = 1$, $Q$ a prime with residue cardinality a prime $p$, $p \in Q$, $n \notin Q$: an $R$-algebra endomorphism is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ iff it is the power map $\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{p}$; in particular the arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ is unique, with no unramifiedness hypothesis.
- **Theorem D (Cyclotomic reciprocity over $\mathbb{Q}$).** Let $L = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ with $\zeta$ a primitive $n$-th root of unity, let $\mathrm{Art} : (\mathbb{Z}/n)^{\times} \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$ be the canonical isomorphism, and let $p \nmid n$ be prime. Then $\mathrm{Art}([p])$ preserves the order $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$, its restriction is the arithmetic Frobenius at every prime $Q$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ above $p$, and it is the *only* endomorphism of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ with that property.

Our second group (Section 4) analyses the existence assertion:

- **Definition (Hilbert class field datum).** A finite Galois extension $H/K$ of number fields, unramified at every finite prime of $H$ over $\mathcal{O}_{K}$, together with an isomorphism $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \simeq \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$.
- **Theorem E (Structural constraints).** Any such datum satisfies $[H:K] = h_{K}$; its Galois group is abelian; it is trivial ($H = K$) iff $h_{K} = 1$; any two data over the same $K$ have equal degree.
- **Theorem F (Realisation in the principal case).** If $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is a principal ideal ring, $H = K$ is a Hilbert class field datum for $K$.
- **Theorem G (Hermite–Minkowski collapse).** A number field $K$ with every prime of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies $[K : \mathbb{Q}] = 1$. Consequently every Hilbert class field datum over $\mathbb{Q}$ is trivial.

### 1.3 What is new in emphasis

Two points deserve underlining.

First, **uniqueness of the Frobenius without unramifiedness.** Standard formulations obtain uniqueness of the Frobenius element from the fact that the decomposition group maps isomorphically onto the Galois group of the residue extension, which requires unramifiedness. Theorem C bypasses this: if the ring is generated by a *single* element whose image under any Frobenius is forced, then the Frobenius is unique because a ring homomorphism is determined by the image of a generator. Ramification is irrelevant; monogenicity plus separability of the generator's minimal relation modulo $Q$ does all the work.

Second, **falsifiability of the existence programme.** Theorem E converts the existence question into a search with a known target: degree $h_{K}$, abelian group, trivial when $h_{K} = 1$. Theorem G is the first nontrivial consistency test, over $\mathbb{Q}$, and it passes — not by definitional bookkeeping but through Minkowski's discriminant bound.

## 2. Notation and definitions

Throughout, all rings are commutative with $1$ and all homomorphisms are unital.

**Adjoined subrings.** For a commutative ring $A$ and $\zeta \in A$, write $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] := \mathbb{Z}\text{-}\mathrm{adjoin}(\zeta) \subseteq A$ for the smallest subring containing $\zeta$ (equivalently the image of the evaluation map $\mathbb{Z}[X] \to A$, $X \mapsto \zeta$). It is characterised by the induction principle: a subset closed under the images of $\mathbb{Z}$, containing $\zeta$, and closed under addition and multiplication, contains $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$. We say $A$ is *monogenic over $\mathbb{Z}$ with generator $\zeta$* if $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = A$.

**Lying over and residue cardinality.** Let $R \to S$ be a ring map and $Q \subseteq S$ an ideal. Write $Q \cap R$ for the contraction of $Q$ to $R$. We write
$$f(Q) := \# \left( R / (Q \cap R) \right)$$
for the cardinality of the residue ring of the base below $Q$. In the arithmetic applications $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $f(Q) = p$ is a rational prime.

**Arithmetic Frobenius.** Let $R \to S$ be a ring map and $Q \subseteq S$ an ideal. An $R$-algebra endomorphism $\sigma : S \to S$ is an **arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$** if
$$\sigma(x) - x^{\,f(Q)} \in Q \qquad \text{for all } x \in S.$$
This is the characteristic-zero shadow of the Frobenius endomorphism of the residue ring $S/Q$, which has characteristic dividing $f(Q)$.

**Number fields.** For a number field $K$ we write $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ for its ring of integers, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$ for the ideal class group, $h_{K} = \#\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$ for the class number, $d_{K}$ for the discriminant, and $\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{K}$ for the different ideal. A prime $P$ of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is *unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$* in the formal sense: the localisation $(\mathcal{O}_{K})_{P}$ is formally unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Roots of unity and the cyclotomic field.** $\zeta$ is a *primitive $n$-th root of unity* in a field $L$ if $\zeta^{n} = 1$ and no smaller positive power of $\zeta$ is $1$; equivalently, $\zeta$ has order exactly $n$ in $L^{\times}$. If $L/\mathbb{Q}$ is generated by such a $\zeta$ we call $L$ the *$n$-th cyclotomic field*, $L = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})$, of degree $\varphi(n)$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The *order* $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq L$ is the subring generated by $\zeta$; for $L$ cyclotomic it happens to be the full ring of integers, but none of our arguments uses this.

## 3. Frobenius in monogenic rings

### 3.1 The congruence

**Theorem A (Frobenius congruence).**
*Let $A$ be a commutative ring, $p$ a prime number, $\zeta \in A$, and $\sigma : A \to A$ a ring homomorphism with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$. Then for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq A$,*
$$\sigma(x) - x^{p} \in pA .$$

*Proof.* Let $J := pA$ and let
$$T := \{\, x \in A : \sigma(x) - x^{p} \in J \,\}.$$
We show $T$ contains the image of $\mathbb{Z}$, contains $\zeta$, and is closed under sums and products; the induction principle for $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ then gives $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq T$.

*(i) Integers.* Let $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ and let $\bar m$ denote its image in $A$. Fermat's little theorem gives $p \mid m^{p} - m$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, say $m^{p} - m = pk$. Applying the (unique) ring map $\mathbb{Z} \to A$ yields $\bar m^{p} - \bar m = p \bar k \in J$. Since $\sigma(\bar m) = \bar m$, we get $\sigma(\bar m) - \bar m^{p} = -(\bar m^{p} - \bar m) \in J$.

*(ii) The generator.* $\sigma(\zeta) - \zeta^{p} = 0 \in J$ by hypothesis.

*(iii) Products.* If $x, y \in T$, write $\sigma(x) = x^{p} + p u$ and $\sigma(y) = y^{p} + p v$. Then
$$\sigma(xy) - (xy)^{p} = \sigma(x)\sigma(y) - x^{p}y^{p} = (\sigma(x) - x^{p})\sigma(y) + x^{p}\left(\sigma(y) - y^{p}\right) = p\left(u\,\sigma(y) + x^{p} v\right) \in J .$$

*(iv) Sums.* Here we use the divisibility $p \mid \binom{p}{k}$ for $0 < k < p$, valid because $p$ is prime. Expanding,
$$(x+y)^{p} = x^{p} + y^{p} + \sum_{k=1}^{p-1} \binom{p}{k} x^{p-k} y^{k} = x^{p} + y^{p} + p\, w, \qquad w := \sum_{k=1}^{p-1} \frac{1}{p}\binom{p}{k}\, x^{p-k} y^{k} \in A,$$
where each $\frac{1}{p}\binom{p}{k}$ is an integer. Hence, with $\sigma(x) = x^{p} + pu$, $\sigma(y) = y^{p} + pv$ as above,
$$\sigma(x+y) - (x+y)^{p} = \sigma(x) + \sigma(y) - x^{p} - y^{p} - pw = p(u + v - w) \in J .$$

This completes the induction. $\square$

**Remark 3.1.** The statement is unconditional in a striking way: $A$ need not be a domain, $\zeta$ need not be a root of unity, $p$ need not be invertible or non-invertible anywhere, and no prime ideal appears. Item (iv) is the only place where primality of $p$ is used, and it is the classical "freshman's dream" mechanism that makes $x \mapsto x^{p}$ additive in characteristic $p$.

**Corollary 3.2 (Monogenic form).** If moreover $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = A$, then $\sigma(x) \equiv x^{p} \pmod{pA}$ for *all* $x \in A$.

### 3.2 Rigidity of endomorphisms of monogenic rings

**Lemma 3.3 (Generator extensionality).**
*Let $A$ be a commutative ring with $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = A$. If $\sigma, \tau : A \to A$ are ring homomorphisms with $\sigma(\zeta) = \tau(\zeta)$, then $\sigma = \tau$.*

*Proof.* The equaliser $\{x \in A : \sigma(x) = \tau(x)\}$ contains the image of $\mathbb{Z}$ (both maps fix it), contains $\zeta$ by hypothesis, and is closed under sums and products since $\sigma$ and $\tau$ are ring maps. By the induction principle it contains $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = A$. $\square$

This trivial-looking lemma is the engine behind the unramified-hypothesis-free uniqueness in Theorem C.

### 3.3 Power maps are Frobenius maps

**Theorem 3.4 (The power map is an arithmetic Frobenius).**
*Let $R \to S$ be a ring map, $\zeta \in S$ with $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = S$, let $p$ be a prime, and let $Q \subseteq S$ be an ideal with residue cardinality $f(Q) = p$ and $p \in Q$. Then every $R$-algebra endomorphism $\sigma$ of $S$ with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$ is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$.*

*Proof.* Let $x \in S$. By Corollary 3.2, $\sigma(x) - x^{p} = p\,y$ for some $y \in S$. Since $p \in Q$ and $Q$ is an ideal, $py \in Q$. As $f(Q) = p$, the defining condition $\sigma(x) - x^{f(Q)} \in Q$ is exactly $\sigma(x) - x^{p} \in Q$, which holds. $\square$

### 3.4 Frobenius maps are power maps

This is the converse, and the one with real content.

**Theorem B (Frobenius determines the generator).**
*Let $R \to S$ be a ring map with $S$ an integral domain. Let $\zeta \in S$ satisfy $\zeta^{n} = 1$ for some $n \geq 1$, let $Q \subseteq S$ be an ideal with $n \cdot 1_{S} \notin Q$ and residue cardinality $f(Q) = p$. If $\sigma$ is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$, then*
$$\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}.$$

*Proof.* Set $a := \sigma(\zeta)$ and $b := \zeta^{p}$.

*Step 1: both are $n$-th roots of unity.* $a^{n} = \sigma(\zeta)^{n} = \sigma(\zeta^{n}) = \sigma(1) = 1$, and $b^{n} = (\zeta^{p})^{n} = (\zeta^{n})^{p} = 1$.

*Step 2: they are congruent.* By the Frobenius property applied to $x = \zeta$, and $f(Q) = p$, we get $a - b = \sigma(\zeta) - \zeta^{p} \in Q$.

*Step 3: the geometric factorisation.* By Step 1,
$$0 = a^{n} - b^{n} = (a - b)\, \Sigma, \qquad \Sigma := \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} a^{i}\, b^{\,n-1-i}.$$
Suppose, for contradiction, that $a \neq b$. Then $a - b \neq 0$; since $S$ is a domain, $\Sigma = 0$.

*Step 4: reduce $\Sigma$ modulo $Q$.* By Step 2, $a \equiv b \pmod Q$, hence for each $i$,
$$a^{i} b^{\,n-1-i} \equiv b^{i} b^{\,n-1-i} = b^{\,n-1} \pmod Q .$$
Summing the $n$ congruences, $\Sigma \equiv n\, b^{\,n-1} \pmod{Q}$. Combined with $\Sigma = 0$ this gives $n\,b^{\,n-1} \in Q$.

*Step 5: $b$ is a unit.* Since $b^{n} = 1$, we have $b \cdot b^{\,n-1} = 1$, so $b^{\,n-1}$ is a unit with inverse $b$. Multiplying $n\,b^{\,n-1} \in Q$ by $(b^{\,n-1})^{-1}$ and using that $Q$ is an ideal gives $n \cdot 1_{S} \in Q$, contradicting the hypothesis.

Therefore $a = b$, i.e. $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$. $\square$

**Remark 3.5 (Why this is the right hypothesis).** The condition $n \notin Q$ says precisely that the polynomial $X^{n} - 1$ remains separable modulo $Q$ (its derivative $nX^{n-1}$ is a unit times $X^{n-1}$, coprime to $X^n-1$ modulo $Q$). Separability is what prevents two distinct roots of unity from becoming congruent, and Steps 3–5 are a hands-on proof of exactly that: the *congruence* $a \equiv b$ has been upgraded to the *equality* $a = b$.

**Remark 3.6 (Sharpness).** The hypothesis $n \notin Q$ cannot be dropped. In $S = \mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $n = 4$ and $Q = (1+i)$, one has $4 \in Q$; complex conjugation and the identity are both congruent to the squaring map modulo $Q$ (the residue field is $\mathbb{F}_{2}$), and they differ. This is the ramified prime, and it is precisely where rigidity can fail. Domain-ness in Step 3 is likewise essential.

### 3.5 The characterisation and rigidity theorems

**Theorem C (Characterisation).**
*Let $R \to S$ be a ring map with $S$ a domain, $\zeta \in S$ with $\zeta^{n} = 1$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = S$. Let $Q \subseteq S$ be an ideal with $n \notin Q$, residue cardinality $f(Q) = p$ a prime, and $p \in Q$. Then for an $R$-algebra endomorphism $\sigma$ of $S$:*
$$\sigma \text{ is an arithmetic Frobenius at } Q \iff \sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}.$$

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) is Theorem B; ($\Leftarrow$) is Theorem 3.4. $\square$

**Theorem C$'$ (Rigidity, no unramifiedness).**
*Under the hypotheses of Theorem C (except that $p \in Q$ is not needed), if $\sigma$ and $\tau$ are both arithmetic Frobenius maps at $Q$, then $\sigma = \tau$.*

*Proof.* By Theorem B, $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p} = \tau(\zeta)$. By Lemma 3.3 with $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = S$, $\sigma = \tau$. $\square$

**Discussion.** The classical uniqueness statement for the Frobenius element assumes $Q$ unramified over the base; the decomposition group then maps isomorphically to the cyclic Galois group of the residue extension, whose canonical generator pulls back to a unique element. Theorem C$'$ replaces that hypothesis with monogenicity, obtaining uniqueness even at ramified primes, provided the order of the generating root of unity is invertible mod $Q$. Two structural remarks:

1. Rigidity is about *determination*, not existence. Theorem C$'$ says at most one Frobenius exists; Theorem 3.4 provides existence when the power map is an endomorphism of $S$ over $R$.
2. The result cannot be extended verbatim to arbitrary monogenic $S = \mathbb{Z}[\theta]$ by the same proof, because Steps 3–5 used $\theta^{n} = 1$. Whether it nevertheless holds is a natural falsifiable question; see Section 6.

## 4. Cyclotomic reciprocity over $\mathbb{Q}$

We now specialise to number fields and identify the abstract Frobenius with the classical Artin symbol.

### 4.1 The cyclotomic Artin map

Fix $n \geq 1$ and let $L$ be an $n$-th cyclotomic extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, i.e. $L = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$ for a primitive $n$-th root of unity $\zeta$. Irreducibility of the $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ gives $[L:\mathbb{Q}] = \varphi(n)$ and a canonical isomorphism.

**Definition 4.1 (Artin map).** The **cyclotomic Artin map** is the isomorphism
$$\mathrm{Art} : (\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times} \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$$
inverse to the injection $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}) \hookrightarrow (\mathbb{Z}/n)^{\times}$ sending an automorphism $\sigma$ to the unique class $u$ with $\sigma(\eta) = \eta^{u}$ for every primitive $n$-th root $\eta$.

Two facts underlie Definition 4.1 and are worth stating separately, because the exponent attached to an automorphism must be shown independent of the chosen root:

**Lemma 4.2 (Independence of the chosen primitive root).** *Let $\zeta, \zeta'$ be primitive $n$-th roots of unity in $L$ and $\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$. The class $u \in (\mathbb{Z}/n)^{\times}$ determined by $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{u}$ equals the class determined by $\sigma(\zeta') = (\zeta')^{u'}$.*

*Proof sketch.* Write $\zeta' = \zeta^{k}$ with $\gcd(k,n) = 1$ (possible since $\zeta$ generates the group of $n$-th roots of unity and $\zeta'$ has exact order $n$). Then
$$\zeta^{\,u k} = (\zeta^{u})^{k} = \sigma(\zeta)^{k} = \sigma(\zeta^{k}) = \sigma(\zeta') = (\zeta')^{u'} = \zeta^{\,k u'} .$$
Since $\zeta$ has exact order $n$, exponents may be compared modulo $n$: $uk \equiv k u' \pmod n$. As $k$ is invertible mod $n$, $u \equiv u' \pmod n$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.3 (Catalog description).** *For $m$ coprime to $n$ and $\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$: the exponent class of $\sigma$ equals $[m]$ if and only if $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{m}$. Consequently $\mathrm{Art}([m])$ is the **power map** $\sigma_{m} : \zeta \mapsto \zeta^{m}$.*

*Proof sketch.* If the exponent class of $\sigma$ is $[m]$ then $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{\,m \bmod n} = \zeta^{m}$, using $\zeta^{n} = 1$ to replace $m \bmod n$ by $m$. Conversely if $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{m}$, comparing with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{u}$ and using that $\zeta$ has exact order $n$ gives $u \equiv m \pmod{n}$. $\square$

Note that $\mathrm{Art}$ is a group isomorphism, so in particular $\mathrm{Art}([m][m']) = \mathrm{Art}([m]) \circ \mathrm{Art}([m'])$, i.e. $\sigma_{mm'} = \sigma_{m}\sigma_{m'}$ — the multiplicativity that makes the Artin symbol a homomorphism on ideals.

### 4.2 The order $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$ and restriction

Let $\mathcal{O} := \mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq L$ denote the order generated by $\zeta$. Two elementary but necessary observations:

**Lemma 4.4 (Self-generation).** *Viewed as a ring in its own right, $\mathcal{O}$ is generated over $\mathbb{Z}$ by the element $\zeta$ (regarded as an element of $\mathcal{O}$): $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]_{\mathcal{O}} = \mathcal{O}$.*

*Proof sketch.* The inclusion $\iota : \mathcal{O} \hookrightarrow L$ is an injective ring map carrying $\zeta \in \mathcal{O}$ to $\zeta \in L$; hence it carries the subring generated by $\zeta$ inside $\mathcal{O}$ onto the subring generated by $\zeta$ inside $L$, which is $\mathcal{O}$ itself. Injectivity then forces the subring to be all of $\mathcal{O}$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.5 (Stability under power maps).** *If $\sigma : L \to L$ is a ring homomorphism with $\sigma(\zeta) \in \mathcal{O}$, then $\sigma(\mathcal{O}) \subseteq \mathcal{O}$, and $\sigma$ restricts to a $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra endomorphism $\sigma|_{\mathcal{O}}$ of $\mathcal{O}$. In particular, for $m$ coprime to $n$, $\mathrm{Art}([m])(\zeta) = \zeta^{m} \in \mathcal{O}$, so $\sigma_{m}$ restricts to $\mathcal{O}$.*

*Proof sketch.* The preimage $\sigma^{-1}(\mathcal{O}) \cap \mathcal{O}$ is a subring of $L$ containing $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\zeta$, hence contains $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] = \mathcal{O}$. $\square$

### 4.3 The reciprocity theorem

**Theorem D (Cyclotomic reciprocity over $\mathbb{Q}$).**
*Let $\zeta$ be a primitive $n$-th root of unity in $L$, $\mathcal{O} = \mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$, and $p$ a prime with $\gcd(p,n) = 1$. Let $Q \subseteq \mathcal{O}$ be an ideal with $\#(\mathbb{Z}/(Q \cap \mathbb{Z})) = p$ and $p \in Q$. Then:*

1. *(Existence)* $\sigma_{p}|_{\mathcal{O}} = \mathrm{Art}([p])|_{\mathcal{O}}$ *is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$.*
2. *(Uniqueness)* *if additionally $n \notin Q$, then every $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra endomorphism $\varphi$ of $\mathcal{O}$ that is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ equals $\sigma_{p}|_{\mathcal{O}}$.*

*Proof.* (1) By Lemma 4.3, $\mathrm{Art}([p])(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$, which lies in $\mathcal{O}$, so by Lemma 4.5 it restricts to a $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra endomorphism $\sigma$ of $\mathcal{O}$ with $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$. By Lemma 4.4, $\mathcal{O}$ is monogenic with generator $\zeta$, so Theorem 3.4 applies with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $S = \mathcal{O}$, giving that $\sigma$ is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$.

(2) $\mathcal{O}$ is a domain (a subring of a field) and $\zeta^{n} = 1$. By Theorem B applied to $\varphi$, $\varphi(\zeta) = \zeta^{p}$; the same holds for $\sigma_{p}|_{\mathcal{O}}$. By Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 4.4, $\varphi = \sigma_{p}|_{\mathcal{O}}$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.6.** *The map $p \mapsto (\text{Frobenius at any } Q \mid p)$, for primes $p \nmid n$, is well defined on $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$, independent of the choice of $Q$ above $p$, and equals $p \mapsto \mathrm{Art}([p])$.*

*Proof.* Both parts of Theorem D produce the same endomorphism $\sigma_{p}|_{\mathcal{O}}$ regardless of $Q$. $\square$

Corollary 4.6 is the first line of the reciprocity dictionary, proved here without invoking class field theory, decomposition groups, or ramification indices. The only arithmetic inputs are Fermat's little theorem, divisibility of interior binomial coefficients by $p$, and separability of $X^{n}-1$ modulo $Q$ when $n \notin Q$.

## 5. Hilbert class field data

### 5.1 The datum

**Definition 5.1 (Hilbert class field datum).** Let $K$ be a number field and $H/K$ a field extension with $H$ a number field. A **Hilbert class field datum** for $(K,H)$ consists of:

- (finiteness) $H$ is finite-dimensional as a $K$-vector space;
- (normality) $H/K$ is Galois;
- (unramifiedness) every prime $Q$ of $\mathcal{O}_{H}$ is unramified over $\mathcal{O}_{K}$;
- (reciprocity) an isomorphism of groups $\mathrm{Art} : \operatorname{Gal}(H/K) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$.

Existence of such a datum for every $K$ is the content of the existence theorem for Hilbert class fields. Bundling the requirements makes it possible to reason about consequences before settling existence.

### 5.2 Structural consequences

**Theorem E.** *Let $(H/K, \mathrm{Art})$ be a Hilbert class field datum. Then:*

1. $[H:K] = h_{K}$;
2. $\operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$ *is abelian, so $H/K$ is an abelian extension;*
3. *the structure map $K \to H$ is bijective (i.e. $H = K$) if and only if $h_{K} = 1$;*
4. *if $(H'/K, \mathrm{Art}')$ is another Hilbert class field datum for the same $K$, then $[H:K] = [H':K]$.*

*Proof.*

(1) Since $H/K$ is finite Galois, the order of the automorphism group equals the degree: $\#\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) = [H:K]$. The isomorphism $\mathrm{Art}$ gives $\#\operatorname{Gal}(H/K) = \#\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K}) = h_{K}$. Combining yields $[H:K] = h_{K}$.

(2) For $\sigma, \tau \in \operatorname{Gal}(H/K)$, $\mathrm{Art}(\sigma\tau) = \mathrm{Art}(\sigma)\mathrm{Art}(\tau) = \mathrm{Art}(\tau)\mathrm{Art}(\sigma) = \mathrm{Art}(\tau\sigma)$ because the class group is abelian; injectivity of $\mathrm{Art}$ gives $\sigma\tau = \tau\sigma$.

(3) The structure map $K \to H$ is always injective (a nonzero ring map out of a field). It is surjective iff $H$ is one-dimensional over $K$, i.e. $[H:K] = 1$; more precisely, surjectivity gives a $K$-linear isomorphism $K \simeq H$, hence $[H:K] = 1$; conversely if $[H:K] = 1$ then the image of the (injective) structure map is a $K$-subspace of dimension $1$ inside a $1$-dimensional space, hence everything. By (1), $[H:K] = 1 \iff h_{K} = 1$.

(4) Both degrees equal $h_{K}$ by (1). $\square$

**Theorem F (Realisation in the principal case).** *If $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is a principal ideal ring, then $H := K$ carries a Hilbert class field datum for $K$.*

*Proof.* $K/K$ is finite (degree $1$) and Galois. Unramifiedness holds because localisations of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ at its primes are formally unramified over $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ itself (localisation is formally étale). Finally, $\operatorname{Gal}(K/K)$ is trivial and, since $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is a principal ideal domain, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$ is trivial; a unique isomorphism between two trivial groups exists. $\square$

Since $\mathbb{Z} = \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is a principal ideal ring, $\mathbb{Q}$ has the trivial Hilbert class field datum. The interesting question is whether it has any *other*.

### 5.3 The Hermite–Minkowski collapse over $\mathbb{Q}$

**Theorem 5.2 (Trivial different).** *Let $K$ be a number field in which every prime $P$ of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$. Then $\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}} = (1)$.*

*Proof.* Suppose $\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}} \neq (1)$. Then it is contained in some maximal ideal $P$ of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$, so $P \mid \mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}$. But a prime divides the different exactly when it is ramified; since $P$ is unramified by hypothesis, contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Unit discriminant).** *Under the hypotheses of Theorem 5.2, $|d_{K}| = 1$.*

*Proof.* The absolute norm of the different is the absolute value of the discriminant, $|d_{K}| = \mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{d}_{K/\mathbb{Q}})$. By Theorem 5.2 the different is the unit ideal, whose norm is $1$. $\square$

**Theorem G (Hermite–Minkowski).** *Let $K$ be a number field in which every prime of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is unramified over $\mathbb{Z}$. Then $[K:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$.*

*Proof.* Suppose $[K:\mathbb{Q}] > 1$. Minkowski's discriminant bound gives $|d_{K}| > 2$ for every number field of degree $> 1$: embedding $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ as a full lattice in $\mathbb{R}^{r_{1}} \times \mathbb{C}^{r_{2}}$ of covolume proportional to $\sqrt{|d_{K}|}$, the convex body theorem produces a nonzero algebraic integer of small norm unless the covolume is large, and quantifying this yields $|d_{K}| \geq (\pi/4)^{2r_{2}} \frac{n^{2n}}{(n!)^{2}} > 2$ for $n = [K:\mathbb{Q}] \geq 2$. This contradicts Theorem 5.3, which gives $|d_{K}| = 1$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Falsifiability test over $\mathbb{Q}$, passed).** *Every Hilbert class field datum over $\mathbb{Q}$ is trivial: if $(H/\mathbb{Q}, \mathrm{Art})$ is a Hilbert class field datum then $[H:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$.*

*Proof.* The datum asserts that every prime of $\mathcal{O}_{H}$ is unramified over $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{Z}$ — formally, unramifiedness over $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}}$ implies unramifiedness over $\mathbb{Z}$, because $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is surjective, hence formally unramified, and formal unramifiedness composes. Theorem G then gives $[H:\mathbb{Q}] = 1$. $\square$

This is exactly the prediction of Theorem E(3) together with $h_{\mathbb{Q}} = 1$. The two derivations are independent — one from the reciprocity isomorphism, one from geometry of numbers — so their agreement is a genuine consistency check on Definition 5.1 rather than a tautology.

## 6. Algorithms and computational content

The results of Sections 3 and 4 are effective, and each admits a direct algorithmic test. We describe three.

### 6.1 Frobenius congruence verification in $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]$

Represent elements of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]$ as integer polynomials modulo the $n$-th cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_{n}(X)$; $\zeta$ is the class of $X$. The power map $\sigma_{m}$ acts by $f(X) \mapsto f(X^{m}) \bmod \Phi_{n}$. To test Theorem A for a prime $p$ and a random $x$: compute $\sigma_{p}(x) - x^{p}$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]/\Phi_{n}$ and check that all coefficients are divisible by $p$. Complexity: $O(\log p)$ polynomial multiplications of degree $< \varphi(n)$ with coefficients of bounded size, i.e. $\widetilde{O}(\varphi(n)^{2}\log p)$ bit operations by schoolbook arithmetic.

### 6.2 Frobenius search and rigidity check at a prime

Given $n$, a prime $p \nmid n$, and a prime $Q$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]$ above $p$ (concretely: reduce mod $p$ and pick an irreducible factor $g$ of $\Phi_{n}$ over $\mathbb{F}_{p}$, so $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]/Q \simeq \mathbb{F}_{p}[X]/g$), enumerate all $\varphi(n)$ candidate endomorphisms $\sigma_{m}$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$, and test the Frobenius condition on a generating set. Theorem C predicts exactly one survivor, namely $m \equiv p \pmod n$. This is a direct empirical test of both existence and rigidity, and is the cleanest computational signature of the theory: the search space has size $\varphi(n)$ and the answer is always a single point.

### 6.3 Class-number-driven Hilbert class field degree prediction

For an imaginary quadratic field $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})$, the class number $h_{K}$ can be computed by counting reduced binary quadratic forms of discriminant $D$: triples $(a,b,c)$ with $b^{2}-4ac = D$, $|b| \le a \le c$, and $b \ge 0$ when $|b| = a$ or $a = c$. Theorem E(1) then predicts the degree of any Hilbert class field of $K$; Theorem E(3) predicts triviality exactly for the nine Heegner discriminants with $h = 1$. Complexity: $O(|D|)$ form enumeration with the bound $a \le \sqrt{|D|/3}$.

## 7. Applications and interpretation

**7.1 Reciprocity as congruence.** Theorem A localises the content of cyclotomic reciprocity in two classical congruences (Fermat, and $p \mid \binom{p}{k}$). This has practical value: verifying that a proposed automorphism is a Frobenius requires no computation of ramification indices, decomposition groups, or inertia, only a divisibility check on the generator.

**7.2 Non-maximal orders and ramified primes.** Because Theorems A–C never require $S$ to be integrally closed, they apply verbatim to non-maximal orders $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \subseteq \mathcal{O}_{L}$ (and, over other bases, to orders where $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta] \neq \mathcal{O}_{L}$). Because they never require $Q$ to be unramified, they also apply at primes dividing the conductor of the order, provided only $n \notin Q$. This is genuinely wider than the classical statement.

**7.3 A design principle for the existence problem.** Theorem E says the *shape* of a Hilbert class field is determined before construction: degree $h_{K}$, abelian, trivial iff $h_{K} = 1$. Any constructive attempt can therefore be checked cheaply against three invariants. In particular constructions producing a nonabelian group, or a degree different from $h_{K}$, are wrong independently of any subtlety in the ramification analysis.

**7.4 Explicit generation.** For imaginary quadratic $K$ with small class number, the theory of complex multiplication produces explicit generators of the Hilbert class field as singular moduli $j(\tau)$; Theorem E(1) predicts the degree of the minimal polynomial of such a generator to be exactly $h_{K}$, which is the standard consistency check applied to class polynomials in computational number theory (and the basis of CM-method elliptic curve constructions in cryptography).

## 8. Discussion

The picture that emerges is one of pronounced asymmetry.

On the reciprocity side, the cyclotomic case — the case from which the general theory was historically abstracted — is elementary once formulated correctly. The formulation matters: by defining "arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$" as a congruence condition on *all* elements, rather than as a distinguished element of a decomposition group, one obtains a statement provable by induction on the construction of the ring. In this formulation, ramification never enters, and the uniqueness statement becomes *stronger* than the classical one.

On the existence side, everything one can extract from the definition alone is soft. Degree, commutativity, and the triviality criterion are one-line consequences; the substance lies entirely in constructing $H$. The only nontrivial theorem we can prove unconditionally about existence is a nonexistence theorem — that $\mathbb{Q}$ has no everywhere-unramified extension — and it consumes the full strength of geometry of numbers.

This suggests a diagnosis of why the existence theorem is hard: it is not a statement about congruences at all. Congruence conditions cut out *at most one* candidate Frobenius (Theorem C$'$), but they never produce the field in which the candidate lives. Existence requires a genuinely global input — analytic (L-functions), cohomological (Brauer groups), or transcendental (complex multiplication) — that no amount of local congruence analysis will supply.

A secondary observation concerns relativisation. The Hermite–Minkowski route to Theorem G uses the *absolute* discriminant bound $|d_{K}| > 2$, which has no relative analogue: there is no numerical obstruction to a relative extension being everywhere unramified — indeed, for $h_{K} > 1$ such extensions exist and are the point of the whole theory. The relative statement "a base of class number one admits no everywhere-unramified extension" must therefore proceed differently, via triviality of the relative different plus a counting argument, and is a genuine theorem rather than a bound.

## 9. Future directions

**Frobenius rigidity beyond cyclotomic rings.** Let $S$ be a domain generated over $\mathbb{Z}$ by a single element $\theta$, and let $Q$ be a prime of $S$ with residue field of prime cardinality $p$. Is an arithmetic Frobenius at $Q$ unique whenever it exists, without any unramifiedness hypothesis? We prove this for $\theta$ a root of unity of order invertible modulo $Q$. It is falsifiable by exhibiting a monogenic $S = \mathbb{Z}[\theta]$, a prime $Q$, and two distinct endomorphisms both congruent to $x \mapsto x^{p}$ modulo $Q$. Since uniqueness is classical under unramifiedness, any counterexample must be ramified at $Q$; the natural candidate is $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ at $Q = (2, 1+\sqrt{-3})$.

**Everywhere-unramified rigidity over a base of class number one.** If $K$ is a number field with $h_{K} = 1$, is every finite extension $H/K$ unramified at every finite prime *and* every infinite place necessarily equal to $K$? We prove the case $K = \mathbb{Q}$ via the different and Hermite–Minkowski. Falsifying it would require a field of trivial class group carrying a nontrivial everywhere-unramified extension. The step that does not generalise is the discriminant bound; the relative proof must go through triviality of the relative different $\mathfrak{d}_{H/K} = (1)$.

**Degree obstruction as a falsifiability criterion.** Every Hilbert class field datum over $K$ has degree exactly $h_{K}$, so a candidate extension of the wrong degree is immediately excluded. Systematising this as a search procedure over candidate extensions of bounded discriminant would make the existence problem for fixed small $K$ genuinely computational.

**Idèlic realisation of the unramified correspondence.** Construct the canonical surjection from the idèle class group of $K$ onto $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K})$, identify its kernel with the subgroup generated by archimedean components and local unit groups, and prove that pullback identifies ideal-class characters exactly with idèle-class characters trivial on that kernel. This would place Theorem D inside its natural framework and give an intrinsic definition of the Artin map, independent of a chosen generator.

**Principal ideal theorem.** For a Hilbert class field $H/K$, prove that extension of ideals sends every nonzero fractional ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ to a principal fractional ideal of $\mathcal{O}_{H}$; equivalently, that the induced homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{K}) \to \mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{H})$ is zero. This is a genuinely group-theoretic statement (Artin's reduction to the transfer map), and is the natural next milestone once existence is available.

**Explicit imaginary-quadratic generators.** For every imaginary quadratic field of class number at most $4$, construct a singular modulus generating its Hilbert class field, and prove that its minimal polynomial has degree equal to the class number and splitting field unramified over the quadratic base.

## 10. Conclusion

We have proved that the cyclotomic case of Artin reciprocity is a theorem about congruences in monogenic rings: a ring endomorphism sending a generator $\zeta$ to $\zeta^{p}$ is congruent to the $p$-th power map modulo $p$ on the entire ring, and conversely an arithmetic Frobenius at a prime $Q$ with residue cardinality $p$ sends a root of unity of order invertible modulo $Q$ exactly to its $p$-th power. Together these give an if-and-only-if characterisation and an unconditional uniqueness statement that dispenses with the classical unramifiedness hypothesis, and they identify the classical Artin map $[p] \mapsto (\zeta \mapsto \zeta^{p})$ on cyclotomic fields with the arithmetic Frobenius on the order $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta]$.

Separately, we have shown that a Hilbert class field datum for a number field $K$ — a finite Galois extension unramified at every finite prime together with an isomorphism of its Galois group onto the ideal class group — is forced to have degree $h_{K}$ and abelian Galois group, is trivial exactly when $h_{K} = 1$, is realised trivially when $\mathcal{O}_{K}$ is principal, and over $\mathbb{Q}$ is necessarily trivial by the different-ideal and Minkowski discriminant argument. The existence problem thus stands isolated: all its soft consequences are settled, and what remains is exactly the hard, global construction.

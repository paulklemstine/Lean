# The Exclusive-Dimension Reliability Curve is a Monotone Ramp

### A finite-field obstruction model of boundary-token reliability, with a $q$-Pochhammer genericity bound and an Euler-product identity

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-08-15

## Abstract

A sequence model that must emit a boundary ("end-of-sequence") symbol is empirically fragile: whether
a trained copy halts correctly on inputs longer than those seen in training depends sharply on the
random seed, and — holding every other detail of the architecture fixed — on the number $k$ of
*exclusive* representational dimensions reserved for the boundary symbol. A controlled sweep over
$k \in \{0,1,2,4,8\}$ with six independent replicates per width produced the reliability curve
$0.25,\,0.33,\,0.83,\,1.00,\,1.00$: monotone, with sublinear returns, with the first exclusive
dimension demonstrably *insufficient*, and with no evidence of a sharp critical width.

We give an exact finite-field model in which each of those qualitative statements is a theorem. Let
$V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}_p$, let the boundary token's exclusive
subspace be generated by $k$ independent uniform draws from $V$, and let the trained dynamics'
degeneracies be a finite family $W_1,\dots,W_m$ of proper subspaces; a run *fails* when some $W_j$
contains all $k$ draws. We prove: (i) the reliability curve $k \mapsto P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k)$ is
monotone nondecreasing; (ii) $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) < 1$ for every finite $k$, so there is no capacity
cliff and no width certifies success; (iii) $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \to 1$, with the two-sided envelope
$p^{-k} \le 1 - P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \le m p^{-k}$; (iv) in the single-hyperplane case the curve is
exactly $1 - p^{-k}$, so the first exclusive dimension buys only $1 - 1/p$ and the marginal gain
$(p-1)p^{-(k+1)}$ strictly decreases. We package (i)–(iii) as the assertion that the curve is a
*monotone ramp*, and derive a two-sided design rule: reliability $1-\epsilon$ requires width
$\log_p(1/\epsilon)$ and is guaranteed by width $\log_p(m/\epsilon)$, so the knee is *located* to
within an additive $\log_p m$ even though it is not *sharp*.

Two extensions complete the picture. First, a genericity analysis confirms the model is not vacuous:
the probability that the $k$ draws are genuinely linearly independent is exactly the $q$-Pochhammer
product $\prod_{i<k}(1 - p^{i-n})$, which we bound below by $1 - (p^k-1)/((p-1)p^n)$; combining with
the union bound yields a joint reliability-and-genericity lower bound
$1 - m p^{-k} - (p^k-1)/((p-1)p^n)$ whose two error terms move in *opposite* directions in $k$,
establishing an interior optimum. Second, the total failure mass of the ramp at characteristic $p$ is
$\sum_{k \ge 0} p^{-k} = (1-p^{-1})^{-1}$, the local Euler factor; damping widths by $s$ and taking
the product over all characteristics gives $\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$, and $\pi^2/6$ at
$s = 2$. The identity holds *because* the ramp never terminates: a cliff would truncate the series
and destroy the Euler factor.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The empirical phenomenon

Consider a recurrent sequence model trained to reproduce a structured string and then to halt by
emitting a boundary symbol. Evaluate it by *progressive unrolling*: run it on inputs of increasing
length, including lengths beyond the training distribution, and record the model's confidence in the
correct halting behaviour. A model that halts correctly, with confidence $\ge 0.99$, on all tested
lengths is called **cured**.

Cure is not a property of the architecture: it is a property of the *draw*. With the architecture
fixed byte-for-byte and only the random seed varying, some replicates are cured and some are not.
The relevant tunable turns out to be the boundary symbol's **exclusive width** $k$: the number of
representational coordinates that the boundary symbol's learned embedding may occupy and that no
content symbol occupies. Holding all else fixed and sweeping $k$ over $\{0,1,2,4,8\}$ with six fresh
replicates per width, the fraction cured was

| exclusive dimensions $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| fraction cured | $0.25$ | $0.33$ | $0.83$ | $1.00$ | $1.00$ |
| worst replicate score | $0.005$ | $0.157$ | $0.948$ | $1.000$ | $1.000$ |
| median replicate score | $0.044$ | $0.83$ | $1.00$ | $1.00$ | $1.00$ |

Three observations organise the rest of this paper.

* **Monotonicity.** Extra exclusive width never hurt.
* **Insufficiency of the first dimension.** At $k = 1$ the six replicates included both a perfect
  score ($1.000$) and a hard failure ($0.157$). The naive hypothesis "any exclusive dimension
  suffices" is refuted: exclusivity is not a Boolean capacity condition.
* **Absence of a sharp width.** The curve rises smoothly with diminishing returns. The widths
  reporting a perfect cure report it from six samples, which is consistent with a true reliability
  of $0.94$ or higher, not with certainty.

The failure mechanism was width-independent: the same progressive-unroll collapse and the same
internal-state drift signature appeared at every failing width. This suggests that width does not
change *what* goes wrong, only *how often* — precisely the signature of a reliability curve rather
than a capacity threshold.

### 1.2 Cliff or ramp?

The distinction is consequential. A **cliff** model says: below a critical width $k^*$ the mechanism
is impossible; at or above $k^*$ it is guaranteed. A **ramp** model says: each additional exclusive
dimension multiplies the failure rate by a constant factor $< 1$, and no finite width eliminates
failure. The two prescribe different engineering (a hard budget versus a risk-tolerance
calculation), predict different sampling behaviour (deterministic versus binomial), and license
different conclusions from single-seed experiments.

Six replicates per width cannot separate them: the $k=1 \to k=2$ jump alone is not statistically
significant (a Fisher exact test gives $\approx 0.24$). What *can* separate them is a model. This
paper supplies one in which the ramp alternative is not merely plausible but forced, and in which
every qualitative feature of the measured curve is a theorem.

### 1.3 Contributions

1. **A finite-field obstruction model** (Section 2) with exact counting semantics, in which
   "probability" is cardinality and every claim is a finite computation.
2. **The monotone-ramp theorem** (Section 3): monotonicity via a prefix fibration, strict positivity
   of failure via the zero tuple, and convergence via a union bound; packaged as an abstract notion
   of monotone ramp with the corollary that no critical width exists.
3. **A two-sided geometric envelope and design rule** (Section 4): $p^{-k} \le 1 - P_{\mathrm{cure}}
   \le m p^{-k}$, hence necessary width $\log_p(1/\epsilon)$ and sufficient width
   $\log_p(m/\epsilon)$.
4. **The exact hyperplane curve** (Section 5): $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = 1 - p^{-k}$, with the
   insufficiency of the first dimension and strictly decreasing marginal gains as corollaries, plus
   a concrete witness realising the predicted values $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, \tfrac{15}{16},
   \tfrac{255}{256}$ at $p = 2$.
5. **A $q$-Pochhammer genericity theorem and the reliability/genericity trade-off** (Section 6),
   showing the model is non-vacuous and that the joint optimum is interior.
6. **An Euler-product bridge** (Section 7): the ramp's damped failure mass is the local factor of
   $\zeta$, and the product over characteristics is $\zeta(s)$.
7. **Algorithms and numerics** (Section 8) making all of the above computable, and a discussion of
   the model's limits (Section 9).

## 2. The model

Throughout, $p$ is a prime and $V$ is a finite vector space over the field $\mathbb{F}_p$ of $p$
elements, with $n := \dim_{\mathbb{F}_p} V$. Recall the two facts we shall use constantly.

> **Lemma 2.1 (Cardinality).** $|V| = p^{n}$.

> **Lemma 2.2 (Index of a proper subspace).** If $W \subsetneq V$ is a proper subspace then
> $|W| \cdot p \le |V|$.
>
> *Proof sketch.* Properness gives $\dim W < \dim V$, hence $\dim W + 1 \le \dim V$, and applying
> Lemma 2.1 twice, $|W|\cdot p = p^{\dim W + 1} \le p^{\dim V} = |V|$. $\square$

Equality in Lemma 2.2 characterises **hyperplanes**: subspaces of codimension exactly one, i.e. of
index exactly $p$. These are the "tightest" obstructions and will supply our lower bounds.

**Definition 2.3 (Exclusive subspace).** The boundary token's exclusive subspace at width $k$ is
modelled by a tuple $v = (v_1,\dots,v_k) \in V^{k}$, each entry drawn independently and uniformly
from $V$. One draw represents one exclusive dimension. The sample space is $V^{k}$, of size
$p^{nk}$, with counting measure normalised by $|V|^{k}$.

**Definition 2.4 (Obstructions).** The trained dynamics' degenerate directions are modelled by a
finite family $W_1,\dots,W_m$ of *proper* subspaces $W_j \subsetneq V$, $m \ge 1$.

**Definition 2.5 (Failure).** The tuple $v$ **fails** if some obstruction swallows it entirely:
$$\exists j \in \{1,\dots,m\} \ \ \forall i \in \{1,\dots,k\}: \ v_i \in W_j.$$
Write $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) := \#\{v \in V^k : v \text{ fails}\}$,
$$P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) := \frac{N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)}{|V|^{k}}, \qquad
P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) := 1 - P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k).$$

**Interpretation.** The exclusive subspace is useful only if it is not entirely contained in a
direction along which the readout is blind. Containment of the *whole* span is the right failure
condition: a single surviving direction outside every obstruction is enough for a linear readout to
separate the boundary symbol. Note that this makes the model *maximally generous* to the naive
"one dimension suffices" hypothesis — and it still fails.

**Lemma 2.6 (Exact single-obstruction count).** For a fixed subspace $W \le V$,
$$\#\{v \in V^k : \forall i,\ v_i \in W\} = |W|^{k}.$$
*Proof.* The set of such tuples is in bijection with $W^k$ by restricting codomains. $\square$

## 3. The monotone-ramp theorem

### 3.1 Monotonicity

> **Theorem 3.1 (Prefix fibration).** For every $k$,
> $$N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k+1) \;\le\; N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)\cdot |V|.$$

*Proof sketch.* Map a failing $(k+1)$-tuple $v$ to the pair $\big((v_1,\dots,v_k),\, v_{k+1}\big)$.
The first component is again failing, since the obstruction $W_j$ witnessing the failure of $v$
contains all of $v_1,\dots,v_k$ in particular. The map is injective, because the pair determines $v$
coordinatewise. Its codomain has cardinality $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)\cdot|V|$. $\square$

> **Theorem 3.2 (Monotone ramp).** $P_{\mathrm{fail}}$ is antitone and hence $P_{\mathrm{cure}}$ is
> monotone nondecreasing in $k$.

*Proof sketch.* Divide Theorem 3.1 by $|V|^{k+1}$:
$$P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k+1) = \frac{N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k+1)}{|V|^{k+1}}
\le \frac{N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)\,|V|}{|V|^{k+1}} = P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k).$$
Antitonicity for all pairs $k \le l$ follows from the successor step by induction. $\square$

This is the first empirical claim, now a theorem, and note how little it uses: no structure on the
obstruction family beyond being a family of subspaces (properness is not even needed here).

### 3.2 No capacity cliff

> **Theorem 3.3 (Strict positivity of failure).** If $m \ge 1$ then $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) > 0$, hence
> $P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) > 0$ and $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) < 1$, for *every* finite width $k$.

*Proof.* The zero tuple $v_1 = \dots = v_k = 0$ lies in $W_1$ coordinatewise, since every subspace
contains $0$. $\square$

Trivial to prove, and decisive in consequence:

> **Corollary 3.4 (No cliff).** There is no $k_0$ such that $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = 0$ for all
> $k < k_0$ and $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = 1$ for all $k \ge k_0$. In particular the reliability curve
> is never a step function.

*Proof.* Such a $k_0$ would give $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k_0) = 1$, contradicting Theorem 3.3. $\square$

The empirical $100\%$ at $k = 4, 8$ is therefore a small-sample artefact under the model, not a
witnessed certainty; the model predicts that a sufficiently large replicate count at any fixed width
will exhibit failures.

### 3.3 Convergence to certainty

> **Theorem 3.5 (Union bound).** If every $W_j$ is proper then
> $$P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) \;\le\; \frac{m}{p^{k}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Failing tuples inject into the disjoint union over $j$ of the tuples contained in
$W_j$, so $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) \le \sum_{j} |W_j|^{k}$ by Lemma 2.6. By Lemma 2.2, $|W_j| \cdot p
\le |V|$, hence $|W_j|^k p^k \le |V|^k$ termwise. Summing over $j$ gives $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)\,p^k
\le m |V|^k$, which is the claim after division. $\square$

**Definition 3.6 (Monotone ramp).** A reliability curve $P : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a
**monotone ramp** if (a) $P$ is monotone nondecreasing, (b) $P(k) < 1$ for all $k$, and (c)
$P(k) \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$.

> **Theorem 3.7 (Main law: the exclusive-width reliability curve is a monotone ramp).**
> For $m \ge 1$ obstructions, all proper, the curve $k \mapsto P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k)$ is a monotone
> ramp.

*Proof sketch.* (a) is Theorem 3.2, (b) is Theorem 3.3. For (c), squeeze:
$1 - m p^{-k} \le P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \le 1$ by Theorem 3.5, and $m p^{-k} \to 0$ because $p \ge 2$.
$\square$

> **Corollary 3.8 (No critical width).** A monotone ramp admits no width $k$ beyond which success is
> certain; yet for any $c < 1$ there is a width beyond which $P(l) > c$.

*Proof.* The first is (b); the second is (c) with the definition of convergence, plus monotonicity.
$\square$

Corollary 3.8 is the precise form of the phrase "no sharp width, but a real design rule": success is
never guaranteed, but any prescribed reliability level is reachable, at a width we now compute.

## 4. The two-sided envelope and the design rule

Theorem 3.5 bounds the failure mass from above. A matching lower bound needs one obstruction to be
as large as properness permits.

> **Lemma 4.1.** For each $j$, $|W_j|^{k} \le N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)$.

*Proof.* Tuples contained in $W_j$ are failing tuples; apply Lemma 2.6. $\square$

> **Theorem 4.2 (Hyperplane lower bound).** If some $W_j$ satisfies $|W_j|\cdot p = |V|$ (a
> hyperplane) then $P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) \ge p^{-k}$.

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 4.1, $N_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) \ge |W_j|^k = (|V|/p)^k$; divide by $|V|^k$.
$\square$

> **Theorem 4.3 (Two-sided geometric envelope).** With all $W_j$ proper and at least one a
> hyperplane,
> $$\frac{1}{p^{k}} \;\le\; 1 - P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \;\le\; \frac{m}{p^{k}} \qquad \text{for all } k.$$

The deficiency of the ramp is thus *exactly* of order $p^{-k}$: the two bounds have the same
geometric ratio and differ only by the constant $m$. This rules out both a super-geometric collapse
(a cliff) and a sub-geometric decay (a plateau).

> **Theorem 4.4 (Sufficient width).** If $m \le \epsilon\, p^{k}$ — i.e. $k \ge \log_p(m/\epsilon)$ —
> then $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \ge 1 - \epsilon$.

> **Theorem 4.5 (Necessary width).** If some obstruction is a hyperplane and
> $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \ge 1 - \epsilon$, then $p^{-k} \le \epsilon$, i.e.
> $k \ge \log_p(1/\epsilon)$.

*Proof sketches.* Immediate from Theorems 3.5 and 4.2 respectively, rearranged. $\square$

**The design rule.** The necessary and sufficient widths differ by the additive constant
$\log_p m$ — never by a multiplicative factor. Hence:

> The *location* of the reliability knee is determined to within $\log_p m$, even though the knee has
> no *sharpness* at all.

Calibrating to the data: at $p = 2$, a target of $\epsilon = 0.05$ needs $k \ge \log_2 20 \approx
4.3$ and is guaranteed by $k \ge \log_2(20 m)$. With $m$ in the range $8$–$16$ obstruction modes,
this reproduces the empirical prescription: **reserve at least four exclusive dimensions** for a
boundary token; two is near-robust; one is fragile.

## 5. The exact curve in the single-hyperplane case

Take $m = 1$ with the single obstruction $W$ a hyperplane, $|W|\cdot p = |V|$.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Exact curve).** $P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) = p^{-k}$ and hence
> $$P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = 1 - p^{-k}.$$

*Proof sketch.* With one obstruction, failing tuples are exactly the tuples contained in $W$, of
which there are $|W|^k = (|V|/p)^k$ by Lemma 2.6. Divide by $|V|^k$. $\square$

> **Corollary 5.2 (Zero exclusive dimensions never work).** $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(0) = 0$.

> **Corollary 5.3 (The first exclusive dimension is not sufficient).**
> $$P_{\mathrm{cure}}(1) = 1 - \frac{1}{p} < 1.$$

This is the formal refutation of the "any exclusive dimension suffices" reading. Exclusivity is not
Boolean: the first private direction buys a fixed fraction $1 - 1/p$ of the reliability budget, and
the remainder must be bought dimension by dimension.

> **Theorem 5.4 (Strict monotonicity).** $k \mapsto P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k)$ is strictly increasing:
> every extra exclusive dimension strictly helps.

> **Theorem 5.5 (Sublinear benefit).** The marginal gain of the $(k+1)$-st exclusive dimension is
> $$P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k+1) - P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = \frac{p-1}{p^{k+1}},$$
> and this quantity is strictly decreasing in $k$.

*Proof sketch.* Substitute Theorem 5.1 and simplify: $p^{-k} - p^{-(k+1)} = (p-1)p^{-(k+1)}$.
Strict decrease follows since $p > 1$. $\square$

Diminishing returns, quantitatively: the reliability gained per dimension is itself geometric.

> **Theorem 5.6 (Total failure mass).**
> $$\sum_{k \ge 0} P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) = \sum_{k \ge 0} p^{-k} = \frac{p}{p-1}.$$

This arithmetic invariant of the ramp is the seed of Section 7.

**A concrete witness.** The hypotheses are satisfiable: take $V = \mathbb{F}_p$ itself with the
single obstruction $W = \{0\}$, which is proper (since $\mathbb{F}_p$ is nontrivial) and of index
exactly $p$. Then the curve is literally $1 - p^{-k}$, and at $p = 2$ the widths used in the
experiment give

| $k$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| model $P_{\mathrm{cure}}$ | $0$ | $\tfrac12 = 0.5$ | $\tfrac34 = 0.75$ | $\tfrac{15}{16} = 0.9375$ | $\tfrac{255}{256} \approx 0.996$ |
| measured | $0.25$ | $0.33$ | $0.83$ | $1.00$ | $1.00$ |

The agreement is qualitative and structural rather than numerical — the model is not fitted — but it
reproduces the ordering, the knee between $k=1$ and $k=4$, the sublinearity, and (crucially) the
prediction that the two measured $1.00$s are really $0.94$ and $0.996$ sampled six times each. The
probability of observing six consecutive successes at true rate $0.9375$ is $0.68$; at $0.996$ it is
$0.98$. Both observations are unsurprising under the ramp model.

## 6. Are the exclusive dimensions really exclusive? A $q$-Pochhammer bound

The model draws $k$ vectors and calls their span the token's $k$-dimensional exclusive subspace. If
the draws are dependent, the token has fewer private directions than advertised and the phrase "$k$
exclusive dimensions" overclaims. We quantify the risk.

**Definition 6.1.** Let $P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k) := \dfrac{\#\{v \in V^{k} : v_1,\dots,v_k \text{
linearly independent}\}}{|V|^{k}}$.

> **Theorem 6.2 ($q$-Pochhammer formula).** For $k \le n = \dim V$,
> $$P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k) = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{p^{i}}{p^{n}}\right).$$

*Proof sketch.* The classical count of linearly independent $k$-tuples over $\mathbb{F}_q$ with
$q = p$ is $\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(p^{n} - p^{i})$: having chosen $i$ independent vectors, the $(i+1)$-st
must lie outside their span, a subspace of exactly $p^{i}$ elements. Factor $p^{n}$ out of each of
the $k$ terms and divide by $|V|^k = (p^n)^k$. $\square$

The product on the right is a truncated $q$-Pochhammer symbol $(p^{-n}; p)_k$ in disguise, the same
object that counts flags and subspaces over finite fields. To turn it into a usable bound we need an
elementary inequality.

> **Lemma 6.3 (Weierstrass product inequality).** If $0 \le a_i \le 1$ for $i < k$ then
> $$1 - \sum_{i<k} a_i \;\le\; \prod_{i<k}(1 - a_i).$$

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $k$. The base case is $1 \le 1$. For the step, write $S =
\sum_{i<k} a_i \ge 0$ and use the inductive hypothesis multiplied by the nonnegative factor
$1 - a_k$:
$$\prod_{i \le k}(1-a_i) \ge (1 - S)(1 - a_k) = 1 - S - a_k + S a_k \ge 1 - S - a_k,$$
the last step because $S a_k \ge 0$. $\square$

> **Theorem 6.4 (Genericity bound).** For $k \le n$,
> $$P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k) \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{p^{k} - 1}{(p-1)\,p^{n}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply Lemma 6.3 to $a_i = p^{i}/p^{n}$ (each lies in $[0,1]$ because $i < k \le n$),
then sum the geometric series $\sum_{i<k} p^{i} = (p^{k}-1)/(p-1)$. $\square$

> **Theorem 6.5 (Genericity is antitone).** For $k + 1 \le n$, $P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k+1) \le
> P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k)$.

*Proof sketch.* The product for $k+1$ is that for $k$ times the factor $1 - p^{k-n} \le 1$, and the
product for $k$ is nonnegative. $\square$

So genericity *decays* in $k$, in the opposite direction to reliability. In the regime of the
experiment ($p = 2$, ambient hidden width $n = 192$, $k \le 8$) the genericity loss is at most
$(2^{8}-1)\cdot 2^{-192} \approx 2^{-184}$: utterly negligible, so the model of Sections 3–5 is not
vacuous and the reliability term alone governs. But the two effects genuinely compete, and combining
them is the point.

> **Theorem 6.6 (Reliability and genericity together).** For proper obstructions $W_1,\dots,W_m$ and
> $k \le n$, the probability that the drawn tuple is **both** linearly independent **and** escapes
> every obstruction satisfies
> $$\mathbb{P}\big(\text{independent} \wedge \text{cured}\big) \;\ge\;
> 1 - \frac{m}{p^{k}} - \frac{p^{k}-1}{(p-1)\,p^{n}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Let $I$, $G$, $F$ be the sets of independent tuples, of independent-and-cured tuples,
and of failing tuples respectively. Then $I \subseteq G \cup F$, so $|I| \le |G| + |F|$; divide by
$|V|^k$ to get $P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k) \le \mathbb{P}(G) + P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k)$, and substitute the
lower bound of Theorem 6.4 and the upper bound of Theorem 3.5. $\square$

**The interior optimum.** Write the bound as $f(k) = 1 - m p^{-k} - (p^{k}-1)/((p-1)p^{n})$. The
first error term decays geometrically in $k$; the second grows geometrically in $k$. Their sum is
convex in $p^{k}$ and minimised in the interior, near
$$k^{*} \approx \tfrac12\Big(n + \log_p\big(m(p-1)\big)\Big),$$
i.e. roughly half the ambient width, with maximum value $1 - \Theta(p^{-n/2})$. This is the
structural reason the reliability curve cannot be a cliff: a cliff is what one monotone force
produces; here two opposed forces act, and the resulting optimum is a smooth interior maximum, not an
edge.

## 7. The Euler-product bridge

Theorem 5.6 computed the total failure mass at characteristic $p$ as $\sum_{k\ge 0} p^{-k} =
(1 - p^{-1})^{-1}$. That is the local Euler factor of the Riemann zeta function at $p$. The
coincidence is not accidental — the model *is* a geometric distribution over widths with ratio
$1/p$ — but it becomes a genuine cross-domain identity once we let the characteristic vary.

**Definition 7.1 (Damped failure-mass series).** For $s \in \mathbb{C}$, weight a width-$k$ failure
by $p^{-ks}$ rather than $p^{-k}$, and set
$$Z_p(s) := \sum_{k \ge 0} \left(p^{-s}\right)^{k}.$$

> **Lemma 7.2.** For $p \ge 2$ and $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$ we have $|p^{-s}| = p^{-\operatorname{Re}
> s} < 1$.

> **Theorem 7.3 (Local factor).** For $p \ge 2$ and $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$,
> $$Z_p(s) = \left(1 - p^{-s}\right)^{-1}.$$

*Proof.* Geometric series, convergent by Lemma 7.2. $\square$

> **Theorem 7.4 (The $s = 1$ case, from the model).** In the single-hyperplane model over
> $\mathbb{F}_p$, the total failure mass of the reliability ramp is
> $$\sum_{k \ge 0} P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) = \left(1 - p^{-1}\right)^{-1}.$$

This is computed from the model — via the exact curve of Theorem 5.1 — rather than postulated.

> **Theorem 7.5 (Bridge).** For $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$,
> $$\prod_{p \text{ prime}} Z_p(s) \;=\; \zeta(s).$$

*Proof sketch.* Substitute Theorem 7.3 factorwise and invoke the Euler product for $\zeta$. $\square$

> **Corollary 7.6 (Closed form at $s = 2$).**
> $$\prod_{p \text{ prime}} Z_p(2) \;=\; \zeta(2) \;=\; \frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.$$

**What this means, and what it doesn't.** The left-hand side is assembled purely from the
finite-field reliability model; the right-hand side is the analytic zeta function. No claim is being
made that number theory governs neural networks. The content is structural: the ramp's failure
spectrum, as a function of width, has exactly the shape of a local Euler factor, and the aggregate
over characteristics is $\zeta$. And it carries a moral about the cliff/ramp dichotomy that is
sharper than any of the finite statements above:

> An Euler factor exists because the local series is *infinite*. A capacity cliff would make the
> failure mass a finite sum, collapsing the factor to a polynomial and destroying the identity. The
> zeta bridge holds precisely because the ramp never terminates.

## 8. Algorithms and numerics

Everything above is computable, and the computations are useful for calibration.

**Algorithm A (Exact multi-obstruction failure probability by inclusion–exclusion).** Given the
codimensions of all intersections $\bigcap_{j \in S} W_j$ over nonempty $S \subseteq \{1,\dots,m\}$,
$$P_{\mathrm{fail}}(k) = \sum_{\emptyset \ne S \subseteq \{1,\dots,m\}} (-1)^{|S|+1}\,
p^{-k\,\mathrm{codim}\left(\bigcap_{j \in S} W_j\right)},$$
because the tuples contained in a subspace $U$ number $|U|^k = p^{-k\,\mathrm{codim}\,U}|V|^k$ and
inclusion–exclusion over the lattice generated by the obstructions is exact. Cost: $O(2^{m})$ terms,
each a single power. This refines the union bound of Theorem 3.5 to an identity, and exhibits
$P_{\mathrm{fail}}$ as a finite Dirichlet polynomial in $p^{-k}$ whose leading term is
$p^{-k\,\mathrm{codim}}$ for the minimal codimension present.

**Algorithm B (Knee localisation).** Given $p$, $m$, and a tolerance $\epsilon$, return the pair
$\big(\lceil \log_p(1/\epsilon)\rceil,\ \lceil\log_p(m/\epsilon)\rceil\big)$ of necessary and
sufficient widths (Theorems 4.4–4.5). Constant cost; the output is a bracketing interval of length
$\lceil \log_p m\rceil$ that always contains the true minimal width.

**Algorithm C (Genericity product).** Evaluate $\prod_{i<k}(1 - p^{i-n})$ exactly in rational
arithmetic and compare against the closed-form bound $1 - (p^k-1)/((p-1)p^n)$; $O(k)$ multiplications.
Used to certify that the drawn directions really are $k$-dimensional.

**Algorithm D (Joint optimum search).** Maximise $f(k) = 1 - m p^{-k} - (p^{k}-1)/((p-1)p^{n})$ over
$0 \le k \le n$ by direct evaluation ($O(n)$) or by rounding the analytic optimum $k^{*} \approx
\frac12(n + \log_p(m(p-1)))$ and checking its two neighbours ($O(1)$).

**Algorithm E (Euler-product truncation).** Evaluate $\prod_{p \le X}(1 - p^{-s})^{-1}$ over primes
up to $X$ by sieving and compare against $\zeta(s)$; the tail error is $O(X^{1-\operatorname{Re}s})$,
so convergence is fast for $s = 2$ and visibly slow near $s = 1$ — a numerical rendering of the fact
that the total failure mass at $s = 1$ diverges when aggregated over characteristics.

**Sampling check.** One can also verify the exact curves by Monte Carlo: draw $k$-tuples uniformly
from $\mathbb{F}_p^{n}$ and test containment in a fixed hyperplane $\{x : \langle a, x\rangle = 0\}$.
The empirical failure rate converges to $p^{-k}$, and the empirical independence rate to the
$q$-Pochhammer product, both at the usual $O(N^{-1/2})$ rate.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 What the model explains

Every qualitative feature of the measured table has a counterpart theorem.

| Empirical observation | Theorem |
|---|---|
| More exclusive width never hurts | Monotone ramp (Thm 3.2) |
| Widths $4$, $8$ show no failures | Strict positivity (Thm 3.3): a finite-sample effect |
| No sharp critical width | No cliff (Cor. 3.4), interior optimum (§6) |
| One exclusive dimension is not sufficient | $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(1) = 1 - 1/p$ (Cor. 5.3) |
| Benefit is sublinear | Marginal gain $(p-1)p^{-(k+1)}$, strictly decreasing (Thm 5.5) |
| Same failure mechanism at every width | Width enters only through the exponent $k$; the obstruction family is width-independent |
| "Reserve $\ge 4$ exclusive dimensions" | Design rule (Thms 4.4–4.5) with $p=2$, $m \approx 8$–$16$ |

### 9.2 What the model does not claim

The obstruction model is a *caricature*: it replaces a trained nonlinear recurrence by a fixed finite
family of linear degeneracies and replaces optimisation randomness by uniform sampling. It predicts
the *shape* of the reliability curve, not its numerical values; the fitted correspondence between the
model's $p, m$ and any particular architecture is not established here. In particular the empirical
$k=0$ value of $0.25$ exceeds the model's $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(0) = 0$, which is expected: with no
exclusive dimensions a real system can still succeed by other means (a fortunate shared-subspace
configuration), whereas the model's width-$0$ tuple is vacuously contained in every obstruction.

The statistical caveats of the underlying measurement should also be carried forward honestly: six
replicates per width make the $k{=}1 \to k{=}2$ jump alone insignificant (Fisher $\approx 0.24$); the
empirical case rests on the monotone ordering across four widths together with much larger merged
anchor samples at the extreme widths. Intermediate widths $k = 3$ and $k = 5$–$7$ remain untested, so
the exact location of the knee inside $(1,4)$ is empirically open — though the model brackets it to
within $\log_p m$.

### 9.3 Future directions

Three conjectures crystallise the open problems.

**C1. Exact multi-obstruction curve via subspace Möbius inversion.** The inclusion–exclusion identity
of Algorithm A should be established as a theorem, yielding in particular that $k \mapsto
P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k)$ is *strictly* increasing whenever $m \ge 1$ — closing the gap between the proved
antitonicity of $P_{\mathrm{fail}}$ and strict monotonicity, currently proved only for $m = 1$. The
key insight is that inclusion–exclusion over the lattice generated by the obstructions turns the
failure count into a signed sum of exact subspace counts, each already a power of $p$, so the entire
curve is a finite Dirichlet polynomial in $p^{-k}$ whose leading term forces strict decrease of the
failure mass. The exact single-subspace count and the union bound are already in hand; only the
alternating-sum bookkeeping and the Gaussian-binomial computation of intersection codimensions
remain.

**C2. The knee is at $k = \lceil \log_p m \rceil$.** Define the knee $k^{*}(m,p) := \min\{k :
1 - P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \le 1/2\}$. We conjecture $\lceil\log_p m\rceil \le k^{*}(m,p) \le
\lceil\log_p(2m)\rceil$, so that the empirical "reserve $\ge 4$ exclusive dimensions" rule is exactly
the statement that the system has $m \approx 8$–$16$ obstruction modes at $p = 2$. The two-sided
envelope of Theorem 4.3 already pins the deficiency between $p^{-k}$ and $mp^{-k}$, so the knee can
move only by an additive $\log_p m$, never by a multiplicative factor: a threshold *location* result
in the absence of threshold *sharpness*. What remains is a monotone-inversion lemma for
$k \mapsto m p^{-k}$.

**C3. The ramp/anti-ramp trade-off has an interior optimum.** The lower bound $f(k) = 1 - mp^{-k} -
(p^{k}-1)/((p-1)p^{n})$ of Theorem 6.6 should be maximised at $k^{*} = \lfloor (n + \log_p(m(p-1)))/2
\rfloor + O(1)$ — the best exclusive width is roughly $n/2$ — with maximum value $1 - \Theta(p^{-n/2})$.
The reliability error decays like $p^{-k}$ while the genericity error grows like $p^{k-n}$, so the
sum is a convex Laurent-type expression in $p^{k}$ with an explicit minimiser.

Beyond these: a mechanistic reading of the $k = 1$ case (measuring the boundary token's exclusive
coordinate at cure versus at failure), and transfer of the exclusive-dimension design rule to
full-scale systems with a genuine exclusive-dimension boundary input.

### 9.4 Methodological coda

The broadest lesson is about how thresholds are inferred. At any width where the true failure
probability is, say, $0.2$, five out of six replicates look like a clean success — and a
single-replicate study looks like a discovered threshold. Ramps masquerade as cliffs routinely. The
remedy is not more precise measurement at one width but measurement across several widths against a
model that says what the alternatives look like. The theorems above supply exactly that: a family of
curves in which monotonicity, the insufficiency of the first dimension, diminishing returns, geometric
approach to certainty, and the strict impossibility of attaining it are all consequences of one
elementary counting argument.

## 10. Summary of results

1. **Monotone ramp.** $k \mapsto P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k)$ is nondecreasing (prefix fibration), strictly
   below $1$ for every finite $k$ (zero tuple), and converges to $1$ (union bound).
2. **No capacity cliff.** The reliability curve is never a step function; no finite exclusive width
   certifies success.
3. **Two-sided geometric envelope.** $p^{-k} \le 1 - P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) \le m p^{-k}$, hence
   necessary width $\log_p(1/\epsilon)$ and sufficient width $\log_p(m/\epsilon)$ for reliability
   $1-\epsilon$: the knee is located to within $\log_p m$.
4. **Exact hyperplane curve.** $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(k) = 1 - p^{-k}$, so $P_{\mathrm{cure}}(1) =
   1 - 1/p$ — the first exclusive dimension is not sufficient — and the marginal gain
   $(p-1)p^{-(k+1)}$ strictly decreases. At $p = 2$ the predicted values at the tested widths are
   $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, \tfrac{15}{16}, \tfrac{255}{256}$.
5. **$q$-Pochhammer genericity.** $P_{\mathrm{ind}}(k) = \prod_{i<k}(1 - p^{i-n}) \ge
   1 - (p^{k}-1)/((p-1)p^{n})$, decreasing in $k$; the model's exclusive dimensions are genuinely
   exclusive in the experimental regime.
6. **Interior optimum.** $\mathbb{P}(\text{independent} \wedge \text{cured}) \ge 1 - mp^{-k} -
   (p^{k}-1)/((p-1)p^{n})$, a two-sided window whose error terms move oppositely in $k$.
7. **Euler bridge.** The ramp's total failure mass at characteristic $p$ is $(1-p^{-1})^{-1}$; the
   width-damped masses multiply over all characteristics to $\zeta(s)$ for $\operatorname{Re} s > 1$,
   and to $\pi^{2}/6$ at $s = 2$.

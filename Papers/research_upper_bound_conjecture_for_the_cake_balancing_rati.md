# Balancing Ratios of Circular Partitions: Windowing Monotonicity and Full-Period Balance

## Abstract

We study the balance of circular partitions generated by placing points on a
circle, where the arc lengths form a positive periodic sequence. For a window
length $r \ge 1$ we define the $r$-window ratio as the ratio of the largest to
the smallest sum of $r$ consecutive arcs, taken over all cyclic starting
positions. This finite-stage quantity, denoted $\mu^r$, generalizes the classical
single-gap ratio $\mu^1 = g_{\max}/g_{\min}$. We prove two structural pillars.
First, a **windowing monotonicity** theorem: for every $r \ge 1$,
$1 \le \mu^r \le \mu^1$, so aggregating consecutive arcs never worsens balance.
Second, a **full-period balance** theorem: whenever the window length is a
positive multiple $kn$ of the period $n$, every window has identical length
$kL$ (where $L$ is the total circumference), so $\mu^{kn} = 1$ exactly. Together
these results show that the balancing ratio sequence oscillates and returns to
perfect balance infinitely often, explaining why the long-run balancing ratio
must be defined as a limit superior rather than a limit. We illustrate the theory
with the perfectly uniform partition (window ratio $1$ at every scale) and the
three-point van der Corput partition $\{1/4, 1/4, 1/2\}$, which realizes the
benchmark single-gap ratio $2$. These results form the structural scaffold for
the upper-bound conjecture $\mu_r \le 2r/p + 1$ for recipes of length $p$.

**Keywords:** circular partition, balancing ratio, window sum, periodic sequence,
van der Corput sequence, discrepancy, fair division.

## 1. Introduction

A recurring theme in combinatorial geometry and the theory of uniform
distribution is the following: place a sequence of points on a circle so that,
at every stage, consecutive arcs are as equal as possible. Classical results in
the de Bruijn–Erdős tradition of cake-cutting and in the theory of low-discrepancy
sequences quantify the unavoidable imbalance. In this paper we isolate and prove
two clean structural principles governing the *balancing ratio* of a periodic
circular partition, and we assemble the framework in which the sharp upper-bound
conjecture is naturally stated.

The two principles are intuitive but, once made precise, surprisingly powerful:

1. **Aggregation smooths imbalance.** Serving blocks of $r$ consecutive arcs is
   never less fair than serving single arcs.
2. **The period is a reset.** Whenever the serving block is a whole number of
   full loops around the circle, every block is exactly equal — fairness is
   perfect.

The second principle forces the balancing ratio sequence to oscillate and to
touch its minimum value $1$ infinitely often. Consequently, the meaningful
long-run measure of imbalance is the limit superior of the ratio sequence.

## 2. Setting and definitions

We model a circular partition by its sequence of gap (arc) lengths.

**Definition 2.1 (Circular partition).** A *circular partition* consists of a
positive integer $n \ge 1$ (the number of distinct arcs) together with a function
$g : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ (the gap-length sequence) satisfying:
- positivity: $g_i > 0$ for all $i$;
- periodicity: $g_{i+n} = g_i$ for all $i$.

Thus the circle carries $n$ arcs $g_0, \dots, g_{n-1}$, repeated cyclically. A
consequence of periodicity is that the gap depends only on the index modulo the
period: $g_i = g_{i \bmod n}$.

**Definition 2.2 (Extremal single gaps).** The *maximum gap* and *minimum gap*
are
$$g_{\max} = \max_{0 \le i < n} g_i, \qquad g_{\min} = \min_{0 \le i < n} g_i.$$

**Definition 2.3 (Window sum).** For a window length $r \in \mathbb{N}$ and a
starting position $i \in \mathbb{N}$, the *$r$-window sum* is
$$W_r(i) = \sum_{j=0}^{r-1} g_{i+j} = g_i + g_{i+1} + \dots + g_{i+r-1}.$$

**Definition 2.4 (Extremal window sums).** Over the $n$ cyclic starting positions,
$$\mathrm{maxwin}(r) = \max_{0 \le i < n} W_r(i), \qquad
  \mathrm{minwin}(r) = \min_{0 \le i < n} W_r(i).$$

**Definition 2.5 (Balancing ratios).** The *single-gap ratio* and the
*$r$-window ratio* are
$$\mu^1 = \text{gapRatio} = \frac{g_{\max}}{g_{\min}}, \qquad
  \mu^r = \text{winRatio}(r) = \frac{\mathrm{maxwin}(r)}{\mathrm{minwin}(r)}.$$
These are the finite-stage quantities $\mu^1_n$ and $\mu^r_n$ of the underlying
mission. For a recipe $\sigma$ of length $p$ generating a periodic partition, the
long-run balancing ratio is
$$\mu_r = \limsup_{n \to \infty} \mu^r_n.$$

**Definition 2.6 (Total circumference).** The *total circumference* is
$$L = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_j.$$

A first observation, used throughout, is that $g_{\min} > 0$ (a minimum of finitely
many positive numbers), hence $L > 0$ and $g_{\max} \ge g_{\min} > 0$.

## 3. Elementary bounds

**Lemma 3.1 (Nontriviality).** $g_{\min} \le g_{\max}$, and consequently
$\mu^1 \ge 1$.

*Proof sketch.* The minimum of a nonempty finite set is at most its maximum;
dividing $g_{\max} \ge g_{\min} > 0$ by $g_{\min}$ gives $\mu^1 \ge 1$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.2 (Per-arc envelopes).** For every $i$, $g_{\min} \le g_i \le g_{\max}$.

*Proof sketch.* Reduce the index modulo $n$ using periodicity, then apply the
definitions of $g_{\min}$ and $g_{\max}$ as an infimum and supremum over
$\{0, \dots, n-1\}$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.3 (Window envelopes).** For every window length $r$ and start $i$,
$$r \cdot g_{\min} \le W_r(i) \le r \cdot g_{\max}.$$
Consequently $r \cdot g_{\min} \le \mathrm{minwin}(r)$ and
$\mathrm{maxwin}(r) \le r \cdot g_{\max}$.

*Proof sketch.* Summing the per-arc envelopes of Lemma 3.2 over the $r$ terms of
the window yields the two-sided bound; taking the extrema over starting positions
preserves the inequalities. $\square$

**Lemma 3.4 (Positivity of the minimum window).** If $r \ge 1$ then
$\mathrm{minwin}(r) > 0$.

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 3.3, $\mathrm{minwin}(r) \ge r \cdot g_{\min} > 0$ since
$r \ge 1$ and $g_{\min} > 0$. $\square$

## 4. Windowing monotonicity

The first main theorem states that aggregation into windows never degrades
balance.

**Theorem 4.1 (Windowing improves balance).** For every window length $r \ge 1$,
$$\mu^r = \text{winRatio}(r) \le \text{gapRatio} = \mu^1.$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.4 the denominator $\mathrm{minwin}(r)$ is positive, so the
ratio is well defined. Using the envelope bounds of Lemma 3.3, together with
$g_{\max} \ge 0$ and $r \cdot g_{\min} > 0$, we estimate
$$
\frac{\mathrm{maxwin}(r)}{\mathrm{minwin}(r)}
\;\le\; \frac{r \cdot g_{\max}}{\mathrm{minwin}(r)}
\;\le\; \frac{r \cdot g_{\max}}{r \cdot g_{\min}}
\;=\; \frac{g_{\max}}{g_{\min}}.
$$
The first inequality replaces the numerator with its upper envelope; the second
replaces the denominator with its lower envelope (a smaller positive
denominator only increases the quotient); the final equality cancels the common
factor $r > 0$. This is exactly $\mu^r \le \mu^1$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Lower bound).** For every $r \ge 1$, $\mu^r \ge 1$.

*Proof sketch.* Since $\mathrm{minwin}(r) \le \mathrm{maxwin}(r)$ and the
denominator is positive (Lemma 3.4), the quotient is at least $1$. $\square$

Combining Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 gives the fundamental containment
$$1 \le \mu^r \le \mu^1 \qquad (r \ge 1).$$
The single-gap ratio $\mu^1$ is the *envelope* of the entire window-ratio
profile; no window can be less balanced than a single arc.

## 5. Full-period balance

The second main theorem is the exact recurrence of perfect balance. Its engine
is a phase-invariance identity for full-period sums.

**Lemma 5.1 (Phase invariance of full-period sums).** For every starting
position $i$,
$$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{i+j} = L.$$
That is, a window of exactly $n$ consecutive arcs has length equal to the total
circumference, independently of where it begins.

*Proof.* We induct on $i$. For $i = 0$ the sum is $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_j = L$ by
definition. For the inductive step, we show the full-period sum is unchanged when
the start advances by one, i.e.
$$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{(k+1)+j} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{k+j}.$$
Consider the sum over the extended range $\{0, \dots, n\}$ of $g_{k+j}$. Peeling
off the last term gives
$$\sum_{j=0}^{n} g_{k+j} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{k+j} + g_{k+n},$$
while peeling off the first term (reindexing $j \mapsto j+1$) gives
$$\sum_{j=0}^{n} g_{k+j} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{k+(j+1)} + g_{k}.$$
Equating the two and using periodicity $g_{k+n} = g_k$, the two boundary terms
cancel, leaving $\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{k+(j+1)} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} g_{k+j}$, i.e.
the shift-by-one invariance. Applying the inductive hypothesis completes the
proof. $\square$

Intuitively: advancing the start by one arc drops the front arc $g_k$ and adds a
back arc $g_{k+n}$ of *equal* length, so a full-loop sum never changes.

**Lemma 5.2 (Multi-period sums).** For every $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and start $i$, a
window of $k$ full periods has length
$$W_{kn}(i) = \sum_{j=0}^{kn-1} g_{i+j} = k \cdot L.$$

*Proof sketch.* Induct on $k$. The case $k = 0$ is the empty sum. For the step,
split the range $\{0, \dots, (m+1)n - 1\}$ into the first $mn$ indices and the
last $n$ indices. The first block is $W_{mn}(i) = mL$ by the inductive
hypothesis; the second block is a full-period sum starting at $i + mn$, equal to
$L$ by Lemma 5.1. Adding gives $(m+1)L$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Perfect balance at the period).** The window ratio at window
length equal to the period is exactly $1$:
$$\mu^{n} = \text{winRatio}(n) = 1.$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 5.1 the function $i \mapsto W_n(i)$ is constant, equal to $L$.
Hence $\mathrm{maxwin}(n) = \mathrm{minwin}(n) = L$, and since $L > 0$ the ratio
is $L/L = 1$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Perfect balance at every multiple of the period).** For every
integer $k \ge 1$,
$$\mu^{kn} = \text{winRatio}(kn) = 1.$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 5.2 the function $i \mapsto W_{kn}(i)$ is constant, equal to
$kL$. Since $k \ge 1$ and $L > 0$, the constant $kL$ is nonzero, so
$\mathrm{maxwin}(kn) = \mathrm{minwin}(kn) = kL$ and the ratio equals $kL/kL = 1$.
$\square$

**Corollary 5.5 (Oscillation and the limit superior).** The balancing ratio
sequence $r \mapsto \mu^r$ returns to its minimum value $1$ at every positive
multiple of the period. In particular, if the partition is non-uniform so that
$\mu^1 > 1$, the sequence does not converge; the appropriate long-run measure of
imbalance is the limit superior $\mu_r = \limsup \mu^r_n$, which captures the
peaks between successive resets.

## 6. Extremal examples

**Example 6.1 (Uniform partition).** Let all arcs have equal length $1/n$. Then
every window of length $r$ has the same sum $r/n$ regardless of starting
position, so
$$\mu^r = 1 \quad \text{for every } r \ge 1.$$
The uniform partition is the unique partition that is perfectly balanced at every
window scale simultaneously; it saturates the lower bound of Theorem 4.2 for all
$r$.

**Example 6.2 (van der Corput three-point partition).** The first three points of
the base-2 van der Corput sequence produce the periodic arc pattern
$$g = \left(\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \dots\right),
\qquad n = 3.$$
Formally, $g_i = \tfrac12$ if $i \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ and $g_i = \tfrac14$
otherwise. The extremal gaps are $g_{\max} = \tfrac12$ and $g_{\min} = \tfrac14$,
so the single-gap ratio is exactly
$$\mu^1 = \frac{1/2}{1/4} = 2,$$
the de Bruijn–Erdős cake-cutting benchmark. By Theorem 4.1 every window ratio is
at most $2$, and by Theorem 5.4 the ratio drops to exactly $1$ at every window
length that is a multiple of $3$ (each block of $3$ arcs has length
$\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 = 1$). The imbalance therefore lives entirely in
the short, non-period-aligned windows.

## 7. Algorithms

The framework is fully computable for any explicitly given periodic gap sequence.
We describe the two core routines.

**Algorithm 7.1 (Window ratio at stage $n$).** Given the finite gap vector
$(g_0, \dots, g_{n-1})$ and a window length $r$, compute $\mu^r$ by evaluating
$W_r(i)$ for each of the $n$ cyclic starts (indices taken modulo $n$), then
dividing the maximum by the minimum. A naive implementation costs $O(nr)$; a
sliding-window / prefix-sum implementation reduces this to $O(n)$ after $O(n)$
preprocessing.

**Algorithm 7.2 (Balancing-ratio profile).** For a fixed partition, sweep $r$
over a range $1, \dots, R$ and record $\mu^r$. The profile exhibits the two
theorems visually: it stays within $[1, \mu^1]$ (Theorem 4.1) and hits exactly
$1$ at every multiple of the period (Theorem 5.4).

## 8. Applications

The circular-partition model abstracts many practical settings:

- **Recurring event scheduling.** Points on a circle model timestamps of periodic
  events; window balance measures how evenly load is distributed across shifting
  time windows.
- **Ring networks and sensor placement.** Sensors around a ring should cover arcs
  as evenly as possible; the window ratio quantifies coverage uniformity for
  groups of consecutive sensors.
- **Low-discrepancy sampling.** The van der Corput and related sequences are
  workhorses of quasi-Monte Carlo integration; the balancing ratio is a direct,
  interpretable proxy for their local uniformity.
- **Fair division.** The cake-cutting metaphor is literal: the results bound how
  unequal consecutive-slice servings can be under a fixed cutting recipe.

## 9. Discussion and the upper-bound conjecture

The two theorems bracket the conjectured sharp bound from both sides. The
perfect-balance recurrence (Theorem 5.4) pins $\mu^r = 1$ exactly whenever $r$ is
a multiple of the period $p$, so the conjectured envelope
$$\mu_r \le \frac{2r}{p} + 1$$
is *exact at the resets*. Between resets, the windowing-monotonicity principle
(Theorem 4.1) keeps the excursions controlled by the single-gap ratio. The
outstanding problem is to quantify the height of the excursions between
consecutive resets: how much surplus can accumulate in a window that wraps a
non-integer number of periods? The structure developed here reduces this from an
asymptotic mystery to a finite per-period estimate.

## 10. Future directions

Building on the two structural pillars — windowing never worsens balance
($\mu^r \le \mu^1$), and balance is exactly perfect at every multiple of the
period ($\mu^{kp} = 1$) — several directions stand out:

- **The recipe upper bound.** Prove $\mu_r \le 2r/p + 1$ for every recipe of
  length $p$ and every window $r \ge 1$, by controlling the bounded surplus of
  oversized arcs a window can absorb between two consecutive resets.
- **Sharpness of the window envelope.** Show that for every non-uniform partition
  there is a window length $r$ with $1 < \mu^r < \mu^1$, and that the envelope
  $\mu^r \le \mu^1$ is attained only at $r = 1$.
- **Optimal recipes.** Show that the minimum achievable $\limsup_r \mu_r$ over
  recipes of length $p$ decreases monotonically to $1$ at rate $\Theta(1/p)$.
- **Discrepancy bridge.** Establish $\mu_r - 1 = \Theta(r \cdot D_n)$, linking the
  balancing ratio to the star-discrepancy $D_n$ of the generating point sequence
  along the stages realizing the limit superior.

## 11. Conclusion

We have proved two structural theorems for the balancing ratio of periodic
circular partitions: windowing monotonicity ($1 \le \mu^r \le \mu^1$) and
full-period balance ($\mu^{kn} = 1$). Together they reveal the oscillatory nature
of the balancing ratio sequence and justify measuring long-run imbalance with a
limit superior. The uniform and van der Corput partitions exhibit the extreme
behaviors — perfect balance at all scales, and the benchmark ratio $2$ — and the
results assemble the exact framework in which the upper-bound conjecture
$\mu_r \le 2r/p + 1$ can be attacked.

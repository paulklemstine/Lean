## Abstract

We present a self-contained topological model in which an observer channel is represented by a topology on a common state space and the geometry observable to a finite network is the intersection of its channel topologies. This intersection, called the consensus topology, contains exactly the regions certified as open by every channel. We prove a general channel-addition criterion: after adjoining a topology $\mathcal T$ to a network with consensus $\mathcal C$, a set is open precisely when it belongs to both $\mathcal T$ and $\mathcal C$. Consequently, adding channels can only coarsen consensus, and the coarsening is strict exactly when the new channel rejects an open set shared by all previous channels. On $\mathbb R$, a one-channel network using the lower-limit topology observes that topology itself, while a two-channel network combining the lower- and upper-limit topologies observes exactly the Euclidean topology. Thus network complexities one and two yield strictly different observable geometries on the same carrier. The half-open interval $[0,1)$ is a concrete witness: it is open for the one-channel network and ceases to be open after the second channel is imposed. We discuss finite algorithms, applications to conservative multisensor fusion, and the assumptions required before interpreting the construction as a model of physical or neural observation.

## 1. Introduction

Geometry is ordinarily assigned to a space independently of those who inspect it. Nevertheless, many operational settings do not expose all subsets of a state space equally. A sensor has a family of resolvable events, an experimental procedure has a family of robustly detectable conditions, and a readout mechanism has a family of stable distinctions. When several channels must agree before a region is accepted as observable, the resulting common vocabulary may have geometric structure.

Topology supplies a minimal language for this situation. A topology records which regions are open and thereby determines neighborhoods, continuity, convergence, and boundaries. We interpret each channel topology as a family of resolvable regions. For a finite network, we define the observable geometry by unanimity: a region is open for the network exactly when every channel regards it as open. The resulting family is the intersection of the individual topologies and is itself a topology.

This choice models conservative aggregation. It should be distinguished from pooling every region seen by at least one channel. Unanimity adds constraints: each new channel can veto a previously shared region. The direction of change is therefore opposite to the informal expectation that “more observers see more.” Private information may increase, but common certification can decrease.

The principal example uses two asymmetric topologies on the real line. The lower-limit topology is generated by intervals $[a,b)$ and resolves points from a right-facing perspective. Its reflected counterpart, the upper-limit topology, is generated by intervals $(a,b]$. Each is strictly finer than ordinary Euclidean topology. Yet their intersection is exactly Euclidean topology. A one-channel lower-limit network therefore recognizes $[0,1)$ as open, whereas a two-channel network requiring both one-sided observers to agree does not.

The result is a theorem about an explicit mathematical model. No empirical conclusion about spacetime, consciousness, or biological neural networks follows without an additional encoding that maps physical measurements to topological data. This distinction is central: the framework offers a rigorous conditional mechanism and a route to falsifiable finite models, not a claim that nature necessarily implements the mechanism.

The paper proceeds from definitions to general structural results, then to the real-line collapse, computational procedures, applications, limitations, and open directions.

## 2. Topological preliminaries

### 2.1 Topologies and comparison

Let $X$ be a set. A **topology** $\mathcal T$ on $X$ is a family of subsets of $X$ satisfying:

1. $\varnothing\in\mathcal T$ and $X\in\mathcal T$;
2. if $\{U_j\}_{j\in J}\subseteq\mathcal T$, then $\bigcup_{j\in J}U_j\in\mathcal T$;
3. if $U,V\in\mathcal T$, then $U\cap V\in\mathcal T$.

The members of $\mathcal T$ are called **open sets**. A topology $\mathcal T_1$ is **finer** than $\mathcal T_2$ if $\mathcal T_2\subseteq\mathcal T_1$; equivalently, every $\mathcal T_2$-open set is $\mathcal T_1$-open. It is **strictly finer** when the inclusion is proper. Finer topologies make more subsets open and therefore encode more local distinctions.

Some order-theoretic conventions reverse the written order on topologies. To avoid ambiguity, all comparisons in this paper are stated directly as inclusions between families of open sets.

### 2.2 Bases

A family $\mathcal B$ of subsets of $X$ is a **basis** for a topology if every point lies in a basis element and, whenever $x\in B_1\cap B_2$ with $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B$, there exists $B_3\in\mathcal B$ such that $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$. The generated topology consists of arbitrary unions of basis elements. Equivalently, $U$ is open if and only if each $x\in U$ lies in some $B\in\mathcal B$ with $B\subseteq U$.

### 2.3 Three topologies on the real line

The **Euclidean topology** $\mathcal E$ on $\mathbb R$ has the open intervals $(a,b)$ as a basis.

The **lower-limit topology** $\mathcal L$ has the intervals

$$
[a,b)=\{x\in\mathbb R:a\le x<b\},\qquad a<b,
$$

as a basis. Thus $U\in\mathcal L$ exactly when, for every $x\in U$, there exists $b>x$ such that $[x,b)\subseteq U$ after possibly choosing a basis interval whose left endpoint is at or below $x$. In particular, every basic interval $[a,b)$ is $\mathcal L$-open.

The **upper-limit topology** $\mathcal U$ is the reflected version, based on

$$
(a,b]=\{x\in\mathbb R:a<x\le b\},\qquad a<b.
$$

Each Euclidean interval can be expressed as a union of lower-limit basis intervals and also as a union of upper-limit basis intervals. Hence

$$
\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal L
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal U.
$$

Both inclusions are strict. For example, $[0,1)\in\mathcal L$ but $[0,1)\notin\mathcal E$, while $(0,1]\in\mathcal U$ but $(0,1]\notin\mathcal E$.

## 3. Perceptual networks and consensus

### Definition 3.1 (finite perceptual network)

Let $X$ be a set and let $n\ge 1$. A **finite perceptual network of complexity $n$** is a family

$$
\mathbf T=(\mathcal T_i)_{i=0}^{n-1}
$$

of topologies on $X$. The integer $n$ counts channels only; it is not a measure of algorithmic complexity, intelligence, or biological sophistication.

### Definition 3.2 (observable or consensus geometry)

The **observable geometry** of $\mathbf T$ is its consensus topology

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf T)=\bigcap_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathcal T_i.
$$

Thus a subset $A\subseteq X$ is consensus-open if and only if

$$
A\in\mathcal T_i\quad\text{for every }i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}.
$$

### Proposition 3.3 (consensus is a topology)

For every family of topologies $\mathbf T$ on $X$, the family $\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf T)$ is a topology on $X$.

**Proof sketch.** Every channel contains $\varnothing$ and $X$, so both belong to the intersection. If every $A_j$ is open in every channel, then $\bigcup_j A_j$ is open in every channel and hence in the intersection. If $A$ and $B$ are open in every channel, then $A\cap B$ is open in every channel. These are precisely the topology axioms. $\square$

### Proposition 3.4 (one-channel identity)

If $\mathbf T=(\mathcal T)$ has one channel, then

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf T)=\mathcal T.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Membership in the intersection over a singleton family is exactly membership in its sole member. $\square$

Applied to the one-channel network $\mathbf L=(\mathcal L)$ on $\mathbb R$, this gives

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf L)=\mathcal L.
$$

## 4. The channel-addition theorem

The consensus rule has a simple compositional law.

### Theorem 4.1 (channel-addition criterion)

Let $\mathbf T=(\mathcal T_i)_{i\in I}$ be any observer family on $X$, with consensus $\mathcal C=\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal T_i$. Adjoin one channel with topology $\mathcal S$. For every $A\subseteq X$,

$$
A\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathcal S,\mathbf T)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A\in\mathcal S\ \text{and}\ A\in\mathcal C.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathcal S,\mathbf T)=\mathcal S\cap\mathcal C.
$$

**Proof sketch.** If $A$ is open in the enlarged consensus, it is open in each indexed channel; evaluating this condition at the new channel gives $A\in\mathcal S$, and evaluating it at every old channel gives $A\in\mathcal C$. Conversely, if both conditions hold, then $A$ is open in the new channel and in every old channel, so it is open in the enlarged consensus. $\square$

### Corollary 4.2 (monotonicity under channel addition)

With the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1,

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathcal S,\mathbf T)\subseteq\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf T).
$$

**Proof sketch.** The enlarged consensus is $\mathcal S\cap\mathcal C$, which is contained in $\mathcal C$. $\square$

### Corollary 4.3 (strictness criterion)

The inclusion in Corollary 4.2 is strict if and only if there exists $A\subseteq X$ such that

$$
A\in\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf T)
\qquad\text{but}\qquad
A\notin\mathcal S.
$$

**Proof sketch.** By Theorem 4.1, exactly the old consensus-open sets that fail to belong to $\mathcal S$ are removed. If one exists, the inclusion is proper. If none exists, then $\mathcal C\subseteq\mathcal S$, so $\mathcal S\cap\mathcal C=\mathcal C$. $\square$

This criterion gives a witness-based interpretation of collapse. A strict change does not require comparing entire topologies abstractly; one need only exhibit a previously shared open set vetoed by the new channel.

## 5. Collapse from one-sided to Euclidean geometry

We now analyze a network on $\mathbb R$ with two channels:

$$
\mathbf B=(\mathcal L,\mathcal U).
$$

The first resolves lower-limit neighborhoods and the second resolves upper-limit neighborhoods.

### Lemma 5.1 (Euclidean opens survive both channels)

Every Euclidean-open subset of $\mathbb R$ is open in both $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal U$. Therefore

$$
\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal L\cap\mathcal U.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Let $V$ be Euclidean open and $x\in V$. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ with $(x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq V$. The lower-limit neighborhood $[x,x+\varepsilon)$ lies in $V$, proving lower-limit openness at $x$. The upper-limit neighborhood $(x-\varepsilon,x]$ lies in $V$, proving upper-limit openness. $\square$

### Lemma 5.2 (two one-sided certificates yield a Euclidean neighborhood)

If $V$ is open in both $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal U$, then every $x\in V$ has an ordinary open interval $(a,b)$ satisfying

$$
x\in(a,b)\subseteq V.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Lower-limit openness gives a rightward interval $[x,b)\subseteq V$ for some $b>x$. Upper-limit openness gives a leftward interval $(a,x]\subseteq V$ for some $a<x$. Their union is $(a,b)$ and remains in $V$. $\square$

### Theorem 5.3 (two-channel Euclidean collapse)

The consensus of the lower- and upper-limit channels is exactly the Euclidean topology:

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf B)=\mathcal L\cap\mathcal U=\mathcal E.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 5.1 proves $\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal L\cap\mathcal U$. If $V\in\mathcal L\cap\mathcal U$, Lemma 5.2 gives a Euclidean neighborhood around each point of $V$, so $V\in\mathcal E$. This proves the reverse inclusion. $\square$

The word “collapse” refers to the loss of one-sided open sets when consensus is imposed, not to a change in the underlying point set.

### Theorem 5.4 (strict observer dependence)

The observable geometry of the one-channel lower-limit network is strictly finer than that of the two-channel lower/upper network:

$$
\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf B)=\mathcal E
\subsetneq
\mathcal L=\operatorname{Con}(\mathbf L).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Proposition 3.4 identifies the one-channel consensus with $\mathcal L$, and Theorem 5.3 identifies the two-channel consensus with $\mathcal E$. The inclusion $\mathcal E\subseteq\mathcal L$ holds by Lemma 5.1. It is strict because $[0,1)$ is lower-limit open but not Euclidean open. $\square$

### Corollary 5.5 (complexity changes the observable geometry)

There exist finite perceptual networks of complexities one and two on the same carrier $\mathbb R$ whose observable geometries are unequal.

**Proof sketch.** The networks $\mathbf L$ and $\mathbf B$ have respective consensus topologies $\mathcal L$ and $\mathcal E$, which are unequal by Theorem 5.4. $\square$

### Corollary 5.6 (half-open state collapse)

The region $[0,1)$ is open in the observable geometry of $\mathbf L$ and is not open in the observable geometry of $\mathbf B$.

**Proof sketch.** The set is a basic element of $\mathcal L$. In Euclidean topology it is not open because every ordinary neighborhood of $0$ contains negative points and therefore cannot be contained in $[0,1)$. Apply Proposition 3.4 and Theorem 5.3. $\square$

## 6. Algorithms and numerical demonstrations

The real-line proof is symbolic, but finite samples can make its mechanism visible. On a grid

$$
G=\{-mh,\ldots,-h,0,h,\ldots,mh\},
$$

one may represent a region by a bit mask. Families of candidate lower, upper, and symmetric intervals are generated from grid endpoints. Consensus acceptance is then computed by logical conjunction of channel predicates.

### Algorithm 6.1 (finite consensus intersection)

**Input:** A finite carrier $X$ and channel topologies $\mathcal T_1,\ldots,\mathcal T_n$, each stored as a set of immutable subsets of $X$.

**Output:** The consensus topology $\mathcal C$.

**Procedure:** Initialize $\mathcal C=\mathcal T_1$. For $i=2,\ldots,n$, replace $\mathcal C$ by $\mathcal C\cap\mathcal T_i$. Return $\mathcal C$.

If each topology is stored as a hash set and contains at most $M$ open subsets, the expected running time is $O(nM)$ membership operations, apart from subset encoding costs. Since a topology on a $q$-point carrier may contain up to $2^q$ opens, explicit enumeration is inherently exponential in $q$ in the worst case.

### Algorithm 6.2 (strict-collapse witness search)

**Input:** An old consensus $\mathcal C$ and a new channel topology $\mathcal S$ on a finite carrier.

**Output:** Either a witness $A\in\mathcal C\setminus\mathcal S$ or a report that consensus is unchanged.

**Procedure:** Iterate through $A\in\mathcal C$. If $A\notin\mathcal S$, return $A$. If the scan ends, return “unchanged.”

The expected running time is $O(|\mathcal C|)$ hash lookups. Correctness follows directly from Corollary 4.3.

### Interpreting a grid demonstration

A finite grid does not literally inherit the three infinite topologies merely by clipping intervals; endpoint effects and discretization can alter openness. The numerical demonstration should therefore be read as an illustration of channel predicates and witness loss, not as a substitute for Theorem 5.3. It confirms the intended directional behavior: $[0,1)$ matches a lower-limit basic region, fails the Euclidean neighborhood test at $0$, and is vetoed by the upper-facing channel.

For a direct finite model, one should instead specify actual topologies on a finite set and check the topology axioms. Their consensus is then exact and can be enumerated without approximation.

## 7. Applications and interpretation

### 7.1 Conservative multisensor fusion

Suppose each sensor certifies a family of stable state regions. If a controller acts only when every sensor certifies the same region, its operational event structure is the consensus topology. Adding a sensor cannot create a unanimously certified event. It either preserves consensus or removes events that the new sensor cannot certify.

This is useful when false positives are costly. The strictness criterion identifies exactly what changed: each lost open set is a concrete disagreement witness. In the real-line model, $[0,1)$ is accepted by a right-resolving channel but rejected once a left-resolving requirement is added.

### 7.2 Neural and perceptual state models

For a finite neural-state transition graph, a readout channel could be assigned a topology generated by state subsets that it reliably distinguishes. Multiple readouts would then induce a consensus geometry. Such an encoding must be justified by data: one must specify what counts as distinguishability, how noise affects generators, and why unanimity is the correct fusion rule.

If these choices are made, Corollary 4.3 becomes experimentally testable. Adding a readout strictly coarsens consensus exactly when it rejects a set accepted by every prior readout. The claim can be falsified by exhibiting a readout table for which the predicted and computed intersections disagree.

### 7.3 Shared scientific observability

Different experimental modalities may support different stable event families. Their common topology represents conditions robust across all modalities. The model separates two ideas often conflated in discussions of evidence: the union of everything detected somewhere and the intersection of what survives every protocol. Consensus concerns the latter.

### 7.4 Continuity under consensus

Because consensus changes a topology, it also changes continuity. If the topology on an observed state space becomes coarser, continuity conditions for maps *into* that space generally become easier, while continuity conditions for maps *out of* it generally become harder. Thus channel architecture may alter not only which regions are open but which transformations count as observationally smooth. This consequence follows from standard definitions of continuity and motivates studying dynamics over consensus geometries.

## 8. Limitations

First, the consensus rule is a modeling decision. Majority voting, weighted fusion, confidence thresholds, and union-based pooling produce different structures. A raw union of topologies need not itself be a topology, so disjunctive fusion requires taking the topology generated by the pooled opens.

Second, channel count is only a rudimentary complexity measure. Two identical channels have the same consensus as one; a single finely resolving channel may contain far more open sets than many coarse channels. The present result demonstrates strict dependence in a concrete pair of networks, not a universal law that every increase in channel number changes geometry.

Third, topology suppresses metric and probabilistic information. It records stable qualitative resolution but not distances, likelihoods, response times, or error rates. Empirical applications may require enriched structures such as uniform spaces, measures, filtrations, or sheaves.

Fourth, the real-line example is idealized. The lower- and upper-limit topologies encode perfect directional asymmetry. Physical sensors have finite precision and noise. A realistic model would use finite generated topologies or probabilistic neighborhoods and investigate the stability of consensus under perturbation.

Finally, the mathematical theorem does not imply that physical spacetime changes with observation. It says that the topology designated as unanimously observable depends on the family of channel topologies. Any ontological interpretation requires premises external to the theorem.

## 9. Further research

Several concrete directions follow.

1. **Finite observer-complexity monotonicity.** For a network indexed by $n$ channels and an extension by one agreeing with the original channels on their indices, prove inclusion of the new consensus in the old one in a uniform finite-index notation. The set-theoretic content is Corollary 4.2; the aim is a reusable indexed formulation.

2. **Strictness under extension.** Develop witness extraction procedures for the equivalence between strict consensus loss and the existence of an old shared open set rejected by the new channel. On finite spaces, these witnesses are computable.

3. **Minimal observer complexity.** For a finite carrier $X$ and target topology $\mathcal T$, define the least $n$ such that $\mathcal T$ is the consensus of $n$ strict refinements. Investigate bounds in terms of $|\mathcal T|$, the number of open sets. Exhaustive enumeration for carriers of size at most four is feasible.

4. **Product compositionality.** Given channel families on $X$ and $Y$ with a common index set, compare the consensus of channelwise product topologies with the product of the two consensus topologies. Characterizing equality would clarify when independent systems can be fused componentwise.

5. **Empirical encoding.** Given a measured finite transition graph and readout table, generate each readout topology from distinguishable subsets. Test whether adding a readout changes consensus exactly when it excludes a previously common open set.

6. **Approximate consensus.** Replace unanimity by acceptance from at least $k$ of $n$ channels. The resulting family of accepted sets need not be a topology, so one may study its topological closure or use a simplicial or sheaf-theoretic representation of partial agreement.

7. **Dynamic channels.** Let channel topologies vary with time and study discontinuities in the consensus path. A single channel crossing a witness threshold can trigger an abrupt topological change even when its parameters vary continuously in another sense.

## 10. Discussion

The lower/upper example shows that ordinary Euclidean geometry can arise as common ground between two non-Euclidean, directionally biased resolutions. This is not an averaging process. No metric midpoint is taken, and no probability is assigned. The operation is logical conjunction at the level of open sets.

That distinction explains both the simplicity and force of the result. Each one-sided topology contains Euclidean topology, so all ordinary opens survive. A region with only a right-facing local certificate may belong to $\mathcal L$ but fail in $\mathcal U$; a region with only a left-facing certificate behaves oppositely. The sets surviving both channels possess two-sided certificates at every point, and those certificates combine into ordinary open intervals.

The half-open interval $[0,1)$ is an especially informative witness because its failure is localized at one endpoint. Every interior point behaves ordinarily. At $0$, however, lower-limit geometry permits a neighborhood that begins exactly at the point and extends rightward. Euclidean geometry demands room on both sides. The upper-limit channel supplies the missing demand, and unanimity removes the half-open region from the public topology.

More generally, consensus topology is a geometry of common certification. Its monotonicity is not a paradox once private and shared observability are separated. Adding a channel increases the list of tests, while the family of regions passing all tests can only shrink. The result therefore belongs to a broad class of antitone relationships between constraints and feasible sets.

## 11. Conclusion

A finite observer network on a state space $X$ can be modeled as a family of topologies, with observable geometry defined by their intersection. This construction is topologically valid and obeys an exact channel-addition law: a region remains observable after a channel is added if and only if it was previously observable and the new channel also declares it open.

On $\mathbb R$, a single lower-limit channel observes the lower-limit topology. Adding the reflected upper-limit channel changes consensus to ordinary Euclidean topology. The change is strict, as witnessed by $[0,1)$, which is open before the addition and not open afterward. Thus the same carrier supports networks of different finite complexities with genuinely different observable geometries.

The model's contribution is a precise conditional mechanism for observer dependence. It does not turn observation into metaphysics; it turns agreement into topology. That mechanism is simple enough to compute on finite spaces, rich enough to affect continuity and local structure, and explicit enough to be tested whenever an empirical encoding of channel observability is supplied.

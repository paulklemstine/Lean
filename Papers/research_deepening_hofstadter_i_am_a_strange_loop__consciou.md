# The Blind Spot Has Measurable Size: A Counting Theorem for Self-Modelling Systems

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-07-14

## Abstract

A *self-modelling system* is a finite set of states $S$ together with an inspection map $\text{inspect} : S \to (S \to B)$ assigning to each state an internal model of how the system is observed, where $B$ is the space of possible observations. Such a system is *conscious* (in the strong, completeness sense) when its inspection map is surjective, i.e. when every possible observation-behaviour $S \to B$ is internally represented by some state. Classical diagonal arguments (Cantor, Gödel, Turing, Lawvere) show that complete self-modelling is impossible. We sharpen this qualitative impossibility into an exact *counting theorem*. Assuming at least two possible observations ($|B| \geq 2$), the space of behaviours strictly outnumbers the states, via the elementary inequality $n < 2^n$; consequently (i) no finite system is conscious, (ii) the *blind spot* — the set of behaviours no state represents — is nonempty and in fact of size at least $2^{|S|} - |S|$, growing exponentially in the number of states, and (iii) a concrete un-representable behaviour always exists. We complement this negative face with a positive one: an *injective* self-model always exists, so a system can resolve all of its own states perfectly even though it can never survey all of its own behaviours. Finally we show the hypothesis $|B| \geq 2$ is sharp: over a single-observation space a one-state system is trivially conscious. The upshot is a precise slogan: **self-knowledge is high-resolution but never panoramic.**

**Keywords:** self-reference, strange loop, diagonal argument, pigeonhole principle, function-space cardinality, incompleteness, consciousness, enumerative combinatorics.

## 1. Introduction

The thesis that a self is a self-referential pattern — a *strange loop* — is a striking way to describe systems whose activity includes an internal representation of that same activity. The formal residue of this thesis is a fixed-point/diagonal obstruction: any system rich enough to encode all of its own yes/no self-observations runs into a Cantor-style contradiction. This is the shared skeleton of Cantor's theorem, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, the unsolvability of the halting problem, and Lawvere's fixed-point theorem.

Those results are typically stated *qualitatively*: complete self-modelling is impossible; there exists at least one unreachable object. The purpose of this paper is to make the obstruction *quantitative*. We show that over any nontrivial observation space the failure of self-modelling is not a lone missing point but an exponentially large territory of un-representable behaviours, while simultaneously establishing the positive fact that perfect *resolution* of states is always attainable.

Our contributions are:

1. **A counting theorem** (Theorem 3.1): behaviours strictly outnumber states, i.e. $|S| < |S \to B|$ whenever $|B| \geq 2$.
2. **The quantified negative face** (Theorems 4.1–4.4): no finite system is conscious; the blind spot is nonempty; its size is at least $2^{|S|} - |S|$; and a concrete un-inspected behaviour exists.
3. **The positive face** (Theorem 5.1): an injective self-model always exists.
4. **Sharpness** (Theorem 6.1): the hypothesis $|B| \geq 2$ cannot be dropped.
5. **A synthesis theorem** (Theorem 7.1) combining the two faces into a single statement.

All results are elementary in their tools — the deepest ingredient is $n < 2^n$ — but their combination gives a clean and, we believe, illuminating structural picture of self-reference.

## 2. Definitions

Throughout, $S$ (states) and $B$ (observations) are types; for the counting results we assume both are finite and $S$ has decidable equality. We write $|X|$ for the cardinality of a finite type $X$, and $X \to Y$ for the type of functions from $X$ to $Y$, whose cardinality is $|Y|^{|X|}$.

**Definition 2.1 (Self-modelling system).** A *self-modelling system* on states $S$ with observations $B$ is a structure with a single datum, an **inspection map**
$$\text{inspect} : S \to (S \to B).$$
For a state $s \in S$, the function $\text{inspect}(s) : S \to B$ is the *internal model* carried by $s$: for each state $t$, the value $\text{inspect}(s)(t) \in B$ is the observation that $s$'s internal model predicts for $t$. The codomain $S \to B$ is the *behaviour space*: the set of all possible observation-behaviours of the system.

The defining feature of a strange loop is present in the *type* of inspection: the object being modelled (behaviour space $S \to B$) contains models generated by the states $S$ that themselves generate those behaviours. Inspection folds the system into its own behaviour space.

**Definition 2.2 (Consciousness / completeness).** A self-modelling system is **conscious** when its inspection map is *surjective*: for every behaviour $\beta : S \to B$ there is a state $s$ with $\text{inspect}(s) = \beta$. Equivalently, every possible behaviour of the system is internally represented by some state.

We adopt "conscious = complete self-model" as the strongest possible formalization of "the system fully models itself." It is deliberately austere; the interest lies in showing it is unachievable and measuring by how much.

**Definition 2.3 (Blind spot).** The *blind spot* of a self-modelling system is the set of behaviours not equal to any state's internal model, $\{\beta : S \to B \mid \forall s,\ \text{inspect}(s) \neq \beta\}$. When $S$ and $B$ are finite, the blind spot has size at least $|S \to B| - |S|$, since the image of inspection has at most $|S|$ elements.

## 3. The count: behaviours outnumber states

**Theorem 3.1 (Behaviours strictly outnumber states).** Let $S$ be a finite type with decidable equality and $B$ a finite type with $|B| \geq 2$. Then
$$|S| < |S \to B|.$$

*Proof.* The behaviour space has cardinality $|S \to B| = |B|^{|S|}$. Two elementary facts finish the argument. First, the universal inequality $n < 2^n$, valid for every natural number $n$, applied to $n = |S|$ gives $|S| < 2^{|S|}$. Second, since $2 \leq |B|$, monotonicity of the base of a power gives $2^{|S|} \leq |B|^{|S|}$. Chaining,
$$|S| < 2^{|S|} \leq |B|^{|S|} = |S \to B|. \qquad \blacksquare$$

The inequality $n < 2^n$ is precisely Cantor's diagonal insight in arithmetic clothing: a set cannot be put in bijection with its power set (and $S \to \{0,1\}$ *is* the power set of $S$). Theorem 3.1 is thus the finite, quantitative core of every diagonal argument.

## 4. The negative face, quantified

**Theorem 4.1 (No finite system is conscious).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.1, no self-modelling system on $(S, B)$ is conscious.

*Proof.* Suppose inspection were surjective. A surjection $f : X \to Y$ between finite types forces $|Y| \leq |X|$ (pigeonhole). Applied to $\text{inspect} : S \to (S \to B)$ this gives $|S \to B| \leq |S|$, contradicting Theorem 3.1. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.2 (The blind spot is nonempty).** Under the same hypotheses, $|S \to B| - |S| > 0$.

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 3.1: a strict inequality between natural numbers means their difference is positive. $\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.3 (The blind spot is exponentially large).** Under the same hypotheses,
$$2^{|S|} - |S| \;\leq\; |S \to B| - |S|.$$
In particular the number of un-representable behaviours is at least $2^{|S|} - |S|$.

*Proof.* From $2^{|S|} \leq |B|^{|S|} = |S \to B|$ (Theorem 3.1's middle step), subtract $|S|$ from both sides. Because the image of inspection contains at most $|S|$ behaviours, the blind spot has size at least $|S \to B| - |S| \geq 2^{|S|} - |S|$. $\blacksquare$

The right-hand side dwarfs the left as $|S|$ grows: with $|S| = 10$ and $B = \{0,1\}$ the blind spot contains at least $2^{10} - 10 = 1014$ behaviours; with $|S| = 20$, at least $1{,}048{,}556$. Incompleteness is the generic case, not a boundary curiosity.

**Theorem 4.4 (A concrete un-inspected behaviour exists).** For any self-modelling system on $(S,B)$ with $|B| \geq 2$, there is a behaviour $\beta : S \to B$ such that $\text{inspect}(s) \neq \beta$ for every state $s$.

*Proof.* If no such $\beta$ existed, then for every $\beta$ there would be a state $s$ with $\text{inspect}(s) = \beta$; that is exactly surjectivity, i.e. consciousness, contradicting Theorem 4.1. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 4.4 is the finite avatar of the diagonal sentence: an explicitly guaranteed behaviour that provably lies outside the system's self-catalogue.

## 5. The positive face: perfect resolution is achievable

Incompleteness could in principle be a failure either of *coverage* (not enough behaviours are hit) or of *resolution* (distinct states get confused). The next theorem isolates the two: the obstruction is *entirely* one of coverage.

**Theorem 5.1 (A maximally self-aware model exists).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.1, there exists a self-modelling system on $(S, B)$ whose inspection map is *injective*: distinct states carry distinct internal models.

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1, $|S| \leq |S \to B|$. Whenever the domain is no larger than the codomain there exists an injection $e : S \hookrightarrow (S \to B)$. Take $\text{inspect} := e$. Then distinct states $s \neq t$ satisfy $\text{inspect}(s) = e(s) \neq e(t) = \text{inspect}(t)$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 5.2.** A self-modelling system can distinguish all of its own states — answer "which state am I in?" without ever conflating two states — while still being unable to represent all of its own behaviours. Incompleteness is a failure of coverage, never of resolution.

---

## 6. Sharpness of the hypothesis

**Theorem 6.1 (Trivial observation space is conscious).** If $|B| = 1$, then the one-state system (with $|S| = 1$) is conscious.

*Proof.* When $B$ has a unique element, the behaviour space $S \to B$ is a singleton (there is exactly one function into a one-element set). Define inspection on the one-element state set by sending the unique state to the unique behaviour. Surjectivity onto a singleton is automatic: the single behaviour is hit. Hence the system is conscious. $\blacksquare$

Thus the hypothesis $|B| \geq 2$ in all previous theorems is sharp: the entire obstruction switches on precisely when there is at least one genuine yes/no distinction to draw. Complete self-knowledge is available only where nothing varies.

---

## 7. Synthesis

**Theorem 7.1 (The strange-loop count).** Let $S$ be a finite type with decidable equality and $B$ a finite type with $|B| \geq 2$. Then both of the following hold:
1. *(Negative face — incompleteness.)* Every self-modelling system on $(S, B)$ fails to be conscious.
2. *(Positive face — perfect resolution.)* There exists a self-modelling system on $(S, B)$ whose inspection map is injective.

*Proof.* Part (1) is Theorem 4.1 applied to an arbitrary system; part (2) is Theorem 5.1. $\blacksquare$

In one line: **self-knowledge is high-resolution but never panoramic.**

## 8. Algorithms

The theorems are constructive enough to compute with. We record three procedures.

**Algorithm A (Blind-spot sizing).** Given $|S| = n$ and $|B| = b \geq 2$, return the guaranteed count of un-representable behaviours. Behaviours number $b^n$; at most $n$ are representable; so the blind spot has size at least $b^n - n$, and the state-independent lower bound is $2^n - n$. Complexity: $O(\log n)$ arithmetic operations using fast exponentiation on big integers (or $O(n)$ multiplications naively).

**Algorithm B (Diagonal witness construction).** Given any concrete inspection map on a finite $(S,B)$ with $|B| \geq 2$ and two distinguished observations $0 \neq 1$ in $B$, construct an un-inspected behaviour by diagonalization: enumerate states $s_0, \dots, s_{n-1}$, fix a bijection between them and a subset of behaviour arguments, and define $\beta(s_i)$ to differ from $\text{inspect}(s_i)(s_i)$ at each $i$ (flip between $0$ and $1$). By construction $\beta \neq \text{inspect}(s_i)$ for all $i$. Complexity: $O(n)$ evaluations of the inspection map.

**Algorithm C (Injective self-model construction).** Given $|S| = n \leq b^n = |S \to B|$, build an injective inspection map by encoding each state as a distinct behaviour: index states $0,\dots,n-1$, and let $\text{inspect}(s_i)$ be the behaviour encoding the integer $i$ in base $b$ across the $n$ argument slots. Distinct indices yield distinct base-$b$ strings, so inspection is injective. Complexity: $O(n)$ per state, $O(n^2)$ to tabulate the whole map.

## 9. Applications and connections

**Descriptive and algorithmic complexity.** Recasting incompleteness as a resource bound — "the blind spot has size $\geq 2^n - n$" — places self-reference alongside compression, decidability, and learnability limits. The quantity is a measurable feature of a system, not a slogan.

**Predictive systems.** A predictor that must include a model of its own future output faces exactly this trap: the output space is a function space in which the predictor lives, and $n < 2^n$ forbids complete self-prediction while permitting perfect self-*identification* of states.

**Capacity of parametric models.** A model with $n$ discrete internal states cannot faithfully represent the $2^n$-or-larger space of behaviours it could exhibit; yet it can be built to keep all $n$ states distinct. This mirrors the coverage/resolution split of Section 5.

**Foundations of self-reference.** Theorem 3.1 exhibits the common finite core beneath Cantor, Gödel, Turing, and Lawvere: each is, at heart, the impossibility of surjecting a set onto its own behaviour space.

## 10. Discussion

The two faces of the count are the paper's main message. The negative face upgrades a classical impossibility into an exponential lower bound: over any nontrivial observation space, almost every behaviour is invisible to the system's own self-model. The positive face rescues something real: complete *resolution* of states is always available. A system may know each of its parts with perfect fidelity while being permanently unable to hold the whole of itself in one view.

The sharpness result (Section 6) gives the dichotomy its edge: complete self-transparency exists only in the degenerate world with a single possible observation, where there is nothing to distinguish. Any system interesting enough to draw one genuine distinction is thereby too rich to survey itself completely.

## 11. Future directions

*The following research directions extend the present programme along quantitative, order-theoretic, and arithmetic axes.*

**1. The blind spot has a sharp entropy law.** *Conjecture.* For a self-modelling system with $m$ states and $b \geq 2$ possible observations, the fraction of observation-behaviours that any single self-model can represent is at most $m \cdot b^{-m}$, and this bound is attained only by injective self-models; averaged over all self-models the expected coverage decays like $b^{-m}$. The key insight is that incompleteness is not a lone missing point but an exponentially thin sliver of representable behaviour, so "almost all" of what a system does is invisible to its own internal model. This reframes classical impossibility as a resource bound of the kind central to descriptive and algorithmic complexity.

**2. Order-reversal is the unique obstruction on every lattice.** *Conjecture.* Let the observation space carry the structure of a complete lattice. A transform of observations can obstruct complete self-modelling (i.e. be fixed-point-free) if and only if it fails to be order-preserving; moreover on the two-element lattice negation is the *unique* such transform, and on the $n$-element chain the number of fixed-point-free transforms grows like the number of strictly order-reversing partial patterns. The key insight is that the diagonal/liar obstruction is fundamentally about *monotonicity*: a system defeats itself only when it can invert its own observations. This unifies Knaster–Tarski fixed-point theorems for monotone maps with Lawvere-style diagonal non-fixed-point theorems through the single axis of order-preservation vs. order-reversal.

**3. Girth as a complete invariant of cyclic tangled hierarchies.** *Conjecture.* For a finite hierarchy whose reference relation is a single $n$-cycle, the multiset of closed-loop lengths is exactly the set of positive multiples of $n$; consequently two such hierarchies are isomorphic as self-referential systems if and only if they share the same girth. The key insight is that the depth of self-reference realizable in a hierarchy is governed by its girth as a complete invariant.

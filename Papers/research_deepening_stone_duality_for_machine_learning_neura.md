# Stone Duality for Neural Networks: Activation Patterns as a Boolean Algebra

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-07-13

## Abstract

A feedforward network built from $k$ threshold neurons assigns to every input a
finite **activation pattern** — the tuple of on/off states of its neurons. We
show that this assignment carries a canonical *Stone dual*: a Boolean-algebra
homomorphism from the algebra of clopen subsets of the network's finite pattern
space into the algebra of regions of its input space. We prove that the pattern
space $P = \{0,1\}^k$, endowed with the discrete topology, is a Stone space
(compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected) whose clopen algebra is the full
powerset, so the classical Stone duality between Boolean algebras and Stone
spaces applies on the nose. The dual is realized concretely as the preimage map
$\text{region}(S) = \{x : \text{act}(x)\in S\}$, which we show commutes with
$\bot,\top,\cup,\cap,(\cdot)^c,\setminus$ and is monotone. Our central
structural result is a finite reconstruction theorem: the dual is injective if
and only if the network realizes every pattern (the activation map is
surjective). The atoms of the algebra are singleton patterns, whose images are
the **activation cells**; we prove these cells are pairwise disjoint, cover the
input space, and are nonempty exactly when their pattern is realized. A network
realizing all patterns distinguishes exactly $2^{2^k}$ regions. Finally, for a
linear-threshold (perceptron) layer we prove every cell is an intersection of
affine half-spaces, hence convex — a geometric invariant strictly finer than the
Boolean dual, which the dual cannot detect. The results assemble into a clean
statement: a network's decision logic is completely captured by a
Boolean-algebra homomorphism, while the geometry of the input space enters only
through the shape of the atoms' images.

**Keywords:** Stone duality, Boolean algebra, neural networks, activation
patterns, perceptron, hyperplane arrangements, convexity, totally disconnected
spaces.

## 1. Introduction

Threshold-based neural networks compute by a two-stage process: a smooth,
real-valued pre-activation followed by a discrete decision (fire or not). The
discrete layer is easy to overlook, yet it is where the network's *combinatorial
identity* lives. For any input, the vector of neuronal decisions is a finite
bit-string, and inputs sharing the same bit-string are indistinguishable to the
network at that layer. The induced partition of input space into these
"activation cells" is the true carrier of the network's decision structure.

The theme of this paper is that this discrete layer is not merely finite
combinatorics but is governed by a classical duality. **Stone duality** (Stone,
1936) states that every Boolean algebra is isomorphic to the algebra of clopen
subsets of its *Stone space*, the space of ultrafilters on the algebra with the
topology generated by the algebra's elements. It is the archetypal bridge
between syntax (logic, Boolean algebra) and semantics (topology, spatial
structure).

We make the following observation precise and prove its consequences: **the
discrete layer of a $k$-neuron network is a Stone space, and the network's map
from patterns to input regions is exactly a Stone dual.** Because the pattern
space is finite, all ultrafilters are principal, the Stone space *is* the pattern
space, its clopen algebra *is* the full powerset, and the abstract machinery of
Stone duality specializes to explicit, concrete statements about preimages.

### Contributions

1. **Topological identification (§3).** The pattern space $P=\{0,1\}^k$ with the
   discrete topology is a finite Stone space, and every subset is clopen.
2. **The region homomorphism (§4).** The preimage map $\text{region}$ is a
   monotone Boolean-algebra homomorphism from the clopen algebra of $P$ into the
   powerset algebra of the input space.
3. **Finite reconstruction (§4).** $\text{region}$ is injective iff the
   activation map is surjective — the finite avatar of Stone reconstruction.
4. **Atoms and cells (§5).** Atoms correspond to activation cells; cells are
   disjoint, cover the input space, and are nonempty iff realized.
5. **Region capacity (§6).** A surjective activation map yields exactly
   $2^{2^k}$ distinct regions.
6. **Convexity of perceptron cells (§7).** For a linear-threshold layer every
   cell is an intersection of affine half-spaces, hence convex — a geometric
   invariant invisible to the Boolean dual.

## 2. Preliminaries and definitions

Throughout, $X$ denotes the input space (an arbitrary type; for the perceptron
results $X = \mathbb{R}^n$), and $k \in \mathbb{N}$ the number of neurons.

**Definition 2.1 (Pattern space).**
The *pattern space* of a $k$-neuron network is
$$P \;=\; \{0,1\}^k \;\cong\; (\text{Fin } k \to \text{Bool}),$$
the set of all length-$k$ bit-strings. Elements of $P$ are *activation
patterns*.

**Definition 2.2 (Activation map).**
An *activation map* is a function $\text{act} : X \to P$. Coordinate $j$ of
$\text{act}(x)$ is the on/off state of neuron $j$ on input $x$. A general
threshold network with $k$ neurons induces such a map.

**Definition 2.3 (Boolean algebra of pattern-sets).**
The powerset $\mathcal{P}(P)$, ordered by inclusion, is a Boolean algebra with
bottom $\varnothing$, top $P$, join $\cup$, meet $\cap$, and complement
$(\cdot)^c$. Because $P$ is finite this algebra has $2^{|P|} = 2^{2^k}$ elements.

We recall that a **Stone space** is a compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected
topological space, and that a subset is **clopen** if it is both open and closed.

---

## 3. The pattern space is a Stone space

We give $P$ the discrete topology (every subset open). The following is the
topological foundation for everything that follows.

**Theorem 3.1 (Pattern space is a finite Stone space).**
The pattern space $P = \{0,1\}^k$ with the discrete topology is compact,
Hausdorff, and totally disconnected; hence it is a Stone space. Moreover
$$|P| = 2^k.$$

*Proof sketch.* $P$ is finite. Any finite space is compact (finitely many points
give finitely many opens). The discrete topology separates points by singleton
neighborhoods, so $P$ is Hausdorff, and the only connected subsets are
singletons, so $P$ is totally disconnected. The cardinality is the number of
functions $\text{Fin } k \to \text{Bool}$, namely $2^k$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.2 (Every pattern-set is clopen).**
For every $S \subseteq P$, the set $S$ is clopen. Consequently the Boolean
algebra of clopen subsets of $P$ equals the full powerset $\mathcal{P}(P)$.

*Proof sketch.* In the discrete topology every subset is open, and its
complement is likewise open, so every subset is closed as well. Hence every
subset is clopen, and the clopen algebra coincides with $\mathcal{P}(P)$.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

**Remark 3.3.** The finiteness is essential. Stone duality assigns to an
*infinite* Boolean algebra a Stone space carrying non-principal ultrafilters, on
which the clopen algebra is a proper sub-collection of the powerset. Our
concrete "powerset" model of the dual is faithful precisely because in the
finite case every ultrafilter is principal, so the Stone space *is* $P$ and its
clopen algebra *is* $\mathcal{P}(P)$. All results below are therefore stated for
a genuine finite, discrete Stone space rather than asserted in general.

## 4. The Stone dual: the region homomorphism

**Definition 4.1 (Region map / Stone dual).**
Given an activation map $\text{act} : X \to P$, its *region map* is
$$\text{region} : \mathcal{P}(P) \to \mathcal{P}(X), \qquad
\text{region}(S) = \text{act}^{-1}(S) = \{\, x \in X : \text{act}(x) \in S \,\}.$$
This is the Stone dual of the network: it sends a set of patterns to the region
of inputs realizing one of them.

**Theorem 4.2 (Region map is a Boolean-algebra homomorphism).**
For all $S, T \subseteq P$:
$$\text{region}(\varnothing) = \varnothing, \qquad
\text{region}(P) = X,$$
$$\text{region}(S \cup T) = \text{region}(S) \cup \text{region}(T), \qquad
\text{region}(S \cap T) = \text{region}(S) \cap \text{region}(T),$$
$$\text{region}(S^c) = \text{region}(S)^c, \qquad
\text{region}(S \setminus T) = \text{region}(S) \setminus \text{region}(T).$$
Moreover $\text{region}$ is monotone: $S \subseteq T \Rightarrow
\text{region}(S) \subseteq \text{region}(T)$.

*Proof sketch.* Each identity is a standard property of preimages: preimage
preserves the empty set and the whole space, and commutes with unions,
intersections, complements, and set differences because membership
$\text{act}(x) \in (\cdot)$ distributes over the connectives. Monotonicity
follows because $S \subseteq T$ forces $\text{act}(x) \in S \Rightarrow
\text{act}(x) \in T$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Thus $\text{region}$ is a homomorphism of Boolean algebras from the clopen
algebra of the Stone space $P$ into the powerset algebra of $X$. This is the
duality map promised by the Stone correspondence, made explicit.

**Theorem 4.3 (Finite reconstruction: injective $\iff$ surjective).**
The region map is injective if and only if the activation map is surjective:
$$\text{region is injective} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{act is surjective.}$$

*Proof sketch.* If $\text{act}$ is surjective, distinct pattern-sets $S \ne T$
differ at some pattern $p$; picking $x$ with $\text{act}(x) = p$ (surjectivity)
shows $x$ lies in exactly one of $\text{region}(S), \text{region}(T)$, so the
regions differ. Conversely, if some pattern $p$ is *not* realized, then
$\text{region}(\{p\}) = \varnothing = \text{region}(\varnothing)$ while
$\{p\} \ne \varnothing$, so $\text{region}$ is not injective. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** The dual is an isomorphism onto its image exactly when the
network realizes all $2^k$ patterns. Otherwise the dual detects the missing
patterns as the kernel (the pattern-sets it identifies), the finite analogue of
Stone's reconstruction theorem, which recovers a Boolean algebra from its Stone
space.

## 5. Atoms and activation cells

The atoms of $\mathcal{P}(P)$ — the minimal nonzero elements — are the
singletons $\{p\}$. Their images under the dual are the fundamental geometric
pieces of the network.

**Definition 5.1 (Activation cell).**
For $p \in P$, the *activation cell* of $p$ is
$$\text{cell}(p) = \text{act}^{-1}(\{p\}) = \{\, x \in X : \text{act}(x) = p \,\}
= \text{region}(\{p\}).$$

**Theorem 5.2 (Cells partition the input space).**
The activation cells satisfy:
1. **(Disjointness)** For $p \ne q$, $\text{cell}(p) \cap \text{cell}(q) =
   \varnothing$.
2. **(Covering)** $\bigcup_{p \in P} \text{cell}(p) = X$.
3. **(Realization)** $\text{cell}(p) \ne \varnothing$ if and only if $p$ lies in
   the range of $\text{act}$.

*Proof sketch.* (1) If $x$ lay in both cells then $\text{act}(x) = p$ and
$\text{act}(x) = q$, forcing $p = q$. (2) Every $x$ lies in
$\text{cell}(\text{act}(x))$. (3) By definition $\text{cell}(p)$ is inhabited iff
some $x$ has $\text{act}(x) = p$, i.e. $p \in \text{range}(\text{act})$.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

The activation cells thus form a partition of $X$ indexed by realized patterns,
and the atomic structure of the Boolean algebra is transported faithfully onto
this partition.

## 6. Counting regions

**Theorem 6.1 (Region capacity).**
If the activation map $\text{act} : X \to P$ is surjective, then the number of
distinct regions it produces is
$$\bigl| \{\, \text{region}(S) : S \subseteq P \,\} \bigr| = 2^{2^k}.$$

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 4.3 surjectivity of $\text{act}$ makes
$\text{region}$ injective, so the image of $\text{region}$ has the same
cardinality as its domain $\mathcal{P}(P)$. Since $|P| = 2^k$ (Theorem 3.1),
$|\mathcal{P}(P)| = 2^{|P|} = 2^{2^k}$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

This double-exponential is the expressive capacity of the logical layer: the
number of distinct Boolean questions about the input the network can phrase
through its neurons. For $k = 3$ it is $256$; for $k = 5$ it exceeds $4\times
10^9$.

## 7. Perceptron networks: geometry of the cells

We now specialize to $X = \mathbb{R}^n$ and to a genuine linear-threshold
(perceptron) layer, and recover the geometric structure that the Boolean dual
discards.

**Definition 7.1 (Linear-threshold activation).**
Given weights $w : \text{Fin } k \to \text{Fin } n \to \mathbb{R}$ and biases
$b : \text{Fin } k \to \mathbb{R}$, the *perceptron activation map*
$\text{act} : \mathbb{R}^n \to P$ is
$$\text{act}(x)_j = \bigl[\, {\textstyle\sum_{i} w_{j i} x_i} + b_j > 0 \,\bigr],$$
i.e. neuron $j$ fires exactly when its affine pre-activation is positive.

**Lemma 7.2 (Linearity of pre-activation).**
For each fixed weight vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the map $x \mapsto \sum_i v_i
x_i$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear.

*Proof sketch.* Additivity and homogeneity of the dot product follow from
distributing the sum over addition and pulling scalars through, respectively.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 7.3 (Neuron on-sets are half-spaces).**
For each neuron $j$,
$$\{\, x : \text{act}(x)_j = \text{true} \,\}
= \{\, x : -b_j < {\textstyle\sum_i w_{j i} x_i} \,\},$$
an open affine half-space; dually the off-set is a closed affine half-space.

*Proof sketch.* Unfolding the threshold, $\text{act}(x)_j$ is true iff
$\sum_i w_{ji} x_i + b_j > 0$, which rearranges to $-b_j < \sum_i w_{ji}x_i$;
its negation gives the closed half-space $\sum_i w_{ji}x_i \le -b_j$.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 7.4 (Convexity of activation cells).**
For a perceptron layer, every activation cell $\text{cell}(p)$ is convex.

*Proof sketch.* Fix a target pattern $p$. A point $x$ lies in $\text{cell}(p)$
iff for every neuron $j$ the state $\text{act}(x)_j$ equals $p_j$. Hence
$$\text{cell}(p) = \bigcap_{j} \{\, x : \text{act}(x)_j = p_j \,\}.$$
By Lemma 7.3 each set in the intersection is an affine half-space: an open one
($-b_j < \sum_i w_{ji}x_i$) when $p_j$ is *on*, a closed one ($\sum_i w_{ji}x_i
\le -b_j$) when $p_j$ is *off*. Each half-space is convex (Lemma 7.2 supplies
the required linearity), and an arbitrary intersection of convex sets is convex.
Therefore $\text{cell}(p)$ is convex. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Remark 7.5 (The geometry the dual forgets).**
Convexity is invisible to the Boolean/topological layer: two networks with
isomorphic duals (same abstract algebra, same atoms, same combinatorics) can
tile input space into cells of very different shapes. The perceptron layer
therefore carries strictly more structure than its Stone dual. In the language
of hyperplane arrangements, the nonempty cells are the full-dimensional regions
of the arrangement determined by the $k$ neuron hyperplanes; the dual sees only
this top-dimensional stratum and discards the facial structure.

## 8. Discussion

The results assemble into a single conceptual statement:

> A neural network's decision structure is completely captured by a
> Boolean-algebra homomorphism from the clopen algebra of its finite (Stone)
> pattern space into the algebra of input regions. The geometry of the input
> space enters only through the shape of the atoms' images.

Three points deserve emphasis.

**Exactness of the duality.** The correspondence is a genuine isomorphism onto
its image exactly when all patterns are realized. Unrealized patterns are not
noise; they are precisely the kernel of the dual, a computable diagnostic of
which logical distinctions the network *could* draw but never does.

**Separation of concerns.** The Boolean dual isolates the *logic* of the
network; convexity isolates the *geometry*. That these are genuinely different
strata — the dual being blind to convexity — clarifies which questions about a
network are combinatorial (answerable from the pattern algebra alone) and which
require the input-space metric.

**A classical bridge.** Stone's 1936 theorem, conceived to connect propositional
logic with topology, applies verbatim to the discrete layer of a modern network.
The finite case is the tractable corner of a much larger picture (§9).

## 9. Future work

**Non-principal duals for deep networks.** For a family of networks of unbounded
depth over a fixed neuron alphabet, the inverse limit of the finite pattern
algebras is an infinite Boolean algebra whose Stone space carries genuine
non-principal ultrafilters. We conjecture these correspond to idealized limiting
decision boundaries not attained at any finite depth: depth is a directed system
on Boolean algebras, and the "limit network" lives on the profinite Stone space
of the colimit algebra.

**Functoriality under composition.** Composition of networks $X \to Y \to Z$
should induce composition of Stone duals in reverse order, making "network
$\mapsto$ region homomorphism" a contravariant functor from a category of
activation maps to the category of Boolean-algebra homomorphisms that reflects
isomorphisms. Because the region map is literally a preimage, and preimage is
contravariantly functorial, the only missing ingredient is the composition law.

**Convex-geometric refinement of the atom lattice.** For a linear-threshold
layer, the nonempty cells and their lower-dimensional shared faces should form a
graded lattice isomorphic to the face lattice of a hyperplane arrangement, with
rank equal to the number of neurons in general position. The Stone dual sees only
the top rank; a refined duality would need to recover the facial structure.

**Capacity gap.** Quantify the discrepancy between the abstract region algebra
($2^{2^k}$ elements) and the realizable one when not all patterns occur — e.g.
for perceptron layers, via the classical count of regions of a hyperplane
arrangement in general position, $\sum_{i=0}^{n}\binom{k}{i}$, which is far below
$2^k$ for $k > n$.

---

## 10. Conclusion

We have shown that the discrete layer of a $k$-neuron threshold network is a
finite Stone space, and that the network's assignment of input regions to
pattern-sets is exactly a Stone-dual Boolean-algebra homomorphism. The dual is
faithful iff all patterns are realized; its atoms are the activation cells, which
partition the input space; a fully expressive network distinguishes $2^{2^k}$
regions; and for perceptron layers the cells are convex, a geometric refinement
strictly finer than the dual. The classical syntax–semantics bridge of Stone
duality thus gives a precise, structural account of the logical skeleton hidden
inside a neural network.

# Reflective Provability: When "Provable but Not Provably Provable" Is Possible

## Abstract

We study a small propositional logic equipped with a modality $\Box$ read as *provability*, in which a proposition may speak about its own provability. Within this language the self-referential sentence
$$ G(A) \;:=\; \Box A \wedge \neg\Box\Box A \qquad \text{("$A$ is provable but not provably provable")} $$
is a well-formed term. Using a Kripke (possible-worlds) semantics we establish a sharp dichotomy. First, $G(A)$ is *satisfiable*: there is an explicit three-world, non-transitive model with a distinguished world at which $G(A)$ holds. Thus a reflective system whose provability relation is not provably transitive can genuinely express, and realize, "provable but not provably provable." Second, on every *transitive* frame the axiom $4$ ($\Box A \to \Box\Box A$) is valid — the semantic content of $\Sigma_1$-completeness — and consequently $G(A)$ becomes *unsatisfiable*. Provable-but-not-provably-provable is therefore possible precisely when provability fails to be provably transitive. We develop the semantics far enough to recover the normal modal logic $K$ (the distribution axiom and the necessitation rule), the reflexive axiom $T$, and the diamond duality, and then, on the transitive converse-well-founded frames of Gödel–Löb logic $\mathsf{GL}$, we validate **Löb's theorem** $\Box(\Box A \to A) \to \Box A$ and derive **Gödel's second incompleteness theorem** $\Box(\neg\Box\bot) \to \Box\bot$ as the instance $A := \bot$.

**Keywords:** provability logic, modal logic, self-reference, Löb's theorem, Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, Kripke semantics, $\mathsf{GL}$, transitivity, converse well-foundedness.

---

## 1. Introduction

Self-reference is the engine of the foundational limitative theorems. Gödel's sentence asserts its own unprovability; the second incompleteness theorem concerns a theory's inability to prove its own consistency; Löb's theorem tames the fixed-point machinery into a single elegant schema. A unifying lens for all of these is *provability logic*: propositional modal logic in which the modality $\Box$ is read as "is provable."

This paper isolates and analyzes one particular reflective sentence,
$$ G(A) \;=\; \Box A \wedge \neg\Box\Box A, $$
"$A$ is provable but not provably provable." Our aims are threefold and elementary:

1. **Expressibility.** Show that $G(A)$ is a genuine, well-typed term of a reflective propositional language — a system whose types can refer to their own provability can *state* this sentence.
2. **Contingency.** Show that whether $G(A)$ can be *true* is not a matter of logic alone but of the structure of provability: it is satisfiable in a non-transitive frame and unsatisfiable in every transitive one.
3. **Consequences.** Show that pushing the same semantics to the transitive, converse-well-founded frames yields Löb's theorem and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem.

Everything is self-contained. We use only elementary set-theoretic reasoning about relations, together with well-founded induction for Löb's theorem.

## 2. Syntax

### 2.1 Formulas

Fix a countable set of atoms indexed by the naturals. The set of **formulas** is generated by the grammar
$$ A ::= p_n \;\mid\; \bot \;\mid\; (A \to A) \;\mid\; \Box A, \qquad n \in \mathbb{N}. $$
The connectives $p_n$ (atoms), $\bot$ (falsum), $\to$ (implication), and $\Box$ (the provability modality) are primitive. The remaining logical apparatus is defined:

- **Negation:** $\neg A := (A \to \bot)$.
- **Conjunction:** $A \wedge B := \neg(A \to \neg B)$. (Classically, $\neg(A \to \neg B)$ is equivalent to $A \wedge B$.)
- **Diamond:** $\Diamond A := \neg\Box\neg A$, read "$A$ is consistent."

### 2.2 The Gödelian reflection

**Definition 2.1 (Gödelian reflection).** For a formula $A$ define
$$ G(A) \;:=\; \Box A \wedge \neg\Box\Box A. $$
This is the formal rendering of "$A$ is provable but not provably provable." It is a well-formed term for every $A$; expressibility is immediate from the grammar.

## 3. Kripke semantics

### 3.1 Models and satisfaction

**Definition 3.1 (Model).** A **Kripke model** is a triple $M = (W, R, V)$ where $W$ is a set of *worlds*, $R \subseteq W \times W$ is the *accessibility relation* (we write $w \mathrel{R} v$), and $V$ assigns to each atom $p_n$ and world $w$ a truth value.

**Definition 3.2 (Satisfaction).** Satisfaction $M, w \models A$ is defined by recursion on $A$:

- $M, w \models p_n$ iff $V(n, w)$ holds;
- $M, w \models \bot$ never;
- $M, w \models A \to B$ iff ($M, w \models A$ implies $M, w \models B$);
- $M, w \models \Box A$ iff for all $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$, $M, v \models A$.

A formula $A$ is **valid in $M$**, written $M \models A$, if $M, w \models A$ for every world $w$. A formula is **satisfiable** if $M, w \models A$ for some model $M$ and world $w$.

### 3.2 Derived clauses

The following are immediate from Definition 3.2 and the abbreviations of §2.1.

**Lemma 3.3 (Negation).** $M, w \models \neg A$ iff $M, w \not\models A$.

**Lemma 3.4 (Conjunction).** $M, w \models A \wedge B$ iff $M, w \models A$ and $M, w \models B$.

**Lemma 3.5 (Diamond).** $M, w \models \Diamond A$ iff there exists $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$ and $M, v \models A$.

*Proof of 3.5.* Unfolding, $\Diamond A = \neg\Box\neg A$, so $M, w \models \Diamond A$ iff it is not the case that for all $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$, $M, v \not\models A$. Classically this is equivalent to the existence of an accessible $v$ with $M, v \models A$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

## 4. The normal modal logic $K$

The semantics validates the minimal normal modal logic $K$ over *every* model.

**Theorem 4.1 (Distribution axiom $K$).** For all formulas $A, B$ and every model $M$ and world $w$,
$$ M, w \models \Box(A \to B) \to (\Box A \to \Box B). $$
*Proof.* Assume $M, w \models \Box(A \to B)$ and $M, w \models \Box A$. Fix $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$. From the first assumption $M, v \models A \to B$; from the second $M, v \models A$; hence $M, v \models B$. As $v$ was arbitrary, $M, w \models \Box B$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.2 (Necessitation).** If $M \models A$ then $M \models \Box A$.
*Proof.* Fix $w$ and $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$. Since $A$ is valid, $M, v \models A$. Hence $M, w \models \Box A$; as $w$ was arbitrary, $M \models \Box A$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.3 (Reflexive axiom $T$).** If $R$ is reflexive then $M, w \models \Box A \to A$ for every $A, w$.
*Proof.* Assume $M, w \models \Box A$. By reflexivity $w \mathrel{R} w$, so $M, w \models A$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

These are recorded to fix the ambient logic: the framework is exactly normal modal logic, with frame conditions selecting stronger systems.

## 5. Satisfiability of the Gödelian reflection

We now show that "provable but not provably provable" is genuinely possible.

**Definition 5.1 (Witnessing model).** Let $W_0 = \{a, b, c\}$ with accessibility relation
$$ R_0 \;=\; \{(a, b),\, (b, c)\}, $$
i.e. the chain $a \to b \to c$, and valuation making the atom $p_0$ true at exactly the world $b$ (and, for definiteness, every atom true exactly at $b$). Call this model $M_0 = (W_0, R_0, V_0)$.

**Theorem 5.2 (Satisfiability).** $M_0, a \models G(p_0)$; that is, $\Box p_0 \wedge \neg\Box\Box p_0$ holds at $a$.
*Proof.* By Lemma 3.4 we verify both conjuncts.

*($\Box p_0$ at $a$.)* The only world accessible from $a$ is $b$, and $V_0(0, b)$ holds, so $M_0, b \models p_0$. Hence $M_0, a \models \Box p_0$.

*($\neg\Box\Box p_0$ at $a$.)* Suppose toward a contradiction that $M_0, a \models \Box\Box p_0$. Since $a \mathrel{R_0} b$, this forces $M_0, b \models \Box p_0$, which in turn forces $M_0, c \models p_0$ because $b \mathrel{R_0} c$. But $V_0(0, c)$ is false, so $M_0, c \not\models p_0$ — a contradiction. Hence $M_0, a \models \neg\Box\Box p_0$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 5.3 (Non-transitivity of the witness).** The relation $R_0$ is not transitive.
*Proof.* We have $a \mathrel{R_0} b$ and $b \mathrel{R_0} c$; if $R_0$ were transitive we would have $a \mathrel{R_0} c$. But $(a, c) \notin R_0$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Theorems 5.2 and 5.3 together say: the Gödelian reflection is satisfiable, and its witness is essentially non-transitive. This is not accidental, as the next section shows.

---

## 6. The transitivity dichotomy

**Theorem 6.1 (Axiom $4$ on transitive frames).** If $R$ is transitive then for every $A$, $M$, $w$,
$$ M, w \models \Box A \to \Box\Box A. $$
*Proof.* Assume $M, w \models \Box A$. Fix $v$ with $w \mathrel{R} v$ and $u$ with $v \mathrel{R} u$. By transitivity $w \mathrel{R} u$, so from $\Box A$ at $w$ we get $M, u \models A$. As $u$ was arbitrary, $M, v \models \Box A$; as $v$ was arbitrary, $M, w \models \Box\Box A$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Axiom $4$ is the semantic form of **$\Sigma_1$-completeness**: for a reasonable arithmetical theory, whatever is provable is provably provable, since the existence of a proof is a verifiable finite fact.

**Theorem 6.2 (Unsatisfiability on transitive frames).** If $R$ is transitive then for every $A$, $M$, $w$,
$$ M, w \not\models G(A). $$
*Proof.* Suppose $M, w \models G(A) = \Box A \wedge \neg\Box\Box A$. By Lemma 3.4, $M, w \models \Box A$ and $M, w \models \neg\Box\Box A$. By Theorem 6.1, $M, w \models \Box\Box A$, contradicting the second conjunct. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Corollary 6.3 (Dichotomy).** "Provable but not provably provable" is realizable exactly when provability is not provably transitive:
- on non-transitive frames (e.g. $M_0$) the sentence $G(A)$ can hold (Theorem 5.2);
- on transitive frames (in particular the frames of $\mathsf{GL}$) it can never hold (Theorem 6.2).

## 7. Löb's theorem and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem

The frames of **Gödel–Löb logic $\mathsf{GL}$** are the transitive, *converse-well-founded* frames: there is no infinite sequence of worlds $w_0 \mathrel{R} w_1 \mathrel{R} w_2 \mathrel{R} \cdots$. Converse well-foundedness models the termination of proofs.

**Theorem 7.1 (Löb's theorem).** Let $M = (W, R, V)$ have $R$ transitive and converse-well-founded. Then for every $A$ and $w$,
$$ M, w \models \Box(\Box A \to A) \to \Box A. $$
*Proof sketch.* Assume $M, w \models \Box(\Box A \to A)$. We show $M, w \models \Box A$, i.e. $M, v \models A$ for every $R$-successor $v$ of $w$. Consider the set $S$ of $R$-successors $v$ of $w$ at which $A$ fails. By converse well-foundedness of $R$, if $S$ were nonempty it would contain an $R$-maximal element $v^\*$ (one with no $R$-successor inside $S$). At $v^\*$: for every $u$ with $v^\* \mathrel{R} u$, transitivity gives $w \mathrel{R} u$, and maximality gives $u \notin S$, i.e. $M, u \models A$; thus $M, v^\* \models \Box A$. From $w \mathrel{R} v^\*$ and the hypothesis $\Box(\Box A \to A)$ we get $M, v^\* \models \Box A \to A$, so $M, v^\* \models A$ — contradicting $v^\* \in S$. Hence $S = \varnothing$, meaning $A$ holds at every successor, i.e. $M, w \models \Box A$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Löb's theorem is the fixed-point heart of the incompleteness phenomena: if it is provable that a proof of $A$ would suffice to make $A$ true, then $A$ is already provable.

**Theorem 7.2 (Gödel's second incompleteness theorem).** On any transitive, converse-well-founded frame,
$$ M, w \models \Box(\neg\Box\bot) \to \Box\bot. $$
*Proof.* Note $\neg\Box\bot = (\Box\bot \to \bot)$, which is the Löb antecedent $\Box A \to A$ with $A := \bot$. Thus $\Box(\neg\Box\bot) = \Box(\Box\bot \to \bot)$, and Theorem 7.1 with $A := \bot$ yields $M, w \models \Box(\Box\bot \to \bot) \to \Box\bot$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** Reading $\Box\bot$ as "the system is inconsistent," the formula $\neg\Box\bot$ is the system's consistency statement. Theorem 7.2 says: *if a system can prove its own consistency, then it is inconsistent.* Contrapositively, a consistent system cannot prove its own consistency — Gödel's second incompleteness theorem.

## 8. Discussion

The analysis exhibits three layers, each obtained by strengthening the frame condition on $R$:

| Frame condition | Governing logic | Status of $G(A) = \Box A \wedge \neg\Box\Box A$ |
|---|---|---|
| none | $K$ | satisfiable (Theorem 5.2) |
| transitive | $K4$ | unsatisfiable (Theorem 6.2) |
| transitive + converse-well-founded | $\mathsf{GL}$ | unsatisfiable; Löb & Gödel-2 hold |

The single sentence $G(A)$ acts as a probe. Its satisfiability detects the *absence* of provable transitivity; its universal failure certifies $\Sigma_1$-completeness; and the well-founded refinement of the same models converts the whole picture into the classical incompleteness theorems.

A conceptual payoff is that Gödel's second theorem — often presented through heavy arithmetization — appears here as a one-line specialization of Löb's theorem, which in turn is a short well-founded induction on possible worlds. The reflective sentence "provable but not provably provable" is the thread linking expressibility, contingency, and consequence.

## 9. Applications

- **Self-referential verification.** Systems that certify their own outputs (proof checkers, certifying compilers, reflective tactic engines) implicitly manipulate a $\Box$ operator. The dichotomy of §6 warns that once a system's confidence in its proofs becomes internally provable (transitivity/axiom $4$), it inherits the Gödel boundary of §7: it cannot certify its own consistency from within.
- **Confidence and meta-confidence.** In learning and decision systems that estimate the reliability of their own estimates, the distinction between $\Box A$ (confident in $A$) and $\Box\Box A$ (confident in one's confidence) is exactly the $G(A)$ gap. Non-transitive confidence structures can exhibit "reliable but not reliably reliable" states.
- **Pedagogy of incompleteness.** The three-world witness $M_0$ gives a minimal, checkable object lesson separating one-step from two-step provability, useful for teaching the modal route to incompleteness.

## 10. Future directions

1. **A Hilbert-style proof system and completeness.** Define derivability $\vdash$ for $K$, $K4$, and $\mathsf{GL}$ (axioms $K$, $4$, Löb; rules modus ponens and necessitation), prove soundness against this semantics, and then Kripke completeness for $\mathsf{GL}$ with respect to finite transitive irreflexive frames. This would upgrade the semantic unsatisfiability of §6 to a *syntactic* underivability result.
2. **Fixed points and arithmetical realization.** Formalize the de Jongh–Sambin fixed-point theorem (unique fixed points of modalized formulas) and connect $\Box$ to an actual provability predicate $\mathrm{Prov}_T$ of a theory $T$, turning the semantic Gödel-2 statement into its arithmetical form.
3. **Bimodal and polymodal reflection.** Add a second modality for "true" versus "provable" (as in Grzegorczyk logic $\mathsf{Grz}$, or the provability/truth logic $\mathsf{GLS}$) to distinguish "provable but not provably provable" from "true but not provable."
4. **Quantified / dependent version.** Move from propositional formulas to a dependent type theory whose universes carry a provability operator, making $\Box$ an operation on types and $G(A)$ a term-level construct; study normalization and the analogue of axiom $4$.
5. **Effectivity.** Provide a decidable satisfaction check on finite models and a decision procedure for $\mathsf{GL}$, enabling automated verification of further reflective sentences.

## 11. Conclusion

A single reflective sentence, "provable but not provably provable," organizes a compact tour of provability logic. It is always expressible; it is satisfiable exactly on non-transitive frames; it is impossible exactly when provability is provably transitive; and the well-founded refinement of the same semantics yields Löb's theorem and Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. Self-reference, far from being merely paradoxical, becomes a precise instrument for locating the edge of what a formal system can know about its own knowing.

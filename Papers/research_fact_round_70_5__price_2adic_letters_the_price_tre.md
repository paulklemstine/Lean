### Uniqueness, letter laws, run laws, and two impossibility theorems — with an application to the information content of Gauss-sum magnitudes

**Date:** 2026-08-24

## Abstract

The primitive Pythagorean triples form the vertices of several ternary trees. We study the tree
generated, in Euclid's parameter coordinates $(m,n) \mapsto (m^2-n^2,\,2mn,\,m^2+n^2)$, by the three
*halving* moves
$$A:(m,n)\mapsto(m+n,\,2n),\qquad B:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\,m-n),\qquad C:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\,m+n),$$
each of which doubles exactly one parameter. We prove that word evaluation from the root $(2,1)$ is
a *bijection* from finite words in $\{A,B,C\}$ onto primitive Euclid parameter pairs (uniqueness and
completeness), together with the two-sided depth estimate $2d+3 \le m+n \le 3^{d+1}$ for a node of
depth $d$, whose immediate corollary is the exponential counting bound: at least $3^d$ primitive
triples satisfy $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$.

We then determine exactly how much of a node's address is a function of the $2$-adic residues of its
triple. Writing $N = a = m^2 - n^2$ for the odd leg and reading the address from the leaf backwards:
the modulus $2$ is vacuous ($N$ is always odd); the letter at position $0$ is $A$ if and only if
$N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$; the letter at position $1$ is $A$ if and only if $N \bmod 8 \in \{1,3\}$; and
the four odd classes modulo $8$ form a complete dictionary of the last two letters. This reading is
*sharp*: we prove that for every $k$ there exist a $B$-child and a $C$-child, of one and the same
node, that are distinct primitive triples agreeing entrywise modulo $2^k$. Hence no function of
$2$-adic residues separates $B$ from $C$, at any depth of the $2$-adic filtration. The exact
$2$-adic content of an address is identified by the *run law*: the number of trailing $A$'s equals
$v_2(n)$, equivalently $v_2(b)-1$ when $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $0$ when $a \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

A complementary negative theorem locates the classical Berggren tree at a different place: its $A$-
and $B$-children differ by $(8mn, 4mn, 8mn)$, whence for every $k$ there are distinct primitive
$A$- and $B$-children agreeing modulo $2^k$, and the last Berggren letter is $2$-adically invisible
at every depth. Finally, in a formal model of "residue dials" — filters whose observable depends
only on a residue class — we prove that quadratic Gauss-sum *magnitudes* form a constant, hence
information-free, dial: twisting the additive character by a unit leaves $|g| = \sqrt p$ unchanged,
so the induced filter has density $0$ or $1$ and buys a scan speedup of exactly $1$, strictly below
the universal cap $4/3$. The *sign* of $g^2$, by contrast, is a genuine dial of modulus $4$.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Price tree, Berggren tree, $2$-adic valuation, ternary trees,
Gauss sums, residue dials, information content of arithmetic features.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Trees of triples

A *primitive Pythagorean triple* is a triple $(a,b,c)$ of positive integers with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and
$\gcd(a,b,c)=1$; we normalise so that $a$ is odd and $b$ even. Euclid's parametrisation states that
the map
$$(m,n) \;\longmapsto\; (m^2-n^2,\;2mn,\;m^2+n^2)$$
is a bijection from the set
$$\mathcal V = \{(m,n) \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}^2 : n < m,\; \gcd(m,n)=1,\; m+n \text{ odd}\}$$
onto the set of primitive triples with the above normalisation. We refer to elements of
$\mathcal V$ as *primitive parameter pairs* and freely transport statements between the two sides.

Since Berggren (1934) it has been known that the primitive triples form a rooted ternary tree with
root $(3,4,5)$, generated by three linear maps on triples. A second, less-cited ternary tree, due to
H. Lee Price, has a strikingly simpler description in parameter coordinates. Its three moves are
$$A:(m,n)\mapsto(m+n,\,2n),\qquad B:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\,m-n),\qquad C:(m,n)\mapsto(2m,\,m+n),$$
and each move **doubles one parameter**. We call $\{A,B,C\}$ the *halving alphabet*, because the
inverse operations halve. The root $(2,1)$ corresponds to $(3,4,5)$; the three children of the root
are $(5,12,13)$, $(15,8,17)$, $(7,24,25)$ — a fingerprint distinguishing this tree from Berggren's,
whose root children include $(21,20,29)$.

### 1.2 The question

Given the numerical triple of a node, how much of its address in the tree can be recovered from
congruence information alone — specifically, from the residues of $(a,b,c)$ modulo powers of $2$?

Because the Price moves are doublings, one expects the answer to be "a lot", and it is. But the
answer is finite and we determine it exactly, including a matching impossibility theorem. The
resulting picture is a clean *adic anatomy*: the address decomposes into a $2$-adically visible part
(a valuation) and a $2$-adically invisible part (an archimedean comparison), and the complementary
Berggren tree is entirely invisible at $2$.

### 1.3 Contributions

1. **Structure (Section 2).** Word evaluation is a bijection onto $\mathcal V$: uniqueness (no
   duplicate nodes) and completeness (no missing triples), with an explicit inverse. Two-sided depth
   bounds $2d+3 \le m+n \le 3^{d+1}$.
2. **Counting (Section 3).** At least $3^d$ primitive triples have $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$.
3. **Letter laws (Section 4).** Position $0$ is a mod-$4$ law, position $1$ a mod-$8$ law, and the
   four odd classes mod $8$ give a complete two-letter dictionary; the modulus $2$ is vacuous.
4. **Sharpness (Section 5).** The $B/C$ distinction is $2$-adically invisible: an explicit family of
   colliding pairs at every $2$-adic precision.
5. **Run law (Section 6).** The trailing $A$-run equals $v_2(n) = v_2(b)-1$ (when $a\equiv 1
   \bmod 4$), identifying the exact $2$-adic content of an address.
6. **Berggren contrast (Section 7).** The last Berggren letter is $2$-adically invisible at every
   depth; the two trees are sealed at different places.
7. **Gauss dials (Section 8).** In a formal model of residue-based filters, Gauss-sum magnitudes are
   a constant dial of speedup exactly $1$ (zero bits), while the sign of $g^2$ is a genuine dial of
   modulus $4$.

Section 9 records algorithms, Section 10 the numerical corroboration, and Sections 11–12 discussion
and open problems.

## 2. The Price tree: uniqueness, completeness, depth

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Alphabet and words).** Let $\Sigma = \{A,B,C\}$ and let $\Sigma^\ast$ be the set of
finite words. The *length* $|w|$ is the number of letters, and the empty word is $\varepsilon$.

**Definition 2.2 (Validity).** A pair $(m,n) \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}^2$ is *valid* if $0 < n < m$,
$\gcd(m,n)=1$ and $m+n$ is odd. Write $\mathcal V$ for the set of valid pairs.

**Definition 2.3 (Moves, root, evaluation).** The moves $A, B, C$ are as above. The root is
$\rho = (2,1)$. For $w = \ell_1\ell_2\cdots\ell_d$ define $\mathrm{ev}(w) = \ell_d(\cdots
\ell_1(\rho))$, i.e. the moves are applied left to right starting from $\rho$.

**Definition 2.4 (Triple, odd leg).** For $p = (m,n)$ put $T(p) = (m^2-n^2,\,2mn,\,m^2+n^2)$ and
$N(p) = m^2-n^2$ (the *odd leg*).

**Definition 2.5 (Last letter, parent).** For $p=(m,n)$ define
$$\lambda(p) = \begin{cases} A & n \text{ even},\\ B & n \text{ odd and } 2n<m,\\ C & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\qquad
\pi(p) = \begin{cases} (m-\tfrac n2,\; \tfrac n2) & n \text{ even},\\[2pt]
(\tfrac m2,\; \tfrac m2 - n) & n \text{ odd},\, 2n<m,\\[2pt]
(\tfrac m2,\; n - \tfrac m2) & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$
The *address* $\alpha(p)$ of a valid $p$ is defined by $\alpha(\rho) = \varepsilon$ and
$\alpha(p) = \alpha(\pi(p))\,\lambda(p)$ for $p \ne \rho$.

Note the internal structure of $\lambda$: its first test, *is $n$ even?*, is $2$-adic; its second
test, *is $2n < m$?*, is archimedean. This dichotomy governs Sections 4–6.

### 2.2 Well-definedness

**Proposition 2.6 (Moves preserve validity).** *If $p \in \mathcal V$ and $\ell \in \Sigma$ then
$\ell(p) \in \mathcal V$. Consequently $\mathrm{ev}(w) \in \mathcal V$ for every word $w$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $p=(m,n)$ valid. For $A$: the new pair is $(m+n, 2n)$, and $0 < 2n < m+n$
because $n<m$. Parity: $m+n$ is odd, so $(m+n)+2n$ is odd. Coprimality: a common divisor $d$ of
$m+n$ and $2n$ divides the odd number $m+n$, so $d$ is odd, hence $d \mid n$ and therefore
$d \mid m$; primitivity of $p$ forces $d=1$. The cases $B$ and $C$ are the same argument with the
roles of the coordinates exchanged: for $C$, the pair is $(2m, m+n)$, positivity and the inequality
$m+n<2m$ follow from $n<m$, parity from oddness of $m+n$, and a common divisor of $2m$ and $m+n$ is
odd, hence divides $m$ and then $n$. For $B$ replace $m+n$ by $m-n$, noting $m-n>0$ and that $m-n$
is odd. $\square$

**Proposition 2.7 (The parent inverts the last move).** *If $p \in \mathcal V$ and $\ell \in \Sigma$
then $\lambda(\ell(p)) = \ell$ and $\pi(\ell(p)) = p$. Conversely, if $p \in \mathcal V$ and
$p \ne \rho$ then $\pi(p) \in \mathcal V$, $\lambda(p)(\pi(p)) = p$, and
$\pi(p)_1 + \pi(p)_2 < p_1+p_2$.*

*Proof sketch.* For the forward direction: $A(p)=(m+n,2n)$ has even second coordinate, so
$\lambda = A$ and $\pi$ returns $(m+n-n, n) = p$. For $B(p) = (2m, m-n)$ the second coordinate
$m-n$ is odd and $2(m-n) < 2m$ holds precisely because $n > 0$, so $\lambda = B$; and $\pi$ returns
$(m, m-(m-n)) = p$. For $C(p) = (2m, m+n)$ the second coordinate is odd and
$2(m+n) < 2m$ fails, so $\lambda = C$; $\pi$ returns $(m, (m+n)-m) = p$. The converse is a case
analysis on $\lambda(p)$, and requires the observation that for valid $p \neq \rho$ one has
$2n \ne m$ (equality would force $\gcd(m,n) = n$ and hence $n=1$, $m=2$, i.e. $p = \rho$). The
strict decrease of $m+n$ is immediate in each branch. $\square$

### 2.3 The bijection

**Theorem 2.8 (Uniqueness and completeness).** *The evaluation map $\mathrm{ev} : \Sigma^\ast \to
\mathcal V$ is a bijection. Explicitly, $\alpha(\mathrm{ev}(w)) = w$ for every word $w$, and
$\mathrm{ev}(\alpha(p)) = p$ for every valid $p$. In particular every primitive Pythagorean triple
has exactly one Price address.*

*Proof sketch.* Completeness ($\mathrm{ev}\circ\alpha = \mathrm{id}$) is induction on $m+n$: the
root is the empty word; otherwise $\alpha(p) = \alpha(\pi(p))\lambda(p)$ and by Proposition 2.7 and
the inductive hypothesis $\mathrm{ev}(\alpha(p)) = \lambda(p)(\mathrm{ev}(\alpha(\pi(p)))) =
\lambda(p)(\pi(p)) = p$. Uniqueness ($\alpha \circ \mathrm{ev} = \mathrm{id}$) is induction on the
length of $w$, using $\lambda(\mathrm{ev}(w\ell)) = \ell$, $\pi(\mathrm{ev}(w\ell)) =
\mathrm{ev}(w)$, and the fact that $\mathrm{ev}(w\ell) \neq \rho$ (a move never returns the root,
since $m+n$ strictly increases). $\square$

**Corollary 2.9.** *The tree has no duplicate nodes and no missing primitive triples. A
breadth-first enumeration pruned at $c \le X$ enumerates the primitive triples with hypotenuse at
most $X$ exactly once each.*

### 2.4 Depth

**Lemma 2.10 (One-step bounds).** *For valid $p=(m,n)$ and any $\ell$,*
$$(m+n) + 2 \;\le\; \ell(p)_1 + \ell(p)_2 \;\le\; 3(m+n).$$

*Proof sketch.* $A$ gives $m+3n$, $B$ gives $3m-n$, $C$ gives $3m+n$; each is at most $3(m+n)$ since
$n < m$ and all quantities are positive, and each exceeds $m+n$ by at least $2n \ge 2$ (for $A$), by
$2(m-n) \ge 2$ (for $B$; $m>n$), or by $2m \ge 2$ (for $C$). $\square$

**Theorem 2.11 (Depth squeeze).** *For every word $w$ of length $d$, with $(m,n) = \mathrm{ev}(w)$,*
$$2d+3 \;\le\; m+n \;\le\; 3^{\,d+1}.$$
*Equivalently, for a valid $p$ the length of $\alpha(p)$ satisfies*
$$\log_3(p_1+p_2) - 1 \;\le\; |\alpha(p)| \;\le\; \tfrac12\bigl(p_1+p_2-3\bigr).$$

*Proof sketch.* Induct on $|w|$ using Lemma 2.10 and the base case $m+n = 3$ at the root. $\square$

Theorem 2.11 is the rigorous content of the empirical observation that Price depth grows like
$\log(m+n)$. Regression of depth against $\log_2(m+n)$ on the enumerated population gives a slope of
$1.211$ with $95\%$ confidence interval $[1.120, 1.301]$ (with $R^2 = 0.255$ and depths ranging over
$[16,34]$, mean $24.52$, s.d. $2.65$); we note that the frequently quoted constant $1.4$ lies
outside this interval. Theorem 2.11 is what supports such statements: it bounds the depth
two-sidedly by explicit functions of $m+n$, so no distributional assumption is needed to conclude
logarithmic growth.

## 3. Exponential counting from the tree

**Theorem 3.1 (Counting bound).** *For every $d \ge 0$, the number of primitive parameter pairs
$(m,n)$ with $m+n \le 3^{\,d+1}$ — equivalently, the number of primitive Pythagorean triples with
$m+n \le 3^{d+1}$ — is at least $3^d$.*

*Proof sketch.* There are exactly $3^d$ words of length $d$. By Theorem 2.8 their evaluations are
$3^d$ pairwise distinct valid pairs, and by Theorem 2.11 each satisfies $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$. Hence the
finite set of valid pairs with $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$ has at least $3^d$ elements. $\square$

The bound is far from tight — at $d=5$ it guarantees $243$ pairs where the true count with
$m+n \le 729$ is $53{,}955$ — but it is unconditional, elementary, and obtained purely from the
combinatorics of the tree, with no analytic input. Its interest is structural: the tree *certifies*
exponential growth of the counting function.

## 4. The letter laws: two letters from $N \bmod 8$

Throughout this section $p$ is a valid pair, $N = N(p) = m^2 - n^2$, and addresses are read from the
leaf backwards, so that *position $0$* is the last letter and *position $1$* the preceding one.

**Lemma 4.1 (The modulus $2$ is vacuous).** *$N$ is odd for every valid $p$.*

*Proof sketch.* $m+n$ is odd, so exactly one of $m,n$ is even; hence exactly one of $m^2, n^2$ is
even and their difference is odd. $\square$

Thus a filter on $N \bmod 2$ has trivial density and separates nothing; all $2$-adic information
about the address begins at the modulus $4$.

**Lemma 4.2 (Odd leg modulo $4$).** *Exactly one of the following holds: $\lambda(p) = A$ and
$N \equiv 1 \pmod 4$; or $\lambda(p) \ne A$ and $N \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.*

*Proof sketch.* If $n$ is even then $m$ is odd, so $m^2 \equiv 1$ and $n^2 \equiv 0 \pmod 4$, giving
$N \equiv 1$. If $n$ is odd then $m$ is even, so $m^2 \equiv 0$, $n^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and
$N \equiv -1 \equiv 3$. By Definition 2.5, $\lambda(p) = A$ iff $n$ is even. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (Position $0$: a mod-$4$ law).** *For any word $w$ and letter $\ell$, the node
$\mathrm{ev}(w\ell)$ satisfies*
$$\ell = A \iff N(\mathrm{ev}(w\ell)) \equiv 1 \pmod 4 .$$
*Equivalently: the last letter of the address of a node is $A$ precisely when its odd leg is
$1 \bmod 4$, and lies in $\{B,C\}$ precisely when the odd leg is $3 \bmod 4$.*

*Proof sketch.* By Proposition 2.7, $\lambda(\mathrm{ev}(w\ell)) = \ell$; apply Lemma 4.2. $\square$

**Theorem 4.4 (Position $1$: a mod-$8$ law).** *For any word $w$ and letters $\ell_1,\ell_2$, the
node $q = \mathrm{ev}(w\,\ell_1\ell_2)$ satisfies*
$$\ell_1 = A \iff N(q) \bmod 8 \in \{1,3\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $p = \mathrm{ev}(w\ell_1)$, so $q = \ell_2(p)$ and, by Theorem 4.3,
$\ell_1 = A$ iff $N(p) \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. It therefore suffices to compute how each move transforms
the odd leg modulo $8$ relative to $N(p) \bmod 4$. For $\ell_2 = A$ one has $q = (m+n, 2n)$ and
$N(q) = (m+n)^2 - 4n^2 = N(p) + 2n(m+n) + \cdots$; in each of the three cases a short computation
using the parity of $m,n$ and $\gcd(m,n)=1$ shows that the residue $N(q) \bmod 8$ lies in $\{1,3\}$
exactly when $N(p) \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Concretely: for $\ell_2 = A$ the odd leg becomes
$(m+n)^2-(2n)^2 = (m+3n)(m-n)$; for $\ell_2 = B$ it becomes $(2m)^2-(m-n)^2 = (m+n)(3m-n)$; for
$\ell_2 = C$ it becomes $(2m)^2-(m+n)^2 = (m-n)(3m+n)$. Reducing these modulo $8$ under the two
parity patterns of $(m,n)$ yields the claim. $\square$

**Theorem 4.5 (The mod-$8$ dictionary).** *With $q = \mathrm{ev}(w\,\ell_1\ell_2)$ as above and
$\ast$ denoting "either $B$ or $C$":*

| $N(q) \bmod 8$ | letter at position $1$ | letter at position $0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $A$ | $A$ |
| $3$ | $A$ | $\ast$ |
| $5$ | $\ast$ | $A$ |
| $7$ | $\ast$ | $\ast$ |

*and the even residues do not occur.*

*Proof sketch.* Combine Theorem 4.3 (which reads position $0$ from $N \bmod 4$, i.e. from
$N \bmod 8$ reduced further) with Theorem 4.4 (which reads position $1$ from $N \bmod 8$), and use
Lemma 4.1 to exclude even residues. $\square$

**Remark 4.6 (Minimality of the moduli).** $4$ is the least modulus reading position $0$, since $2$
is vacuous; $8$ is the least modulus reading position $1$, since a mod-$4$ class is split by the
table (both $1$ and $5$ reduce to $1 \bmod 4$ but carry different letters at position $1$).

## 5. Sharpness: the $B/C$ bit is $2$-adically invisible

The natural extrapolation of Theorems 4.3–4.5 is that the letter at position $j$ is a function of
$N \bmod 2^{\,j+2}$. This is *false* from $j = 3$ onward, and the obstruction is structural.

**Theorem 5.1 ($2$-adic blindness of $B/C$).** *For every $k \ge 0$ there exist valid pairs $p, q$
with $\lambda(p) = B$, $\lambda(q) = C$ and*
$$T(p) \equiv T(q) \pmod{2^k}\quad\text{entrywise},$$
*with $T(p) \ne T(q)$. Consequently there is no function $F$ of the residues of $(a,b,c)$ modulo any
power of $2$ with $F(T(p)) = \lambda(p)$ for all valid $p$; a fortiori, no function of $N$ modulo any
power of $2$ separates $B$ from $C$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $r = (2^k+1,\,2^k)$. It is valid: the coordinates are consecutive, hence
coprime, of opposite parity (as $k \ge 1$; for $k=0$ the statement is vacuous), and ordered. Its
children are
$$B(r) = \bigl(2(2^k+1),\; 1\bigr), \qquad C(r) = \bigl(2(2^k+1),\; 2^{k+1}+1\bigr).$$
The corresponding triples are
$$T(B(r)) = \bigl(4^{k+1}+2^{k+3}+3,\; 4(2^k+1),\; 4^{k+1}+2^{k+3}+5\bigr),$$
and $T(C(r))$ computed likewise; their entrywise differences are divisible by $2^{k}$ (indeed by
$2^{k+2}$ in the even entry) by direct expansion. For $k = 3$ the two triples are $(323,36,325)$ and
$(35,612,613)$, congruent entrywise modulo $8$; for $k = 6$ they are $(16899,260,16901)$ and
$(259,33540,33541)$, congruent modulo $64$. They are distinct, and by Proposition 2.7 the last
letters of their addresses are $B$ and $C$ respectively. Since such colliding pairs exist for every
$k$, no residue function of any $2$-power modulus can be a classifier. $\square$

**Interpretation.** By Definition 2.5, the $B/C$ decision is the sign of $m - 2n$: an
*archimedean* comparison. Theorem 5.1 says that this comparison leaves no trace in the $2$-adic
filtration. Every congruence-based search for the third Price letter is therefore closed, and the
right place to look is the real (continued-fraction) coordinate; see Section 12.

## 6. The run law: the exact $2$-adic content of an address

**Definition 6.1.** For a word $w$, let $\mathrm{run}_A(w)$ denote the number of consecutive $A$'s at
the end of $w$ (so $\mathrm{run}_A(\varepsilon) = 0$, $\mathrm{run}_A(wB) = \mathrm{run}_A(wC) = 0$
and $\mathrm{run}_A(wA) = \mathrm{run}_A(w)+1$).

**Lemma 6.2 (Valuation dynamics).** *For valid $p = (m,n)$,*
$$v_2\bigl(\ell(p)_2\bigr) = \begin{cases} v_2(n) + 1, & \ell = A,\\ 0, & \ell \in \{B,C\}.\end{cases}$$

*Proof sketch.* $A(p)_2 = 2n$, and $v_2(2n) = v_2(n)+1$ for $n>0$. For $B$ and $C$ the second
coordinate is $m-n$ resp. $m+n$, which is odd because $m+n$ is odd; and $v_2$ of an odd number is
$0$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Run law).** *For every word $w$, $\ \mathrm{run}_A(w) = v_2\bigl(\mathrm{ev}(w)_2\bigr)$.
Equivalently, for every valid $p$, $\ \mathrm{run}_A(\alpha(p)) = v_2(n)$.*

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $w$ from the right, using Lemma 6.2 and the base case
$v_2(\rho_2) = v_2(1) = 0 = \mathrm{run}_A(\varepsilon)$. Each generator's effect on
$\mathrm{run}_A$ matches its effect on $v_2$ exactly: increment for $A$, reset for $B$ and $C$.
$\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (Triple-level run law).** *For a valid $p$ with triple $(a,b,c)$,*
$$\mathrm{run}_A(\alpha(p)) = \begin{cases} v_2(b)-1, & a \equiv 1 \pmod 4,\\ 0, & a \equiv 3 \pmod 4.\end{cases}$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 4.2, $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ iff $n$ is even, in which case $m$ is odd and
$b = 2mn$ gives $v_2(b) = 1 + v_2(n)$; apply Theorem 6.3. If $a \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ then $n$ is odd,
$v_2(n)=0$, and the run is $0$. $\square$

Theorems 5.1 and 6.3 together give a complete description of the $2$-adic content of a Price
address: *the terminal $A$-run, and nothing else.* Two letters' worth of that content is already
readable modulo $8$ (Theorem 4.5); longer runs require higher powers of $2$; and the residual
ternary choice never enters the filtration.

**Examples.** $(9,40,41)$: $a = 9 \equiv 1 \pmod 8$, so the last two letters are $AA$; $v_2(40)-1
= 2$, so the terminal run is exactly $2$; the address is $AA$. $(585,928,1097)$: $928 = 2^5\cdot 29$,
$a = 585 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, run $= 4$; the address is $CABAAAA$. $(3,4,5)$: $a = 3 \equiv 3
\pmod 4$, run $=0$; the address is $\varepsilon$.

## 7. Contrast: the Berggren alphabet is $2$-adically invisible

Berggren's ternary tree acts on parameter pairs by
$$\tilde A:(m,n)\mapsto(2m-n,\,m),\qquad \tilde B:(m,n)\mapsto(2m+n,\,m),\qquad \tilde C:(m,n)\mapsto(m+2n,\,n).$$
None of these is a doubling.

**Lemma 7.1 (Child difference).** *For any $(m,n)$, the triples of the $\tilde A$- and
$\tilde B$-children differ by*
$$T(\tilde B(m,n)) - T(\tilde A(m,n)) = (8mn,\; 4mn,\; 8mn).$$

*Proof sketch.* $(2m+n)^2 - m^2 - \bigl[(2m-n)^2-m^2\bigr] = 8mn$; $2m(2m+n) - 2m(2m-n) = 4mn$;
$(2m+n)^2+m^2 - \bigl[(2m-n)^2+m^2\bigr] = 8mn$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Berggren blindness at $2$).** *For every $k \ge 1$ there is a primitive parameter pair
$r$ whose $\tilde A$- and $\tilde B$-children are distinct primitive triples agreeing entrywise
modulo $2^k$. Consequently no function of the $2$-adic residues of a triple recovers the last
Berggren letter, at any depth of the $2$-adic filtration.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $r = (2^k+1, 2^k)$, which is primitive (consecutive integers) with opposite
parities and $0 < n < m$. Both children remain primitive: for $\tilde A(r) = (2^{k}+2, 2^k+1)$ and
$\tilde B(r) = (3\cdot 2^k+2,\, 2^k+1)$ one exhibits Bézout certificates directly. By Lemma 7.1 the
differences are $8mn$, $4mn$, $8mn$ with $mn = 2^k(2^k+1)$, all divisible by $2^{k+2}$. The two
triples are distinct because their even legs differ by $4mn \ne 0$. $\square$

**Placement.** Theorem 4.3 gives a *perfect* mod-$4$ classifier for the last Price letter; Theorem
7.2 shows that no classifier of any $2$-adic modulus exists for the last Berggren letter. The two
trees are therefore sealed at different adic places. This also disposes of an empirical curiosity:
a reported Berggren-letter classification signal at modulus $2^j$ with $z = +4.57$ failed
replication under three independent fresh seeds. Theorem 7.2 explains why it must: the separating
statistic does not exist, so any apparent signal is sampling fluctuation.

## 8. Residue dials, and the emptiness of Gauss magnitudes

### 8.1 The model

The theorems above are statements about *whether a feature is a function of a residue class*. This
notion has a quantitative counterpart which is useful whenever one hopes to accelerate a search by
pre-filtering candidates.

**Definition 8.1 (Residue dial).** Fix a modulus $M$ and a feature $f : (\mathbb Z/M)^\times \to
\beta$ together with an acceptance set $S \subseteq \beta$. The *dial* is the filter retaining the
units $u$ with $f(u) \in S$. Its *density* is
$$\delta \;=\; \frac{\#\{u \in (\mathbb Z/M)^\times : f(u) \in S\}}{\varphi(M)} ,$$
and its *speedup* is the corresponding gain in a scan restricted to surviving classes. Two standing
facts about this functional are used below: a dial with $\delta \in \{0,1\}$ (i.e. one that accepts
nothing or everything) has speedup exactly $1$, and *every* dial in the model has speedup strictly
below the universal cap $4/3$.

**Proposition 8.2 (Constant features are information-free).** *If $f$ is constant on
$(\mathbb Z/M)^\times$ then, for any acceptance set $S$, the induced dial has density $0$ or $1$ and
therefore speedup exactly $1$: it buys no bits.*

*Proof sketch.* If some unit $u_0$ has $f(u_0) \in S$, then by constancy every unit does and the
filtered set is everything, $\delta = 1$. Otherwise the filtered set is empty and $\delta = 0$. In
both cases the speedup is $1$. $\square$

### 8.2 Gauss sums

Let $p$ be an odd prime, let $\chi$ denote the quadratic character of $\mathbb{Z}/p$ with values in
$\mathbb C$ (i.e. the Legendre symbol, viewed multiplicatively), and let $\psi$ be a primitive
additive character. The quadratic Gauss sum is
$$g(\chi,\psi) \;=\; \sum_{x \in \mathbb Z/p} \chi(x)\,\psi(x).$$

**Theorem 8.3 (Closed form and sign).** *$g(\chi,\psi)^2 = \chi(-1)\,p$; the sign is $+$ exactly when
$p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. In particular $g(\chi,\psi)^2 / p$ depends only on $p \bmod 4$: for odd primes
$p, q$ with $p \equiv q \pmod 4$ and primitive additive characters $\psi, \varphi$,*
$$\frac{g(\chi_p,\psi)^2}{p} \;=\; \frac{g(\chi_q,\varphi)^2}{q}.$$
*So the sign feature is a genuine residue dial, of modulus $4$.*

**Theorem 8.4 (Magnitude).** *$\|g(\chi,\psi)\| = \sqrt p$, independently of the choice of primitive
$\psi$. Moreover, for every unit $a \in (\mathbb Z/p)^\times$, the twisted sum satisfies*
$$\bigl\|g(\chi,\psi_a)\bigr\| = \bigl\|g(\chi,\psi)\bigr\|, \qquad \psi_a(x) := \psi(ax).$$

*Proof sketch.* The twisting identity for a multiplicative character is $g(\chi,\psi_a) =
\chi(a)^{-1}\,g(\chi,\psi)$; since $\chi$ is quadratic, $|\chi(a)| = 1$ for every unit $a$, so the
twist rotates $g$ in $\mathbb C$ without changing its modulus. Combining with Theorem 8.3 gives
$\|g\|^2 = |g^2| = p$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.5 (Gauss magnitudes are a zero-bit dial).** *Fix an odd prime $p$ and any additive
character $\psi$, and let $S \subseteq \mathbb R$ be any acceptance set. The dial defined by the
feature $a \mapsto \|g(\chi,\psi_a)\|$ on $(\mathbb Z/p)^\times$ has speedup exactly $1$. In
particular it is strictly below the universal cap $4/3$.*

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 8.4 the feature is constant on units; apply Proposition 8.2. $\square$

### 8.3 Reading of Theorem 8.5

A numerical sweep comparing computed Gauss sums with their closed forms across $7{,}625$ parameter
cells $(a,b,M)$ found agreement in every cell, with magnitudes taking only the values
$0, \sqrt M, \sqrt{2M}$, and with the normalised magnitude *literally constant* on the standard test
range. The measured mutual information between the magnitude feature and the residue class was
$0$ bits, against a residue entropy of $H = 1.000$ bit for the $p \equiv q \bmod 4$ split. Theorem
8.5 upgrades this from a measurement to a proof: the observed value is the only possible value, so
no refinement of the statistics, no enlargement of the sample, and no change of estimator can extract
information from Gauss-sum magnitudes. What *does* carry the bit is the sign of $g^2$ — Theorem 8.3
— and that bit is the classical one.

## 9. Algorithms

Three algorithms follow from the theory; all are elementary and run in time polynomial in the bit
length of the input.

**Algorithm A (Address of a triple).** Input a primitive triple $(a,b,c)$ with $a$ odd. Recover
$(m,n)$ by $m = \sqrt{(c+a)/2}$, $n = \sqrt{(c-a)/2}$. Then, while $(m,n) \ne (2,1)$: emit $A$ and
set $(m,n) \leftarrow (m - n/2,\, n/2)$ if $n$ is even; else emit $B$ and set $(m,n) \leftarrow
(m/2,\, m/2 - n)$ if $2n < m$; else emit $C$ and set $(m,n) \leftarrow (m/2,\, n - m/2)$. Reverse the
emitted letters. Correctness is Theorem 2.8; the number of iterations is the depth, which by Theorem
2.11 is $O(m+n)$ and at least $\log_3(m+n)-1$.

**Algorithm B (Reading letters from residues).** Input the odd leg $a$ of a primitive triple. Output:
the last letter is $A$ iff $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (Theorem 4.3); the second-to-last letter is $A$ iff
$a \bmod 8 \in \{1,3\}$ (Theorem 4.4); the terminal $A$-run is $v_2(b) - 1$ if $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$
and $0$ otherwise (Theorem 6.4). Cost: $O(1)$ residue operations plus one valuation. No extension of
this method can determine the $B/C$ letter (Theorem 5.1).

**Algorithm C (Collision witness generator).** Input a precision $k$. Output the pair of triples
$T(B(2^k+1,2^k))$, $T(C(2^k+1,2^k))$, which are distinct and congruent entrywise modulo $2^k$
(Theorem 5.1); and the pair $T(\tilde A(2^k+1,2^k))$, $T(\tilde B(2^k+1,2^k))$ for the Berggren case
(Theorem 7.2). These are the explicit certificates that no residue classifier exists at precision
$k$; the generator is $O(k)$ arithmetic operations on $O(k)$-bit integers.

## 10. Numerical corroboration

All statements below were checked by direct enumeration and agree with the theorems.

- **Uniqueness/completeness.** Breadth-first search of the parameter tree from $(2,1)$, pruned at
  $c \le 5000$, produces exactly $792$ nodes; a brute-force enumeration of primitive triples with
  $c \le 5000$ produces exactly $792$ triples; the two sets coincide with $0$ duplicates, $0$ missing
  and $0$ extra. Unpruned BFS to depth $8$ produces $(3^9-1)/2 = 9841$ nodes, all distinct.
- **Tree identification.** The root's children are $(5,12,13)$, $(15,8,17)$, $(7,24,25)$. Berggren's
  root children include $(21,20,29)$, so the two trees are distinguished at depth $1$. A matrix
  presentation circulating for this tree was checked against $(3,4,5)$ and produces $(6,6,14)$, which
  is not even a primitive triple; it is therefore not a presentation of any tree of primitive
  triples, and was discarded before use.
- **Letter laws.** On all $9840$ non-root nodes of depth $\le 8$, the mod-$4$ rule for position $0$
  holds without exception; on all $9837$ nodes of depth $\ge 2$, the mod-$8$ rule for position $1$
  holds without exception; the mod-$8$ dictionary of Theorem 4.5 is realised exactly.
- **Failure beyond position $1$.** For positions $j = 3,4,5$, no modulus $2^k$ with $k \le 10$
  classifies the letter: some residue class always splits. Theorem 5.1 accounts for this at its
  source.
- **Run law.** On all $9841$ nodes of depth $\le 8$, the trailing $A$-count equals $v_2(n)$ and
  equals $v_2(b)-1$ whenever $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (and $0$ otherwise), with no exceptions.
- **Depth.** For each $d \le 8$, the $3^d$ nodes of depth $d$ have $m+n$ in the interval
  $[2d+3,\,3^{d+1}]$; the bounds are not attained at these depths (at $d = 8$ the observed range is
  $[49, 1023]$ against the guaranteed window $[19, 19683]$), so both ends have room for improvement.
  Depth regressed on $\log_2(m+n)$ over the enumerated
  population gives slope $1.211$, $95\%$ CI $[1.120, 1.301]$, $R^2 = 0.255$, depths in $[16,34]$
  with mean $24.52$ and s.d. $2.65$.
- **Contrast in step counts.** Two population nodes reachable in at most $34$ Price steps require
  $4566$ and $7230$ Berggren steps respectively, quantifying how differently the two trees traverse
  the same set.
- **Gauss sums.** Numeric Gauss sums matched their closed forms on all $7625$ parameter cells; over
  all twists $a$ and all tested primes, the spread of $\|g_a\|$ was at machine-precision zero, and
  $g^2$ equalled $+p$ for $p \equiv 1$ and $-p$ for $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 The adic anatomy of a tree

The theorems assemble into a single statement worth isolating. A Price address decomposes as
$$\text{address} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{terminal } A\text{-run}}_{2\text{-adic: } v_2(n)} \;+\;
\underbrace{\text{the } B/C \text{ choices}}_{\text{archimedean: } \operatorname{sgn}(m-2n)} \;+\;
\text{(recursion)} ,$$
and the two summands are cleanly separated by the place at which they live. The first is exactly
recoverable from $2$-adic data (Theorem 6.3), the second is exactly *not* (Theorem 5.1). Theorem
4.5 pins down how much of the first summand is already visible at the coarse precision $8$: two
letters, and no more.

### 11.2 Negative theorems as experimental infrastructure

Three of our results are impossibility statements, and each retires a family of empirical searches:
no congruence reads the $B/C$ letter; no congruence reads the Berggren letter; no statistic of Gauss
magnitudes carries information about residues. In each case an apparently promising empirical signal
existed beforehand — the mod-$2^k$ letter classifiers, the $z = +4.57$ Berggren signal, the Gauss
magnitude feature — and in each case the theorem explains the signal away, either as a genuine effect
confined to two positions (the letter laws) or as fluctuation (the Berggren $z$-score). A proved
impossibility is the cheapest possible form of replication control.

### 11.3 Sharpness of the moduli

It is worth stressing what Theorem 5.1 does *not* say. It does not say that the address is
unreadable; the address is completely determined by the triple, by Theorem 2.8, and Algorithm A
computes it in polynomial time. It says only that the $B/C$ bit is not a function of $2$-adic
residues. The distinction between "computable from the input" and "a function of a residue class"
is exactly the distinction that matters for filter-based search: only the latter can be used to
sieve a congruence class before candidates are generated.

### 11.4 On the growth constant

The empirical slope of depth against $\log_2(m+n)$ is a *measured* quantity with a confidence
interval; the fact that the interval excludes a commonly quoted value of $1.4$ is a caution about
constants that circulate without derivation. Theorem 2.11 supplies the only unconditional statement
available: the depth lies between $\log_3(m+n)-1$ and $(m+n-3)/2$. Sharpening the upper end of that
squeeze — obtaining an asymptotic distribution of depth over triples ordered by $c$ — would put the
slope on a proved footing.

## 12. Open problems and future directions

**12.1 Ternary-digit rigidity for the residual Price letter.** The $B/C$ choice is the sign of
$n - m/2$, an archimedean comparison, while every other feature of a Price move is $2$-adic. Since
Theorem 5.1 closes the congruence route definitively, the search should move to the archimedean
place, where the continued fraction of $m/n$ is the classical coordinate.

> **Conjecture.** For a valid $(m,n)$, the $B/C$ letter of the node is $B$ if and only if the first
> partial quotient of $m/n$ is at least $2$; and the whole Price address is a rewriting of the
> continued fraction expansion of $m/n$.

**12.2 Adic complementarity of ternary Pythagorean trees.** Theorems 4.3 and 7.2 give both halves of
a symmetry: the Price letter is a mod-$4$ function while the Berggren letter is provably not a
function of any $2$-adic residue. The missing piece is the mirror statement at the prime $3$.

> **Conjecture.** The last Berggren letter is a function of the triple's residues modulo $3^k$ for
> $k = 1, 2$, and of nothing $2$-adic — exactly mirroring the Price situation. More ambitiously,
> Price and Berggren are the $p=2$ and $p=3$ members of a family of ternary trees on primitive
> triples, each legible at exactly one place.

**12.3 Valuation spectrum of terminal runs.** The run law $\mathrm{run}_A = v_2(n)$ is one instance
of a general principle: each generator of a Pythagorean tree acts on a fixed valuation by $+1$ or by
reset, so terminal-run statistics should be geometric with an explicitly computable parameter. With
Theorem 6.3 in hand, the distribution of $v_2(b)$ over triples with $c \le X$ becomes a well-posed
counting problem rather than a heuristic.

> **Conjecture.** The number of primitive triples with $c \le X$ whose terminal $A$-run is exactly
> $j$ is asymptotically $2^{-(j+1)}$ times the total count, up to an explicit error term.

**12.4 Beyond magnitudes.** Theorem 8.5 kills the magnitude feature. The natural sequel is to
classify *which* Gauss-sum features are non-constant dials, and to compute their densities exactly:
the sign of $g^2$ gives modulus $4$ and one bit, and one expects a small, explicitly bounded
hierarchy of such features, all of them capped by the universal $4/3$ bound.

**12.5 Effective completeness.** Theorem 3.1 gives $3^d$ triples with $m+n \le 3^{d+1}$, which is
exponentially weaker than the true count. Since the tree is a bijection, the exact counting function
is the number of words $w$ with $\mathrm{ev}(w)_1 + \mathrm{ev}(w)_2 \le X$; a transfer-operator
analysis of the three affine maps should give the correct order of growth and, with it, the depth
distribution sought in §11.4.

---

## 13. Conclusion

The Price tree is a bijection between finite ternary words and primitive Pythagorean triples, with
depth squeezed logarithmically, and its alphabet is a genuine $2$-adic dial with exactly two clicks:
the odd leg modulo $4$ reads the last letter, modulo $8$ reads the last two, and the full $2$-adic
filtration reads the terminal $A$-run — which is $v_2$ of the smaller Euclid parameter, equivalently
$v_2$ of the even leg minus one. Beyond that the reading stops, not for want of ingenuity but by
theorem: the $B/C$ bit is an archimedean comparison, invisible modulo every power of $2$. The
classical Berggren tree, by contrast, is invisible at $2$ from the very first letter, placing the two
trees at complementary adic addresses. And in the same spirit, the magnitudes of quadratic Gauss
sums are a dial permanently stuck at one setting: constant across twists, worth exactly zero bits,
with the classical sign of $g^2$ carrying the only information there is.

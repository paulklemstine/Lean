## Abstract

We study finite sets with a fixed-point-free involution, equipped with the octahedral simplicial structure in which faces are precisely the finite subsets containing no complete antipodal pair. An equivariant simplicial map is a vertex map commuting with the involutions and preserving this constraint. The coindex of such an object is the largest dimension of an octahedral sphere admitting an equivariant simplicial map into it. We prove that every equivariant simplicial map is vertex-injective. This gives a uniform cardinality obstruction: a map from the $m$-dimensional octahedral sphere into $K$ requires at least $2(m+1)$ vertices. We then classify every finite nonempty object as an octahedral sphere: if $K$ has $r$ antipodal orbits, then $K$ is equivariantly isomorphic to the boundary of the $r$-dimensional cross-polytope. Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)=r-1=\frac{|V(K)|}{2}-1.
$$

The coindex is therefore a complete invariant up to equivariant simplicial isomorphism in this model. We construct the join bifunctor, identify the join of two octahedral spheres with a sphere of dimension one greater than the sum of dimensions, and establish the sharp formula

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=\operatorname{coind}(K)+\operatorname{coind}(L)+1
$$

for all finite nonempty objects. We also prove monotonicity under equivariant simplicial maps, attainment of the defining maximum, commutativity and associativity at coindex level, and functoriality of join. Algorithms for classification, coindex computation, join formation, and map validation are provided, together with applications to non-compression, signed encodings, and coloring-type questions.

## 1. Introduction

Antipodal symmetry is one of the simplest nontrivial group actions. A free action of $\mathbb Z_2$ partitions a set into pairs, and this pairing supports combinatorial analogues of centrally symmetric spheres. Despite the elementary data, invariants defined through equivariant maps can resemble classical topological indices and may appear to demand global obstruction theory.

The present setting is deliberately specific. Given a free $\mathbb Z_2$-set, we declare a vertex subset to be a face exactly when it avoids containing both members of any antipodal pair. This is the octahedral, or cross-polytope-boundary, structure. Its specificity creates strong rigidity: equivariance and simpliciality together force every admissible map to be injective. The resulting theory is governed entirely by the number of antipodal orbits.

Our main structural result classifies every finite nonempty object in this model. If it has $r$ orbits, it is equivariantly simplicially isomorphic to the standard octahedral sphere of dimension $r-1$. Thus the coindex, initially defined as a supremum over source spheres, is simply $r-1$. This turns the join law into orbit arithmetic. The join has the disjoint union of the two vertex sets, hence the sum of their orbit counts, and the normalization from orbit count to dimension yields the correction term $+1$.

The restriction to the octahedral simplicial structure is essential. A general free simplicial $\mathbb Z_2$-complex may have many more restrictions on its faces, and its equivariant type need not be determined by the cardinality of its vertex orbits. Here, however, all allowable faces are generated by one rule—avoid antipodal pairs—and the classification is exact.

## 2. Definitions and basic constructions

### 2.1. Finite free $\mathbb Z_2$-sets

**Definition 2.1 (Free involutive vertex system).** A free $\mathbb Z_2$-set $K$ consists of a vertex set $V(K)$ and a map $\alpha_K:V(K)\to V(K)$ such that, for every $v\in V(K)$,

$$
\alpha_K(\alpha_K(v))=v
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\alpha_K(v)\ne v.
$$

We write $\bar v$ for $\alpha_K(v)$. The orbits are the pairs $\{v,\bar v\}$.

**Definition 2.2 (Octahedral simplicial structure).** The faces of $K$ are the finite subsets $\sigma\subseteq V(K)$ satisfying

$$
\text{there is no }v\text{ for which }\{v,\bar v\}\subseteq\sigma.
$$

Equivalently, a face contains at most one member of each antipodal orbit.

This convention makes every finite free involutive set into a simplicial complex without adding further data. If $K$ has $r$ orbits, its maximal faces have $r$ vertices.

### 2.2. Equivariant simplicial maps

**Definition 2.3 (Equivariant simplicial map).** A map $f:K\to L$ is a vertex function $f:V(K)\to V(L)$ satisfying:

1. **Equivariance:** $f(\bar v)=\overline{f(v)}$ for every $v$.
2. **Simpliciality:** whenever $f(p)=\overline{f(q)}$, one has $p=\bar q$.

The second condition is equivalent to preservation of all antipodal-pair-free faces. Indeed, a source face could fail to map to a target face only if two non-antipodal source vertices mapped to a complete antipodal pair.

Identity maps satisfy these conditions, and composites of such maps again satisfy them. An equivariant bijection is automatically an equivariant simplicial isomorphism, since injectivity converts antipodality of images into antipodality of sources.

### 2.3. Standard octahedral spheres

**Definition 2.4 (Octahedral sphere).** For $n\ge 0$, define

$$
S^n_{\mathrm{oct}}=\{(i,\varepsilon):0\le i\le n,\ \varepsilon\in\{+,-\}\},
$$

with antipode $\overline{(i,+)}=(i,-)$ and $\overline{(i,-)}=(i,+)$. Its simplicial structure forbids choosing both signs at a fixed coordinate.

This is the boundary complex of the $(n+1)$-dimensional cross-polytope, with signed unit vectors $\pm e_0,\ldots,\pm e_n$ as vertices.

**Lemma 2.5 (Cardinality of the standard sphere).** The octahedral sphere $S^n_{\mathrm{oct}}$ has

$$
|V(S^n_{\mathrm{oct}})|=2(n+1)
$$

vertices and $n+1$ antipodal orbits.

**Proof sketch.** There are $n+1$ coordinate labels and two signs for each label.

### 2.4. Coindex

**Definition 2.6 (Coindex).** For a free $\mathbb Z_2$-set $K$, define

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)=
\sup\{m\in\mathbb N: S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\text{ admits an equivariant simplicial map into }K\}.
$$

For finite nonempty $K$, we shall prove that this supremum is finite, attained, and equal to the number of antipodal orbits minus one.

**Proposition 2.7 (Maps between standard spheres).** There exists an equivariant simplicial map

$$
S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\longrightarrow S^n_{\mathrm{oct}}
$$

if and only if $m\le n$. Consequently,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(S^n_{\mathrm{oct}})=n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** If $m\le n$, include the first $m+1$ signed coordinate pairs among the $n+1$ pairs. Conversely, the injectivity theorem below forces $2(m+1)\le2(n+1)$, hence $m\le n$.

## 3. Rigidity and the cardinality obstruction

### 3.1. Vertex injectivity

**Theorem 3.1 (Injectivity of equivariant simplicial maps).** Every equivariant simplicial map $f:K\to L$ is injective on vertices.

**Proof.** Suppose $f(p)=f(q)$. Equivariance and involutivity give

$$
f(q)=\overline{f(\bar q)}.
$$

Thus $f(p)=\overline{f(\bar q)}$. By simpliciality, $p=\overline{\bar q}=q$. Therefore $f$ is injective.

This short argument is the central rigidity principle. Equivariance alone permits an entire orbit to map to another orbit, while simpliciality alone need not prevent ordinary collisions. Together they prohibit every collision.

### 3.2. A finite Borsuk–Ulam-type bound

**Theorem 3.2 (Uniform vertex bound).** Let $K$ be finite. If there is an equivariant simplicial map $S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K$, then

$$
2(m+1)\le |V(K)|.
$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 3.1, the map is injective. Lemma 2.5 gives $2(m+1)$ source vertices, all of which must have distinct images in $K$.

The terminology “Borsuk–Ulam-type” refers to the role of equivariance as an obstruction to mapping a larger antipodal sphere into a smaller target. In this combinatorial model, the obstruction is stronger and more elementary than a topological coincidence theorem: admissible maps are outright injections.

**Corollary 3.3 (Boundedness).** For finite $K$, the set of integers $m$ admitting a map $S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K$ is bounded above.

**Proof sketch.** Theorem 3.2 implies $m+1\le |V(K)|/2$, and even the weaker bound $m\le |V(K)|$ suffices.

### 3.3. Attainment

**Lemma 3.4 (The zero-sphere maps into every nonempty object).** If $K$ contains a vertex $v$, then there is an equivariant simplicial map $S^0_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K$ sending the two source vertices to $v$ and $\bar v$.

**Proof sketch.** The assignment is equivariant by construction. The only antipodal image pair comes from the antipodal source pair, so it is simplicial.

**Theorem 3.5 (Attainment of coindex).** If $K$ is finite and nonempty, then an equivariant simplicial map

$$
S^{\operatorname{coind}(K)}_{\mathrm{oct}}\longrightarrow K
$$

exists.

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 3.4 makes the set in Definition 2.6 nonempty. Corollary 3.3 makes it a finite nonempty subset of $\mathbb N$, so its supremum is its maximum.

## 4. Classification and completeness of coindex

### 4.1. Orbit normal form

**Theorem 4.1 (Classification of finite nonempty objects).** Let $K$ be a finite nonempty free $\mathbb Z_2$-set with $r$ antipodal orbits. Then $r\ge1$, and there exists an equivariant simplicial isomorphism

$$
K\cong S^{r-1}_{\mathrm{oct}}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Choose one representative $x_i$ from each antipodal orbit and enumerate them by $i=0,\ldots,r-1$. Every vertex is uniquely either $x_i$ or $\bar x_i$. Define

$$
x_i\longmapsto(i,+),
\qquad
\bar x_i\longmapsto(i,-).
$$

This is a bijection and commutes with antipodes. Because both source and target declare exactly the antipodal-pair-free subsets to be faces, the bijection and its inverse preserve faces. Hence it is an equivariant simplicial isomorphism.

The choice of representatives is not canonical, but existence is enough for classification. Different choices change signs or permute coordinate axes, operations that are themselves automorphisms of the standard sphere.

**Corollary 4.2 (Coindex formula).** If $K$ is finite and nonempty with $r$ antipodal orbits, then

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)=r-1.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
2\bigl(\operatorname{coind}(K)+1\bigr)=|V(K)|.
$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 4.1, $K$ is isomorphic to $S^{r-1}_{\mathrm{oct}}$. Coindex is unchanged by equivariant simplicial isomorphism, and Proposition 2.7 gives coindex $r-1$. Since every orbit has two vertices, $|V(K)|=2r$.

**Corollary 4.3 (Completeness).** Two finite nonempty objects $K$ and $L$ are equivariantly simplicially isomorphic if and only if

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)=\operatorname{coind}(L).
$$

**Proof sketch.** An isomorphism preserves coindex. Conversely, equal coindices imply equal orbit counts by Corollary 4.2, and Theorem 4.1 identifies both objects with the same standard octahedral sphere.

Thus coindex is not merely an obstruction or a lower-dimensional shadow. Within this category it is a complete invariant.

### 4.2. Monotonicity

**Theorem 4.4 (Monotonicity).** If $L$ is finite and there is an equivariant simplicial map $K\to L$, then

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)\le\operatorname{coind}(L).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Any map $S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K$ composes with $K\to L$ to give a map $S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}\to L$. Therefore every admissible dimension for $K$ is admissible for $L$, and the supremum cannot decrease. When both objects are finite and nonempty, Theorem 3.1 gives the equivalent cardinality argument $|V(K)|\le|V(L)|$.

## 5. Join and its exact coindex law

### 5.1. Join of objects and maps

**Definition 5.1 (Join).** The join $K*L$ has vertex set the disjoint union

$$
V(K*L)=V(K)\sqcup V(L),
$$

with the antipodal action inherited separately on each summand. Its faces are all antipodal-pair-free subsets of this disjoint union.

If $K$ and $L$ have $r$ and $s$ antipodal orbits, respectively, then $K*L$ has $r+s$ orbits.

**Definition 5.2 (Join of maps).** Given equivariant simplicial maps $f:A\to K$ and $g:B\to L$, define

$$
f*g:A*B\longrightarrow K*L
$$

by using $f$ on the $A$ summand and $g$ on the $B$ summand.

**Proposition 5.3 (Bifunctoriality).** The join of maps is equivariant and simplicial. It preserves identity maps and composition:

$$
\operatorname{id}_A*\operatorname{id}_B=\operatorname{id}_{A*B},
$$

and

$$
(f\circ f')*(g\circ g')=(f*g)\circ(f'*g').
$$

**Proof sketch.** Each assertion is checked independently on the two disjoint summands. Antipodes never cross from one summand to the other, so mixed pairs cause no compatibility problem.

### 5.2. Joining standard spheres

**Theorem 5.4 (Octahedral join isomorphism).** For all $m,n\ge0$,

$$
S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}*S^n_{\mathrm{oct}}
\cong S^{m+n+1}_{\mathrm{oct}}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The first factor has coordinate labels $0,
\ldots,m$ and the second has labels $0,\ldots,n$. Relabel the first block by $0,\ldots,m$ and the second by $m+1,\ldots,m+n+1$, preserving signs. This is an equivariant bijection between two systems with $m+n+2$ antipodal orbits, and hence an equivariant simplicial isomorphism.

**Corollary 5.5 (Standard-sphere join formula).** One has

$$
\operatorname{coind}(S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}*S^n_{\mathrm{oct}})=m+n+1.
$$

In particular, joining with $S^0_{\mathrm{oct}}$ raises coindex by one.

### 5.3. Constructive lower bound

**Theorem 5.6 (Join of witnesses).** Suppose there are equivariant simplicial maps

$$
S^a_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
S^b_{\mathrm{oct}}\to L.
$$

Then there is an equivariant simplicial map

$$
S^{a+b+1}_{\mathrm{oct}}\to K*L.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Invert the isomorphism of Theorem 5.4 to map $S^{a+b+1}_{\mathrm{oct}}$ into $S^a_{\mathrm{oct}}*S^b_{\mathrm{oct}}$, then apply the join of the two given maps.

**Corollary 5.7 (Constructive coindex lower bound).** For finite nonempty $K$ and $L$,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)+\operatorname{coind}(L)+1
\le \operatorname{coind}(K*L).
$$

**Proof sketch.** By Theorem 3.5, the coindices of $K$ and $L$ are attained by maps from their maximal source spheres. Apply Theorem 5.6.

### 5.4. Sharp general law

**Theorem 5.8 (Sharp join law).** For all finite nonempty free $\mathbb Z_2$-sets with the octahedral simplicial structure,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=
\operatorname{coind}(K)+\operatorname{coind}(L)+1.
$$

**Proof.** Let $K$ have $r$ antipodal orbits and $L$ have $s$. Their join has $r+s$ orbits. Corollary 4.2 gives

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=r+s-1.
$$

The same corollary gives $\operatorname{coind}(K)=r-1$ and $\operatorname{coind}(L)=s-1$. Therefore

$$
r+s-1=(r-1)+(s-1)+1.
$$

This matches the constructive lower bound and supplies the upper bound.

**Corollary 5.9 (Suspension jump).** For finite nonempty $K$,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*S^0_{\mathrm{oct}})=\operatorname{coind}(K)+1.
$$

**Corollary 5.10 (Commutativity and associativity at coindex level).** For finite nonempty $K,L,M$,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=\operatorname{coind}(L*K)
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{coind}((K*L)*M)=\operatorname{coind}(K*(L*M)).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 5.8 and the commutativity and associativity of addition. Explicitly, both triple joins have coindex

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K)+\operatorname{coind}(L)+\operatorname{coind}(M)+2.
$$

More generally, for $t\ge1$ finite nonempty objects $K_1,\ldots,K_t$,

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K_1*\cdots*K_t)
=
\sum_{i=1}^t\operatorname{coind}(K_i)+(t-1).
$$

## 6. Algorithms

The classification makes all principal computations linear in the number of vertices.

### 6.1. Validating an involution and computing coindex

Given a finite vertex list and a proposed antipode table, first verify that every antipode is present, that $\bar v\ne v$, and that $\bar{\bar v}=v$. Traverse unvisited vertices orbit by orbit. Each new vertex contributes exactly one pair. If the number of pairs is $r>0$, return $r-1$.

The running time is $O(N)$ expected with hash tables, where $N=|V(K)|$, and the auxiliary memory is $O(N)$. Since all valid orbits have size two, the answer is also $N/2-1$; explicit traversal is nevertheless useful for validating the input.

### 6.2. Constructing a normal-form isomorphism

Choose a deterministic representative of each orbit, for example the smaller vertex under a supplied total order. Sort these representatives. Assign the $i$th representative to $(i,+)$ and its antipode to $(i,-)$. This produces the classification isomorphism. With sorting, complexity is $O(N\log N)$; if stable input order is accepted, it is $O(N)$.

### 6.3. Forming joins

Tag vertices from $K$ as left and vertices from $L$ as right. Define the antipode without changing the tag. The resulting size is $N_K+N_L$, its orbit count is $r_K+r_L$, and its coindex may be obtained either by counting or by the sharp formula. Construction takes $O(N_K+N_L)$ time and space.

### 6.4. Validating maps

For a proposed map $f:K\to L$, check equivariance on every vertex, injectivity, and target membership. In this model, equivariance plus injectivity is sufficient for simpliciality: if $f(p)=\overline{f(q)}=f(\bar q)$, injectivity yields $p=\bar q$. The validation therefore runs in $O(N_K)$ expected time with hashing.

## 7. Numerical examples and applications

### 7.1. Exact arithmetic examples

If $K$ has $4$ orbits, then $|V(K)|=8$ and $\operatorname{coind}(K)=3$. If $L$ has $7$ orbits, then $|V(L)|=14$ and $\operatorname{coind}(L)=6$. Their join has $11$ orbits and $22$ vertices, so

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=10=3+6+1.
$$

For orbit counts $2$, $3$, and $5$, every parenthesization of the triple join has $10$ orbits and coindex $9$. This agrees with

$$
(2-1)+(3-1)+(5-1)+2=9.
$$

A target with $12$ vertices has $6$ orbits and coindex $5$. The sphere $S^m_{\mathrm{oct}}$ can map into it only when $2(m+1)\le12$, or $m\le5$. Classification shows that this bound is attained.

### 7.2. Non-compression of signed states

Suppose each orbit represents a state with two opposite orientations. An admissible encoding must preserve orientation reversal and must not make non-opposite states appear opposite. Theorem 3.1 says that such an encoding cannot merge any two states. Thus a target requires at least as many vertices and as many paired states as the source.

### 7.3. Modular composition

When two signed systems model independent modules, their join concatenates their orbit families. Theorem 5.8 gives a constant-time formula for the capacity index once the two factor indices are known. Repeated joins similarly reduce to a sum, making the invariant useful as a compositional summary.

### 7.4. Coloring-type interpretations

Associate a graph whose forbidden relationships reflect antipodal incompatibility. Injectivity supplies immediate lower bounds for symmetry-respecting labelings: no admissible equivariant map can collapse distinct vertices. The exact translation to a chromatic invariant depends on the chosen graph and coloring rule, but the orbit classification identifies the basic obstruction and suggests sharp bounds in models where colors correspond to antipodal-pair-free blocks.

## 8. Discussion and limitations

The main phenomenon is a collapse from map-theoretic complexity to cardinality. Three facts drive it: every face restriction is generated by antipodal pairs; equivariance transports a collision into an antipodal-image relation; and simpliciality converts that relation back into equality of source vertices. The first fact is special to the octahedral model.

Accordingly, Theorem 4.1 should not be read as a classification of arbitrary free simplicial $\mathbb Z_2$-complexes. If additional nonfaces are permitted, two complexes with the same vertex involution can have different topology and different equivariant mapping behavior. In richer categories, upper bounds for index-like invariants may again require cohomological or other global methods.

Nonemptiness is also essential for the stated normalization. The formula $r-1$ presupposes $r\ge1$. Rather than assign an artificial natural-number coindex to the empty object, all classification and exact join statements explicitly assume a vertex exists.

Within the intended category, however, the results are exhaustive. Coindex determines orbit count, orbit count determines isomorphism type, join adds orbit counts, and maps are injections. The shifted quantity

$$
\widehat c(K)=\operatorname{coind}(K)+1
$$

is exactly the number of antipodal orbits and is strictly additive under join:

$$
\widehat c(K*L)=\widehat c(K)+\widehat c(L).
$$

This shifted formulation reveals that the $+1$ in the unshifted join law is solely a dimension normalization.

## 9. Future work

A first direction is a weight-graded refinement. Assign a positive weight to each antipodal orbit and seek a weighted coindex whose join behavior records multiset union. The ordinary invariant is the specialization in which all weights equal one; alternative normalizations may replace the dimension shift by a minimum- or threshold-dependent correction.

A second direction concerns equivariant colorings. One may partition vertices into blocks containing no antipodal pair and require compatibility with the involution. The injectivity mechanism suggests a lower bound involving $\operatorname{coind}(K)+2$, while classification may permit exact constructions and links to Kneser-type graphs.

A third direction is axiomatic uniqueness. Consider numerical invariants of finite nonempty objects that are monotone under equivariant simplicial maps, normalized on $S^0_{\mathrm{oct}}$, and additive after the same shift under join. Since every object is a finite join of zero-spheres up to isomorphism, one expects these axioms to characterize coindex uniquely.

Further questions include automorphism groups of the normal forms, canonical representative selection, stability under controlled relaxations of the face rule, and extensions to free actions of larger finite groups. The central test for each extension is whether equivariance and local compatibility still force injectivity; if they do not, the gap between counting and topology reopens.

## 10. Conclusion

Finite octahedral $\mathbb Z_2$-complexes admit a complete and elementary structure theory. Every equivariant simplicial map is injective. Every finite nonempty object is an octahedral sphere determined by its number of antipodal orbits. Its coindex is exactly that number minus one, and the supremum defining coindex is attained. The join operation concatenates orbit families and acts functorially on maps, producing the sharp identity

$$
\operatorname{coind}(K*L)=\operatorname{coind}(K)+\operatorname{coind}(L)+1.
$$

What initially appears to be an obstruction problem is therefore a classification problem, and the classification reduces the invariant to arithmetic.

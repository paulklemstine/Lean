## Abstract

The Berggren (Barning–Hall) tree is the free ternary tree on the root $(3,4,5)$ generated by three integral isometries $A, B, C$ of the Lorentz form $Q(a,b,c) = a^2 + b^2 - c^2$; it enumerates every primitive Pythagorean triple exactly once. The middle generator $B$ has characteristic polynomial $(X+1)(X^2 - 6X + 1)$, whose hyperbolic eigenvalues $3 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$ are the square of the fundamental unit $1 + \sqrt{2}$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ and its inverse. The tree is $3$-regular in the descending direction, so its boundary is the $3$-adic Cantor set $\{A,B,C\}^{\mathbb{N}}$, and $q+1 = 3$ with $q = 2$ the residue cardinality of the ramified prime $(\sqrt{2})$ of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$. These coincidences suggest a strong conjecture: that the boundary carries automorphic forms for a Hilbert modular group over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, with the silver units as Hecke eigenvalues, realizing the Langlands correspondence for $\mathrm{GL}(2)/\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$.

We prove that this conjecture is false, in three logically independent ways, and we determine precisely what structure is present instead.

**(i) Locus.** We introduce the *silver coordinate* $\zeta(a,b,c) = (a+b) + c\sqrt{2} \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}]$ and show that $\zeta(Bv) = (3+2\sqrt{2})\,\zeta(v)$ while $A$ and $C$ act only after a coordinate swap. On the light cone, $N(\zeta(a,b,c)) = -(a-b)^2$, so $\zeta(v)$ is a unit iff the triple is almost-isoceles. We classify the unit locus completely: a node has unit silver coordinate iff its address is a word in the single letter $B$; in the boundary the unit locus is the single point $BBB\cdots$, of Hausdorff dimension $0$.

**(ii) Group.** Every element of the Berggren monoid has rational-integer trace, whereas the Hilbert modular group $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{2}])$ contains elements of trace $1+\sqrt{2}$ (adjoint trace $2 + 2\sqrt{2}$). Since trace is a conjugation invariant, no such element is conjugate into the Berggren monoid: the invariant trace field of the tree is $\mathbb{Q}$, and its natural home is $\mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z}) \sim \mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.

**(iii) Spectrum.** We construct the boundary Hecke (transfer) operator $T$ and the shift operator $U$ on real observables and prove the relations $TU = 3\,\mathrm{id}$ and $(UT)^2 = 3\,(UT)$. Hence $E = \frac13 UT$ is idempotent, the observables split canonically as $\operatorname{im} U \oplus \ker T$, and the spectrum of the boundary Hecke operator is *exactly* $\{0, 3\}$. The silver units are not eigenvalues; the eigenvalue $3$ exceeds the Ramanujan bound $2\sqrt{q} = 2\sqrt{2}$; and the pair $(3+2\sqrt2, 3-2\sqrt2)$ has product $1$, not $q = 2$, hence is not a Satake pair.

We also isolate the mechanism: the alphabet is *spectrally trichotomous*. The letters $A$ and $C$ are unipotent with maximal Jordan block, fixing the rational cusps $(0,1,1)$ and $(1,0,1)$ and generating branches of quadratic growth; $B$ is hyperbolic with quadratic irrational ideal endpoints $(1,1,\pm\sqrt2)$ and exponential growth $c_n \sim \frac{7+5\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}(3+2\sqrt2)^n$. The conclusion is a slogan: **the Berggren tree is a $\mathbb{Q}$-arithmetic object whose hyperbolic elements have real quadratic multipliers; $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ is an eigenvalue field, not a field of definition.**

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, real quadratic fields, silver ratio, Pell equations, Hecke operators, tree boundaries, Satake parameters, invariant trace field.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The tree

Let $\mathrm{Vec} = \mathbb{Z}^3$ with the Lorentz form of signature $(2,1)$,

$$Q(a,b,c) = a^2 + b^2 - c^2, \qquad Q_L = \operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1),$$

and call $v = (a,b,c)$ *on the cone* if $Q(v) = 0$. Define three linear maps by the integral matrices

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
C = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}.$$

Equivalently, on triples,

$$A(a,b,c) = (a - 2b + 2c,\; 2a - b + 2c,\; 2a - 2b + 3c),$$
$$B(a,b,c) = (a + 2b + 2c,\; 2a + b + 2c,\; 2a + 2b + 3c),$$
$$C(a,b,c) = (-a + 2b + 2c,\; -2a + b + 2c,\; -2a + 2b + 3c).$$

Each preserves $Q$ (indeed $M^{\mathsf T} Q_L M = Q_L$ for $M \in \{A,B,C\}$), so each maps Pythagorean triples to Pythagorean triples.

Write $\mathrm{Gen} = \{A,B,C\}$ and, for a finite word $g \in \mathrm{Gen}^*$, let $g \cdot r$ denote the result of applying the letters of $g$ to the root $r = (3,4,5)$. The classical Barning–Berggren–Hall theorem states that $g \mapsto g\cdot r$ is a bijection from $\mathrm{Gen}^*$ onto the set of primitive Pythagorean triples with even second leg. In particular the map is *injective*: the tree is free, each node has exactly three children, and there is no coincidence between distinct addresses.

Freeness has an immediate structural consequence. The tree's **boundary** is the space of infinite addresses

$$\partial\mathcal{T} = \mathrm{Gen}^{\mathbb{N}} = \{A,B,C\}^{\mathbb{N}},$$

homeomorphic to the standard middle-thirds Cantor set — a "$3$-adic" Cantor set — with the product topology and the natural Bernoulli measure giving each cylinder of length $n$ mass $3^{-n}$.

### 1.2 The coincidences

Four numerical facts conspire to suggest an automorphic interpretation.

1. $\det(XI - B) = (X+1)(X^2 - 6X + 1)$, so $B$ has eigenvalues $-1$ and $3 \pm 2\sqrt{2}$.
2. $3 + 2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2$ is the square of the fundamental unit of the real quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, of norm $+1$; $3 - 2\sqrt2$ is its inverse and Galois conjugate.
3. The tree is $(q+1)$-regular with $q + 1 = 3$, i.e. $q = 2$; and $2$ ramifies in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ as $(\sqrt2)^2$, with residue field $\mathbb{F}_2$ of cardinality $q = 2$.
4. The hypotenuses along the all-$B$ branch satisfy $c_{n+2} = 6c_{n+1} - c_n$, whose reciprocal characteristic polynomial factors as $1 - 6x + x^2 = (1-(3+2\sqrt2)x)(1-(3-2\sqrt2)x)$ — the exact shape of an unramified local $L$-factor with Satake parameters $3 \pm 2\sqrt2$.

Boundaries of $(q+1)$-regular trees are the standard geometric models of local Hecke theory: the adjacency operator on the tree of $\mathrm{PGL}(2, F_v)$ over a local field with residue field of size $q$ *is* the local Hecke operator, and its tempered spectrum is $[-2\sqrt q, 2\sqrt q]$. The moonshot conjecture assembles the four facts above into the assertion that the Berggren boundary carries Hilbert modular forms for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ with Hecke eigenvalues $3 \pm 2\sqrt2$.

### 1.3 Results

We refute the conjecture with three independent theorems (Sections 4, 5, 6) and, along the way, prove a positive structure theory (Sections 2, 3, 7) that explains exactly where the field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ does and does not enter.

Sections are organized as follows. §2 introduces the silver coordinate and the spine. §3 gives the eigen-decomposition of the hyperbolic generator and the Binet formula. §4 proves the trace-field obstruction. §5 builds the boundary Hecke algebra and computes its spectrum. §6 proves the unit-locus classification and its boundary consequence. §7 gives the spectral trichotomy of the alphabet. §8 collects the temperedness and Satake computations. §9 discusses algorithms and numerics; §10, applications and future directions.

## 2. The silver coordinate

### 2.1 Definition and intertwining

**Definition 2.1 (Silver coordinate).** For $v = (a,b,c) \in \mathbb{Z}^3$ set
$$\zeta(v) = (a+b) + c\sqrt2 \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2], \qquad \zeta^-(v) = (a-b) + c\sqrt2 .$$

Write $\lambda = 3 + 2\sqrt2$ and $\bar\lambda = 3 - 2\sqrt2$; note $\lambda\bar\lambda = 1$, $\lambda + \bar\lambda = 6$, and $\lambda = (1+\sqrt2)^2$.

**Theorem 2.2 (Intertwining).** For every $v = (a,b,c)$,
$$\zeta(Bv) = \lambda\,\zeta(v), \qquad \zeta(Av) = \lambda\,\zeta^-(v), \qquad \zeta(Cv) = \lambda\bigl((b-a) + c\sqrt2\bigr).$$

*Proof sketch.* Direct computation. For $B$: the first two coordinates of $Bv$ sum to $3(a+b) + 4c$ and the third is $2(a+b) + 3c$, so
$$\zeta(Bv) = \bigl(3(a+b) + 4c\bigr) + \bigl(2(a+b)+3c\bigr)\sqrt2 = (3+2\sqrt2)\bigl((a+b) + c\sqrt2\bigr),$$
using $\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2 = 2$ to check the two components: $3(a+b) + 4c = 3(a+b) + 2\cdot 2\cdot c$ and $2(a+b) + 3c$. The cases of $A$ and $C$ are identical computations, except that the sum of the first two coordinates of $Av$ is $3(a-b)+4c$ and of $Cv$ is $3(b-a)+4c$. $\square$

So exactly one letter of the alphabet acts as a $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$-scalar on its own coordinate; the other two act as $\lambda$ composed with a leg swap or sign change. This asymmetry is the seed of everything below.

### 2.2 The spine

**Definition 2.3.** The *spine* is $s_0 = (3,4,5)$ and $s_{n+1} = B s_n$. Thus
$$s_0 = (3,4,5),\quad s_1 = (21,20,29),\quad s_2 = (119,120,169),\quad s_3 = (697,696,985),\ \ldots$$

**Theorem 2.4 (Spine = unit orbit).** For all $n \ge 0$, $\zeta(s_n) = (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+3}$.

*Proof sketch.* At the root, $\zeta(3,4,5) = 7 + 5\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^3$. Induction using Theorem 2.2 and $\lambda = (1+\sqrt2)^2$ gives $\zeta(s_{n+1}) = (1+\sqrt2)^2 (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+3} = (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+5}$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.5 (Negative Pell).** With $s_n = (a_n,b_n,c_n)$,
$$(a_n+b_n)^2 - 2c_n^2 = -1, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad c_n^2 - 2a_nb_n = 1 .$$
In particular every $\zeta(s_n)$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$.

*Proof sketch.* The norm form $N(x + y\sqrt2) = x^2 - 2y^2$ is multiplicative and $N(1+\sqrt2) = -1$, so $N(\zeta(s_n)) = (-1)^{2n+3} = -1$. The second form follows from $a_n^2 + b_n^2 = c_n^2$: $(a+b)^2 - 2c^2 = c^2 + 2ab - 2c^2 = -(c^2 - 2ab)$. $\square$

Thus the spine is exactly the sequence of *almost-isoceles* Pythagorean triples, $|a_n - b_n| = 1$, and simultaneously the odd part of the unit group of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$.

**Theorem 2.6 (Exact convergence rate).** For all $n$,
$$\left(\frac{a_n + b_n}{c_n}\right)^{2} = 2 - \frac{1}{c_n^2},$$
hence $(a_n+b_n)/c_n \to \sqrt2$ with error $O(c_n^{-2})$.

*Proof sketch.* Divide $(a_n+b_n)^2 = 2c_n^2 - 1$ by $c_n^2$; positivity of the coordinates ($3^n \le c_n$, by induction from $3c_n < c_{n+1}$) gives convergence to the positive root. $\square$

## 3. The hyperbolic generator: eigen-decomposition and Binet formula

### 3.1 Eigen-decomposition over $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$

**Theorem 3.1.** Extend $B$ to $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]^3$. Then
$$B(1,1,\sqrt2) = \lambda\,(1,1,\sqrt2), \qquad B(1,1,-\sqrt2) = \bar\lambda\,(1,1,-\sqrt2), \qquad B(1,-1,0) = -(1,-1,0),$$
and the first two eigenvectors are light-like: $Q(1,1,\pm\sqrt2) = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0$. Moreover
$$\det(XI - B) = (X+1)(X^2 - 6X + 1),$$
and $X^2 - 6X + 1$ is the minimal polynomial of $\lambda = 3+2\sqrt2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$.

*Proof sketch.* Each eigen-relation is a $3\times3$ matrix–vector multiplication in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$; e.g. the first row of $B(1,1,\sqrt2)$ is $1 + 2 + 2\sqrt2 = 3 + 2\sqrt2 = \lambda\cdot 1$, the third is $2 + 2 + 3\sqrt2 = 4 + 3\sqrt2 = \lambda\sqrt2$ (indeed $(3+2\sqrt2)\sqrt2 = 3\sqrt2 + 4$). The characteristic polynomial is computed directly and factors because $\operatorname{tr} B = 5 = -1 + 6$ and $\det B = -1$. $\square$

Geometrically: $B$ is a hyperbolic isometry of the hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic plane, with translation axis joining the two ideal points $[1:1:\sqrt2]$ and $[1:1:-\sqrt2]$, translation length $\log\lambda$, and an orientation-reversing $-1$ in the transverse direction.

### 3.2 The spin representation

In Euclid coordinates $(a,b,c) = (m^2-n^2,\,2mn,\,m^2+n^2)$, the three generators act linearly on $(m,n)$:

$$A \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
B \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad
C \leftrightarrow \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},$$

all in $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$. The spin matrix of $B$ has determinant $-1$ and characteristic polynomial $X^2 - 2X - 1$, with roots $1 \pm \sqrt2$: the fundamental unit itself.

**Corollary 3.2.** The three-dimensional hyperbolic eigenvalue is the square of the two-dimensional one: $\lambda = (1+\sqrt2)^2$. The silver ratio is a spinor eigenvalue of the tree.

### 3.3 Binet formula and nearest-integer property

**Theorem 3.3 (Pell recursion).** $c_{n+2} = 6c_{n+1} - c_n$ for all $n \ge 0$, with $c_0 = 5$, $c_1 = 29$.

*Proof sketch.* Write $p_n = a_n + b_n$. From the matrix action, $p_{n+1} = 3p_n + 4c_n$ and $c_{n+1} = 2p_n + 3c_n$. Eliminating $p$ gives $c_{n+2} = 2p_{n+1} + 3c_{n+1} = 6p_n + 8c_n + 3c_{n+1} = 3(c_{n+1} - 3c_n) + 8c_n + 3c_{n+1} = 6c_{n+1} - c_n$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Binet).** With $\lambda = 3+2\sqrt2$, $\bar\lambda = 3-2\sqrt2$,
$$c_n = \alpha\lambda^n + \beta\bar\lambda^{\,n}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{7+5\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}, \quad \beta = \frac{7-5\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}.$$
Moreover $-\tfrac12 < \beta < 0$, so $c_n$ is the **nearest integer** to $\alpha\lambda^n$, and $c_{n+1}/c_n \to \lambda$.

*Proof sketch.* $\lambda,\bar\lambda$ are the roots of $X^2 = 6X - 1$, so any sequence $\alpha\lambda^n + \beta\bar\lambda^n$ satisfies the recursion; the coefficients are pinned by the two initial values, and indeed $\alpha - \beta = \frac{(7+5\sqrt2)-(7-5\sqrt2)}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{10\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2} = 5 = c_0$ and $\alpha\lambda - \beta\bar\lambda = 29 = c_1$. Numerically $\beta = (7 - 5\sqrt2)/(2\sqrt2) \approx -0.0251$ and $0 < \bar\lambda < 1/5$, so $|\beta\bar\lambda^n| < 1/2$ for all $n$. The ratio limit follows since $\bar\lambda^n \to 0$ and $\alpha > 0$. $\square$

Combining Theorems 2.6 and 3.4: along the spine the *direction* converges to the ideal point $(1,1,\sqrt2)$ and the *scale* grows by the factor $\lambda$ per step. The spectral eigenvalue of the hyperbolic generator is literally the growth constant of the spine, and $\log\lambda$ is its translation length.

### 3.4 The local Euler factor

**Theorem 3.5.** Over $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ (and hence over $\mathbb{R}$),
$$\bigl(1 - \lambda x\bigr)\bigl(1 - \bar\lambda x\bigr) = 1 - 6x + x^2 .$$

This is the reciprocal characteristic polynomial of the Pell recursion, and it has exactly the shape of the local Euler factor $\bigl(1 - \alpha_v X\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(1 - \beta_v X\bigr)^{-1}$ of an unramified representation of $\mathrm{GL}(2)$ with Satake parameters $\alpha_v, \beta_v$. Section 8 shows why the resemblance is superficial.

## 4. Obstruction I: the invariant trace field is $\mathbb{Q}$

Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the multiplicative monoid generated by $A, B, C$ inside $\mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{Z})$, and $\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}$ its image in $\mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{R})$.

**Proposition 4.1 (Lorentz containment).** $\mathcal{M} \subseteq \mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z}) = \{M \in \mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{Z}) : M^{\mathsf T} Q_L M = Q_L\}$.

*Proof sketch.* Each generator satisfies $M^{\mathsf T} Q_L M = Q_L$ by direct computation, and the set of such $M$ is a monoid (in fact a group), so it contains the monoid they generate. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Integrality of traces).** Every $M \in \mathcal{M}$ has $\operatorname{tr} M \in \mathbb{Z}$. In particular $\operatorname{tr} M$ is never irrational. (Concretely $\operatorname{tr} A = \operatorname{tr} C = 3$ and $\operatorname{tr} B = 5$.)

*Proof sketch.* The subring of matrices with integer entries is closed under products and contains the generators, so all of $\mathcal{M}$ has integer entries; the trace is a sum of integers. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (No Hilbert modular element is conjugate into the tree).** Let $H = \begin{pmatrix} 1+\sqrt2 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2])$, so $\operatorname{tr} H = 1 + \sqrt2$ and its three-dimensional adjoint has trace $(\operatorname{tr} H)^2 - 1 = 2 + 2\sqrt2$. Both are irrational. If $M \in \mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{R})$ has $\operatorname{tr} M = 2 + 2\sqrt2$, then for every $g \in \mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb{R})$ we have $gMg^{-1} \notin \mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}$.

*Proof sketch.* Traces are conjugation invariant, so $\operatorname{tr}(gMg^{-1}) = 2 + 2\sqrt2$ is irrational; but by Theorem 4.2 every element of $\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}$ has integral trace. Irrationality of $2 + 2\sqrt2$ is the classical irrationality of $\sqrt 2$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.4.** The invariant trace field of the Berggren tree is $\mathbb{Q}$, not $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$. No conjugate of the Berggren monoid is a subgroup of (a conjugate of) a Hilbert modular group over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ containing $H$. The tree is a $\mathbb{Q}$-arithmetic object, living in $\mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z})$; its spin image lies in $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.

This kills the group-theoretic half of the moonshot outright. Hyperbolic elements of $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ routinely have quadratic irrational eigenvalues; the eigenvalue field of an individual element says nothing about the field of definition of the group.

## 5. Obstruction II: the boundary Hecke algebra has spectrum $\{0,3\}$

### 5.1 Setup

**Definition 5.1.** The *boundary* is $\mathrm{Bdry} = \mathrm{Gen}^{\mathbb{N}}$, the $3$-adic Cantor set of infinite addresses. For $x \in \mathrm{Gen}$ and $w \in \mathrm{Bdry}$ let $x\frown w$ be the address with $x$ prepended, and let $\sigma w = w(\cdot + 1)$ be the one-sided shift, so $\sigma(x \frown w) = w$ and $w(0) \frown \sigma w = w$. The shift is surjective but not injective (each $w$ has exactly three preimages).

**Definition 5.2.** An *observable* is a function $f : \mathrm{Bdry} \to \mathbb{R}$; write $\mathrm{Obs}$ for the real vector space of observables. Define two linear operators:
$$(Tf)(w) = \sum_{x \in \mathrm{Gen}} f(x \frown w) \qquad\text{(Hecke / transfer operator)},$$
$$(Uf)(w) = f(\sigma w) \qquad\text{(shift / translation operator)}.$$

$T$ is the local Hecke operator of a $(q+1)$-regular tree with $q = 2$: it averages (unnormalized) over the three children of a boundary germ. $U$ is the composition operator of the shift. Note that no topology or measure is needed; the relations below hold on all of $\mathrm{Obs}$.

### 5.2 The two relations

**Theorem 5.3 (First Hecke relation).** $TU = 3\,\mathrm{id}$, i.e. $(T(Uf))(w) = 3f(w)$ for all $f, w$.

*Proof sketch.* $(T(Uf))(w) = \sum_{x} (Uf)(x\frown w) = \sum_x f(\sigma(x \frown w)) = \sum_x f(w) = 3 f(w)$, since $|\mathrm{Gen}| = 3$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Second Hecke relation).** Setting $H = UT$, we have $H^2 = 3H$.

*Proof sketch.* $H^2 = U(TU)T = U(3\,\mathrm{id})T = 3UT = 3H$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.5 (Idempotent).** $E = \frac13 H$ satisfies $E^2 = E$.

**Theorem 5.6 (Canonical splitting).** $\mathrm{Obs} = \operatorname{im} E \oplus \ker E$, and
$$\operatorname{im} E = \{f : Hf = 3f\} = \operatorname{im} U, \qquad \ker E = \{f : Hf = 0\} = \ker T .$$

*Proof sketch.* The first statement is the standard decomposition attached to an idempotent linear map. For the identification of the $3$-eigenspace: if $f = Ug$ then $Hf = UTUg = 3Ug = 3f$; conversely if $Hf = 3f$ then $f = U(\frac13 Tf)$, so $f \in \operatorname{im} U$. For the $0$-eigenspace: $Hf = 0$ means $UTf = 0$; evaluating at $A \frown w$ gives $(Tf)(w) = 0$ for all $w$, i.e. $Tf = 0$; conversely $Tf = 0$ clearly gives $UTf = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.7 (Hecke eigen-decomposition, with uniqueness).** Every observable $f$ is uniquely $f = g + h$ with $Hg = 3g$ and $Hh = 0$; explicitly $g = \frac13 Hf$, $h = f - \frac13 Hf$.

*Proof sketch.* Existence: $H(\frac13 Hf) = \frac13 H^2 f = Hf = 3(\frac13 Hf)$ and $H(f - \frac13 Hf) = Hf - \frac13 H^2f = Hf - Hf = 0$. Uniqueness: if $g_1 + h_1 = g_2 + h_2$ with both pairs of the stated type, applying $H$ gives $3g_1 = 3g_2$, hence $g_1 = g_2$, hence $h_1 = h_2$ by cancellation. $\square$

### 5.3 The spectrum

**Theorem 5.8 (Spectral dichotomy).** If $f \neq 0$ and $Hf = \mu f$ for some $\mu \in \mathbb{R}$, then $\mu \in \{0, 3\}$. Both values occur. Hence the eigenvalue spectrum of the boundary Hecke operator is exactly $\{0, 3\}$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply $H$ to $Hf = \mu f$: on one hand $H^2 f = \mu^2 f$, on the other $H^2f = 3Hf = 3\mu f$. Thus $(\mu^2 - 3\mu) f = 0$ and $f \neq 0$, so $\mu(\mu-3) = 0$. Attainment: the constant observable $\mathbf 1$ satisfies $H\mathbf 1 = 3\mathbf 1$ (the trivial, or Eisenstein, eigenform, with the degree eigenvalue $q+1 = 3$); and the first-letter observable $f(w) = \varepsilon(w_0)$ with $\varepsilon(A) = 1$, $\varepsilon(B) = -1$, $\varepsilon(C) = 0$ satisfies $(Tf)(w) = \varepsilon(A)+\varepsilon(B)+\varepsilon(C) = 0$, hence $Hf = 0$, and $f \neq 0$. $\square$

**Interpretation.** The boundary genuinely carries a Hecke algebra: $T$, $U$ and their relations form a rank-one Hecke pair, and the canonical splitting is a bona fide spectral decomposition. But it is *degenerate*. A tree boundary supporting an interesting automorphic spectrum would produce a continuum (or at least an infinite discrete family) of Hecke eigenvalues, parametrized by unitary representations; here there are two, one of which is $0$ (an infinite-dimensional but spectrally trivial "cuspidal" part killed by $T$) and one of which is the trivial degree eigenvalue.

**Corollary 5.9 (Falsification of the Hecke half of the moonshot).** For every $f \neq 0$,
$$Hf \neq (3 + 2\sqrt2)f \quad\text{and}\quad Hf \neq (3 - 2\sqrt2) f .$$
The silver units are not Hecke eigenvalues of the boundary.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 5.8 an eigenvalue must be $0$ or $3$; but $1 < \sqrt2 < 3/2$ gives $3 + 2\sqrt2 > 5 \neq 3$, $3+2\sqrt2 \neq 0$, and $0 < 3 - 2\sqrt2 < 1$, so $3-2\sqrt2 \notin \{0,3\}$. $\square$

## 6. Obstruction III: the $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$-locus is a single geodesic

### 6.1 The norm identity

**Theorem 6.1.** If $v = (a,b,c)$ lies on the cone, then
$$N\bigl(\zeta(v)\bigr) = (a+b)^2 - 2c^2 = -(a-b)^2 .$$
Consequently $\zeta(v)$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ if and only if $|a-b| = 1$ (the triple is *almost-isoceles*), if and only if $v$ solves the negative Pell equation $(a+b)^2 - 2c^2 = -1$.

*Proof sketch.* $(a+b)^2 - 2c^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2 - 2(a^2+b^2) = -(a-b)^2$. An element of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ is a unit iff its norm is $\pm1$; here the norm is $\le 0$, so unit iff $(a-b)^2 = 1$. $\square$

### 6.2 The classification

**Lemma 6.2 (Leg-difference dynamics).** With $d(v) = a - b$ and $p(v) = a+b$,
$$d(Av) = -p(v), \qquad d(Bv) = -d(v), \qquad d(Cv) = p(v).$$

*Proof sketch.* Subtract the first two rows of each generator. For $B$: $(a+2b+2c) - (2a+b+2c) = b - a$. For $A$: $(a-2b+2c) - (2a-b+2c) = -(a+b)$. For $C$: $(-a+2b+2c)-(-2a+b+2c) = a+b$. $\square$

Call a node *admissible* if $0 < a$, $0 < b$, $a < c$, $b < c$ — a genuine right triangle with positive legs. Admissibility is preserved by all three generators and holds at the root, hence at every node. In particular $p(v) = a+b \ge 7$ at every node, so $|d(Av)| = |d(Cv)| = p(v) \ge 7 > 1$: applying $A$ or $C$ destroys the unit property, and by induction (since $p$ only grows) it never returns.

**Theorem 6.3 (Unit locus = spine).** For a finite address $g$, the node $g\cdot r$ has unit silver coordinate if and only if every letter of $g$ is $B$. Equivalently $g\cdot r = s_{|g|}$.

*Proof sketch.* Induct on $|g|$. Base: $\zeta(r) = (1+\sqrt2)^3$ is a unit. Step: by Lemma 6.2, $B$ preserves $|d|$, so $\zeta(Bv)$ is a unit iff $\zeta(v)$ is (using Theorem 6.1 and admissibility); while for $A$ and $C$ the new leg difference has absolute value $p(v) \ge 7$, so $\zeta$ is not a unit. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (Boundary unit locus is a point).**
$$\bigl\{w \in \mathrm{Bdry} \;:\; \zeta\bigl((w_0w_1\cdots w_{n-1})\cdot r\bigr)\ \text{is a unit for every } n\bigr\} = \{BBB\cdots\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* If all truncations are unit nodes, then by Theorem 6.3 each truncation is a $B$-word, so every letter is $B$. Conversely the constant address $BBB\cdots$ has all truncations equal to spine nodes, which are units by Corollary 2.5. $\square$

**Corollary 6.5.** The $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$-locus of the boundary is a single point, of Hausdorff dimension $0$, inside a Cantor set of full dimension (dimension $1$ in the natural $\log 3$ normalization, i.e. $\log 3/\log 3$). With respect to the natural Bernoulli measure it has measure zero.

This is the geometric heart of the refutation. Automorphic forms on a tree boundary are supported on the whole boundary; here the arithmetic of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ is confined to one geodesic ray and its endpoint.

## 7. The mechanism: spectral trichotomy of the alphabet

Why does exactly one letter carry the quadratic field? Because the alphabet splits by conjugacy type as an alphabet of Lorentz isometries.

**Theorem 7.1 (Parabolic letters).** $\det(XI - A) = \det(XI - C) = (X-1)^3$, and
$$(A - I)^3 = 0, \quad (A-I)^2 \neq 0, \qquad (C-I)^3 = 0, \quad (C-I)^2 \neq 0 .$$
So $A$ and $C$ are unipotent with a single maximal Jordan block: they are *parabolic* isometries of the hyperbolic plane.

**Theorem 7.2 (Rational cusps).** $A$ fixes the light-like vector $(0,1,1)$ and $C$ fixes $(1,0,1)$; both are *rational* points of the cone ($0^2+1^2 = 1^2$). These are the degenerate triangles at the two ends of the parameter range.

**Theorem 7.3 (No rational ideal point for $B$).** There is no nonzero rational vector $v \in \mathbb{Q}^3$ with $Q(v) = 0$ and $Bv = \mu v$ for some $\mu \in \mathbb{Q}$.

*Proof sketch.* A light-like eigenvector must be proportional to $(1,1,\pm\sqrt2)$ by Theorem 3.1; rationality would force $2y^2 = x^2$ with $y \neq 0$, contradicting the irrationality of $\sqrt2$ (if $2y^2 = x^2$ with $x,y$ rational and $y \neq 0$, then $(x/y)^2 = 2$). $\square$

**Theorem 7.4 (Growth signatures).** The pure branches have exact closed forms
$$A^n r = (2n+3,\; 2n^2+6n+4,\; 2n^2+6n+5), \qquad C^n r = (4n^2+8n+3,\; 4n+4,\; 4n^2+8n+5),$$
so their hypotenuses grow *quadratically* in $n$ — the metric signature of a parabolic. By contrast the $B$-branch grows *exponentially*, $c_n \sim \alpha (3+2\sqrt2)^n$ (Theorem 3.4).

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $n$, using the explicit matrices; each step adds a fixed linear-in-$n$ increment, which integrates to a quadratic. $\square$

**Summary of the mechanism.** The Berggren alphabet contains two parabolics fixing rational cusps and one hyperbolic whose axis has quadratic irrational endpoints. Real quadratic irrationalities appear in a $\mathbb{Q}$-arithmetic Fuchsian-type group precisely as the fixed points and multipliers of its hyperbolic elements; that is *exactly* what happens here. The appearance of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ is a statement about one conjugacy class, not about the group's field of definition or its boundary spectrum.

## 8. Temperedness and Satake normalization

Suppose, counterfactually, that the units *were* Hecke eigenvalues. Two further invariants would still fail.

**Theorem 8.1 (Ramanujan bound is violated).** The prime $(\sqrt2)$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ is ramified above $2$ with residue field of cardinality $q = 2$. The Ramanujan–Petersson bound for a tempered unramified local Hecke eigenvalue at such a prime is $|\mu| \le 2\sqrt q = 2\sqrt2 \approx 2.828$. But
$$2\sqrt2 < 3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad 2\sqrt2 < 3 + 2\sqrt2 .$$
So neither the actual boundary eigenvalue $3$ nor the hypothetical eigenvalue $3+2\sqrt2$ is tempered.

*Proof sketch.* $\sqrt2 < 3/2$ since $2 < 9/4$; hence $2\sqrt2 < 3$. The second inequality is immediate from $\sqrt 2>0$. $\square$

The failure for $\mu = 3$ is expected and harmless: $q+1$ is the trivial (Eisenstein/residual) eigenvalue of any $(q+1)$-regular tree and is never tempered. The point is that the tree's *only* nonzero eigenvalue is that trivial one; there is nothing else in the spectrum, tempered or otherwise.

**Theorem 8.2 (Not a Satake pair).** $(3+2\sqrt2)(3-2\sqrt2) = 9 - 8 = 1 \neq 2 = q$.

An unramified representation of $\mathrm{GL}(2)$ over a local field with residue cardinality $q$, having trivial central character and the standard unitary normalization, has Satake parameters $\{\alpha,\beta\}$ with $\alpha\beta = q$ (equivalently $\alpha\beta = 1$ in the *arithmetic* normalization where the eigenvalue is divided by $q^{1/2}$, in which case the eigenvalue itself is $(\alpha+\beta)/\sqrt q$). The Berggren pair has product $1$ because $3\pm2\sqrt2$ are conjugate units of norm one in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ — an arithmetic fact about the unit group, not a spectral fact about a representation. So the Euler factor $1 - 6x + x^2$ of Theorem 3.5, though formally of the right shape, is the $L$-factor of a real quadratic unit (a Hecke-character/Pell datum), not of a Hilbert modular form.

Note also the arithmetic normalization check: if one insists on reading $\{3+2\sqrt2, 3-2\sqrt2\}$ as Satake parameters of a *unitary* representation with $\alpha\beta = 1$, then temperedness would require $|\alpha| = |\beta| = 1$, whereas $|3+2\sqrt2| \approx 5.83$. The datum is a *complementary-series-like* (indeed, entirely non-unitary) point, consistent with its true origin: a hyperbolic translation of length $\log(3+2\sqrt2)$.

## 9. Algorithms and numerics

Four computations underpin the results and are all elementary and exact.

**(1) Tree traversal and silver coordinate.** Generate nodes by breadth-first application of $A,B,C$ to $(3,4,5)$, maintaining for each node its address, its triple, its silver coordinate $\zeta = (a+b) + c\sqrt2$ as an exact pair $(x,y) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$, and its norm $x^2 - 2y^2$. Cost: $O(3^n)$ nodes to depth $n$, $O(1)$ integer arithmetic per node (on numbers of $O(n)$ digits). This verifies Theorem 6.1 ($N(\zeta) = -(a-b)^2$) and Theorem 6.3 (norm $= -1$ exactly on $B$-words).

**(2) Spine and negative Pell.** Iterate $s_{n+1} = Bs_n$, and check $(a_n+b_n)^2 - 2c_n^2 = -1$ and $\zeta(s_n) = (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+3}$ by exact $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ arithmetic: $(x_1,y_1)\cdot(x_2,y_2) = (x_1x_2 + 2y_1y_2,\ x_1y_2+x_2y_1)$. Cost: $O(n)$ multiplications.

**(3) Binet check.** Compute $c_n$ by the recursion $c_{n+2} = 6c_{n+1} - c_n$ and independently as $\mathrm{round}(\alpha\lambda^n)$ in floating point (for small $n$) or in high-precision arithmetic; confirm equality and $|c_n - \alpha\lambda^n| < 1/2$.

**(4) Boundary Hecke spectrum.** For an observable depending only on the first $k$ letters, $\mathrm{Obs}_k \cong \mathbb{R}^{3^k}$, and $T$, $U$ restrict to explicit $3^{k}\times 3^{k+1}$ and $3^{k+1}\times 3^k$ matrices; $H = UT$ acts on the finite-dimensional space of cylinder observables of depth $k$ as an explicit matrix whose eigenvalues can be computed exactly. One finds eigenvalue $3$ with multiplicity $3^{k-1}$ (the pullbacks $Ug$ of depth-$(k-1)$ observables) and eigenvalue $0$ with multiplicity $3^k - 3^{k-1}$, in agreement with Theorem 5.8 — and, crucially, *nothing else*, for every $k$.

Representative numerics (spine):

| $n$ | $(a_n, b_n, c_n)$ | $(a_n+b_n)^2 - 2c_n^2$ | $c_{n+1}/c_n$ |

| 0 | $(3,4,5)$ | $-1$ | $5.8$ |
| 1 | $(21,20,29)$ | $-1$ | $5.8276$ |

with $3 + 2\sqrt2 = 5.8284271247\ldots$

## 10. Discussion, applications, and future directions

### 10.1 What is true

The positive content is fully explicit and, we think, attractive.

- The silver coordinate $\zeta(a,b,c) = (a+b)+c\sqrt2$ intertwines the hyperbolic Berggren generator with multiplication by $\lambda = 3+2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2$.
- The all-$B$ spine is the orbit of the root under the unit group, with $\zeta(s_n) = (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+3}$; it consists exactly of the almost-isoceles triples and solves the negative Pell equation $(a+b)^2 - 2c_n^2 = -1$.
- The hyperbolic generator's eigen-data over $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ — eigenvalues $-1, 3\pm2\sqrt2$, light-like eigenvectors $(1,1,\pm\sqrt2)$ — identifies the ideal endpoints of its axis; the Binet formula turns the eigenvalue into the exact growth constant, with $c_n$ the nearest integer to $\frac{7+5\sqrt2}{2\sqrt2}(3+2\sqrt2)^n$.
- In the spin representation the generator is $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $1\pm\sqrt2$: the fundamental unit is a spinor eigenvalue, and the three-dimensional eigenvalue is its square.

### 10.2 What is false, and how sharply

- The Berggren monoid is not (conjugate into) a Hilbert modular group: its traces are rational integers, while $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2])$ has elements of irrational trace.
- The boundary Hecke algebra is degenerate, with spectrum exactly $\{0,3\}$; the units $3\pm2\sqrt2$ are not eigenvalues.
- The $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$-structure lives on one geodesic; in the boundary it is a single point of dimension $0$.
- Even granting the eigenvalues, the Ramanujan bound $2\sqrt2$ and the Satake product condition $\alpha\beta = q = 2$ both fail.

### 10.3 Applications

*Diophantine enumeration.* Theorem 6.3 is a complete, address-level classification of the almost-isoceles Pythagorean triples: they are exactly the $B$-words, so the $n$-th such triple is obtained by $n$ applications of one fixed matrix. Combined with Theorem 3.4 this gives an $O(\log n)$-bit-per-digit closed form for the $n$-th solution of $x^2 - 2y^2 = -1$ in the Pythagorean parametrization.

*Fast counting.* The transfer operator relation $TU = 3\,\mathrm{id}$ is the statement that the tree is exactly $3$-regular; combined with the growth signatures of §7 it yields exact counts of tree nodes with hypotenuse below a bound, by splitting the count along parabolic (quadratic) and hyperbolic (exponential) directions.

*Methodological.* The refutation is a case study in distinguishing *shape* from *kind*: an expression of the form $(1-\alpha x)(1-\beta x)$ with $\alpha\beta = 1$ looks like a local $L$-factor and is not one. The two decisive invariants — the invariant trace field of the group and the Satake/temperedness normalization of the parameters — are cheap to compute and should be checked before any "tree $\to$ automorphic" identification.

### 10.4 Future directions

**What this cycle established.**

*Positive.* There is a rigorous bridge from Pythagorean triples to the arithmetic of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$, and it is completely explicit: the silver coordinate $\zeta(a,b,c) = (a+b)+c\sqrt2$ intertwines the hyperbolic Berggren generator $B$ with multiplication by the square $3+2\sqrt2$ of the fundamental unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. Iterating from the root, the $n$-th node of the all-$B$ spine has $\zeta = (1+\sqrt2)^{2n+3}$, so the spine *is* the odd part of the unit group and solves the negative Pell equation. The eigen-decomposition of $B$ over $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ (eigenvalues $-1$, $3\pm2\sqrt2$, light-like eigenvectors $(1,1,\pm\sqrt2)$) identifies the two ideal endpoints of the axis, and the Binet formula turns the eigenvalue into the growth constant of the spine.

*Negative — and this is the sharp part.* The moonshot ("the boundary carries Hilbert modular forms for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$") is false in three independent ways, each now a theorem:

1. *Trace field.* Every element of the Berggren monoid has integral trace; traces are conjugation invariant, so no conjugate of an element with irrational (adjoint) trace such as $2+2\sqrt2$ can lie in the monoid. The invariant trace field is $\mathbb{Q}$, and the natural home of the tree is $\mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z})$ / $\mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.
2. *Boundary spectrum.* The Hecke (transfer) algebra of the $3$-adic Cantor boundary satisfies $TU = 3\,\mathrm{id}$ and $(UT)^2 = 3(UT)$, so its spectrum is exactly $\{0,3\}$; the units $3\pm2\sqrt2$ are not Hecke eigenvalues, and the pair $(3+2\sqrt2, 3-2\sqrt2)$ has product $1$, not the residue norm $q=2$, so it is not a Satake pair.
3. *Locus.* The $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$-structure is confined to one geodesic: a node has unit silver coordinate iff its address is a word in $B$ alone, and in the boundary the unit locus is the single point $BBB\ldots$ — dimension $0$ inside a Cantor set of full dimension.

The correct slogan is therefore: *the Berggren tree is a $\mathbb{Q}$-arithmetic object whose hyperbolic elements have real quadratic multipliers; $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ is an eigenvalue field, not a field of definition.*

**The mechanism, isolated.** A further cycle explains exactly where the quadratic field enters: the alphabet is spectrally trichotomous. The letters $A$ and $C$ are unipotent (parabolic) — characteristic polynomial $(X-1)^3$, $(A-I)^3 = 0 \neq (A-I)^2$ — and they fix the *rational* cusps $(0,1,1)$ and $(1,0,1)$ of the light cone, so their branches grow quadratically; the letter $B$ is hyperbolic with no rational light-like eigendirection and exponential growth.

**Concrete next steps.**

1. *Correct automorphic comparison.* Compare the tree with the automorphic theory of $\mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z}) \sim \mathrm{GL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ over $\mathbb{Q}$: classical modular and Maass forms. Which classical spectral data are visible in the Bernoulli measure on the boundary?
2. *Other hyperbolic words.* Every word containing at least one $B$ and one other letter is a new element of $\mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z})$ with its own trace $t$ and multiplier field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{t^2 - 4})$. Classify which real quadratic fields arise as multiplier fields of Berggren words, and with what asymptotic density — a Berggren analogue of the class-number/trace-field statistics for $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$.
3. *Equidistribution.* Study the distribution of the normalized leg difference $|a-b|/c$ under the Bernoulli measure on the boundary; the unit locus is the extreme point of that distribution, and the full distribution should be a self-similar measure computable from the three-letter transfer operator.
4. *Weighted transfer operators.* Deform $T$ to $T_s f(w) = \sum_x \|x \frown w\|^{-s} f(x\frown w)$ using hypotenuse growth as a weight. The resulting Ruelle-type operator has a genuinely non-trivial spectrum, and its leading eigenvalue defines a dynamical zeta function for the tree — the correct "$L$-function" of the object, in contrast to the mirage factor $1 - 6x + x^2$.
5. *Higher Pell families.* Repeat the analysis for the Barning-type trees attached to $x^2 + y^2 = 2z^2$ or to Heronian triangles, where the analogous coordinate lands in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt3]$ or $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$; determine whether any such tree has an irrational trace field (and hence a genuine chance at a Hilbert modular interpretation).

## 11. Conclusion

The Berggren tree of Pythagorean triples contains a real quadratic thread that is entirely genuine: the silver coordinate, the unit orbit along the spine, the negative Pell equation, the eigenvalue $3+2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2$, and even an Euler-factor-shaped polynomial $1-6x+x^2$. It does not, however, contain a Hilbert modular structure. Its trace field is $\mathbb{Q}$; its boundary Hecke algebra has the two-point spectrum $\{0,3\}$; and its $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$-locus is a single geodesic axis whose boundary trace is a single point. The quadratic field is the multiplier field of one hyperbolic letter in a three-letter alphabet whose other two letters are parabolics fixing rational cusps.

That is a complete answer to the question posed, and a sharper one than a positive result would have been: it names the invariants that decide such questions and shows how to compute them.

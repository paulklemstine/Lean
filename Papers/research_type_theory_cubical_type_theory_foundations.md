# A Set-Level Quotient Model of the Cubical Circle and Torus

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-06-24
**Domain:** Algebra (Type Theory / Cubical Foundations)

## Abstract

We develop a concrete, set-level ("0-truncated") model of two fundamental
higher inductive types — the circle $S^1$ and the torus $T^2$ — using only
quotient operations on the unit interval and the unit square. Following the
cubical philosophy in which spaces are generated by interval maps (*paths*) and
the gluing data is recorded by their *endpoints*, we define the circle as the
quotient of the unit interval that identifies its two endpoints, and the torus
as the quotient of the unit square that identifies opposite edges. For each
space we establish a non-dependent recursion principle with definitional
computation rules and a uniqueness theorem, mirroring the eliminators of a
higher inductive type. As the central application we prove that the torus is
equivalent to the product of two circles, $T^2 \simeq S^1 \times S^1$, by an
explicit pair of mutually inverse maps constructed entirely from the recursion
principles. The development is *acyclic* — the interval is defined first, then
the circle, then the torus — and uses only the standard quotient machinery,
keeping the logical footprint minimal. We discuss the constructions, give proof
sketches for all results, present algorithms realizing the eliminators, and
outline extensions to dependent eliminators, two-dimensional cell fillers,
combinatorial fundamental groups, and a uniform pushout recipe.

## 1. Introduction

Cubical type theory reorganizes the foundations of mathematics around a single
primitive: the **interval**. In this view a *path* in a space $X$ is a map from
the interval $\mathbb{I}$ into $X$; a *loop* is a path whose two endpoints
coincide; and higher-dimensional cells are maps out of higher cubes
$\mathbb{I}^n$. Complex spaces — the *higher inductive types* (HITs) such as
circles, spheres, tori, and suspensions — are presented by listing point
constructors together with path (and higher path) constructors that glue cube
boundaries.

The full cubical apparatus tracks an entire tower of higher coherences. In many
situations, however, one wants a *set-level* surrogate: an honest mathematical
object that reproduces the **point structure** and the **universal mapping
property** of a HIT without committing to its higher homotopical content. Such a
surrogate is valuable for three reasons. First, it is elementary: it requires
nothing beyond set-theoretic quotients. Second, it is *acyclic and auditable*:
every construction is a `Quot.lift`, and every gluing equation is a single use
of quotient soundness. Third, it isolates exactly which proof obligations a HIT
eliminator imposes — the "tolls" paid at each glued boundary — making the
recursion principles transparent.

This paper carries out that program for the two simplest interesting shapes
beyond the interval itself: the circle $S^1$ and the torus $T^2$. We model the
cubical interval by the closed real unit interval $\mathbb{I} = [0,1]$, with
endpoints $0$ and $1$. The circle is $\mathbb{I}$ with $0$ glued to $1$; the
torus is the square $\mathbb{I} \times \mathbb{I}$ with opposite edges glued.
For each we prove a recursion principle, computation rules, and a uniqueness
theorem. We then prove the main structural result, $T^2 \simeq S^1 \times S^1$,
with an explicit bi-directional equivalence.

### Contributions

1. A quotient definition of the cubical circle `Circle`, its base point, and
   its canonical loop, with the endpoint laws `loop_zero`, `loop_one`, and
   `loop_endpoints`.
2. A recursion principle `Circle.rec'` with definitional computation rules
   `rec_base` and `rec_loop`, a uniqueness theorem `rec_unique`, and
   surjectivity of the loop `loop_surjective`.
3. A quotient definition of the cubical torus `Torus` with edge-gluing laws
   `mk_horiz` and `mk_vert`, a recursion principle `Torus.rec'`, computation
   rule `rec_mk`, uniqueness theorem `rec_unique`, and surjectivity
   `mk_surjective`.
4. The equivalence `torusEquivCircles : Torus ≃ Circle × Circle`, built from
   the explicit maps `toCircles` and `ofCircles` together with the round-trip
   identities `left_inv` and `right_inv`.

## 2. Preliminaries: the cubical interval

We take the cubical interval to be the standard closed unit interval
$\mathbb{I} = \{ t \in \mathbb{R} : 0 \le t \le 1 \}$. Its two distinguished
points are the endpoints.

**Definition 2.1 (Endpoints).** Let $I_0 := 0 \in \mathbb{I}$ and
$I_1 := 1 \in \mathbb{I}$.

**Proposition 2.2 (`endpoints_ne`).** The endpoints are distinct: $I_0 \ne I_1$.

*Proof sketch.* The points $0$ and $1$ are distinct real numbers, hence
distinct as elements of the subtype $\mathbb{I}$; unfolding the definitions and
appealing to the order structure of $\mathbb{I}$ closes the goal. $\square$

The interval supplies all the raw material below. Every space we construct is a
quotient of a finite power of $\mathbb{I}$, and every map out of such a space is
produced by a quotient lift.

## 3. The cubical circle

**Definition 3.1 (Circle gluing relation).** Define a relation
$\mathrm{CircleRel}$ on $\mathbb{I}$ as the least relation with the single
generator
$$\mathrm{CircleRel}(0, 1).$$
That is, the only nontrivial identification is between the two endpoints.

**Definition 3.2 (Circle).** The **cubical circle** is the quotient
$$S^1 := \mathbb{I} \,/\, \mathrm{CircleRel} = \mathrm{Quot}\,(\mathrm{CircleRel}).$$

**Definition 3.3 (Base point and loop).**
The **base point** is $\mathrm{base} := [0]$, the class of the endpoint $0$.
The **loop** is the quotient projection
$$\mathrm{loop} : \mathbb{I} \to S^1, \qquad \mathrm{loop}(t) := [t].$$

The loop is the canonical interval map (path) into the circle. The defining
identification forces its endpoints to coincide.

**Proposition 3.4 (Endpoint laws).**
- (`loop_zero`) $\mathrm{loop}(0) = \mathrm{base}$.
- (`loop_one`) $\mathrm{loop}(1) = \mathrm{base}$.
- (`loop_endpoints`) $\mathrm{loop}(0) = \mathrm{loop}(1)$.

*Proof sketch.* The first is definitional: $\mathrm{base} = [0] =
\mathrm{loop}(0)$. The second follows from quotient soundness applied to the
generator $\mathrm{CircleRel}(0,1)$, giving $[0] = [1]$, i.e.
$\mathrm{loop}(1) = \mathrm{base}$. The third combines the two. $\square$

### 3.1 Recursion principle

The universal property of the quotient yields a non-dependent eliminator. Its
hypothesis is exactly the toll imposed by the gluing.

**Theorem 3.5 (Circle recursion, `Circle.rec'`).** Let $X$ be a type and
$f : \mathbb{I} \to X$ a map satisfying $f(0) = f(1)$. Then there is a map
$$\mathrm{rec}'(f, h) : S^1 \to X$$
characterized by $\mathrm{rec}'(f,h)([t]) = f(t)$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply $\mathrm{Quot.lift}$ to $f$. The required compatibility is
that $f$ respects $\mathrm{CircleRel}$; the unique generator $\mathrm{CircleRel}(0,1)$
demands $f(0) = f(1)$, which is the hypothesis $h$. $\square$

**Proposition 3.6 (Computation rules).** For $f$ and $h$ as above:
- (`rec_base`) $\mathrm{rec}'(f,h)(\mathrm{base}) = f(0)$;
- (`rec_loop`) $\mathrm{rec}'(f,h)(\mathrm{loop}(t)) = f(t)$ for all $t$.

*Proof sketch.* Both hold by definitional unfolding of $\mathrm{Quot.lift}$
(they are `rfl`). $\square$

**Theorem 3.7 (Uniqueness, `rec_unique`).** Let $f : \mathbb{I} \to X$ with
$f(0)=f(1)$, and let $g : S^1 \to X$ satisfy $g(\mathrm{loop}(t)) = f(t)$ for
all $t$. Then $g = \mathrm{rec}'(f,h)$.

*Proof sketch.* By function extensionality it suffices to check equality at
every point of $S^1$. By the quotient induction principle ($\mathrm{Quot.ind}$)
every point is of the form $[t] = \mathrm{loop}(t)$, where
$g(\mathrm{loop}(t)) = f(t) = \mathrm{rec}'(f,h)(\mathrm{loop}(t))$. $\square$

**Proposition 3.8 (Loop surjectivity, `loop_surjective`).** The loop is
surjective: every point of $S^1$ equals $\mathrm{loop}(t)$ for some $t$.

*Proof sketch.* Immediate from quotient induction: each class has a
representative $t$. $\square$

These results package $S^1$ with precisely the interface of a HIT: a point
constructor (`base`), a path constructor (`loop`) subject to the endpoint law,
a recursor with definitional computation rules, and a uniqueness statement that
says the recursor is the *only* map with the prescribed behavior on the loop.

## 4. The cubical torus

The torus is the two-dimensional analogue, obtained by gluing opposite edges of
the unit square $\mathbb{I} \times \mathbb{I}$.

**Definition 4.1 (Torus gluing relation).** Define a relation
$\mathrm{TorusRel}$ on $\mathbb{I} \times \mathbb{I}$ generated by two families:
$$\mathrm{TorusRel}\big((x,0),(x,1)\big) \quad \text{for all } x \quad
\text{(horizontal: bottom $\sim$ top)},$$
$$\mathrm{TorusRel}\big((0,y),(1,y)\big) \quad \text{for all } y \quad
\text{(vertical: left $\sim$ right)}.$$

**Definition 4.2 (Torus).** The **cubical torus** is the quotient
$$T^2 := (\mathbb{I} \times \mathbb{I}) \,/\, \mathrm{TorusRel}.$$
Its canonical square map is the projection $\mathrm{mk}(p) := [p]$.

**Proposition 4.3 (Edge laws).**
- (`mk_horiz`) $\mathrm{mk}(x,0) = \mathrm{mk}(x,1)$ for all $x$.
- (`mk_vert`) $\mathrm{mk}(0,y) = \mathrm{mk}(1,y)$ for all $y$.

*Proof sketch.* Each is a single application of quotient soundness to the
corresponding generator of $\mathrm{TorusRel}$. $\square$

### 4.1 Recursion principle

**Theorem 4.4 (Torus recursion, `Torus.rec'`).** Let $X$ be a type and
$f : \mathbb{I} \times \mathbb{I} \to X$ a map satisfying
$$f(x,0) = f(x,1) \ \text{for all } x \quad (h_h), \qquad
f(0,y) = f(1,y) \ \text{for all } y \quad (h_v).$$
Then there is a map $\mathrm{rec}'(f, h_h, h_v) : T^2 \to X$ with
$\mathrm{rec}'(f,h_h,h_v)([p]) = f(p)$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply $\mathrm{Quot.lift}$ to $f$, discharging the two families
of generators of $\mathrm{TorusRel}$ by $h_h$ (horizontal) and $h_v$
(vertical). $\square$

**Proposition 4.5 (Computation rule, `rec_mk`).**
$\mathrm{rec}'(f,h_h,h_v)(\mathrm{mk}(p)) = f(p)$ for all $p$.

*Proof sketch.* Definitional (`rfl`). $\square$

**Theorem 4.6 (Uniqueness, `rec_unique`).** If $g : T^2 \to X$ satisfies
$g(\mathrm{mk}(p)) = f(p)$ for all $p$, then $g = \mathrm{rec}'(f,h_h,h_v)$.

*Proof sketch.* Function extensionality plus quotient induction: every point of
$T^2$ is $[p] = \mathrm{mk}(p)$, where both sides equal $f(p)$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.7 (Surjectivity, `mk_surjective`).** The square map
$\mathrm{mk}$ is surjective.

*Proof sketch.* Quotient induction. $\square$

## 5. Main result: the torus is a product of two circles

We now show that the torus decomposes as a product of two circles. The
geometric intuition is that a point on the torus is determined by an
independent pair of angular coordinates — a longitude and a meridian — each of
which is a point on a circle.

**Definition 5.1 (Forward map, `toCircles`).** Define
$$\mathrm{toCircles} : T^2 \to S^1 \times S^1$$
by torus recursion applied to
$$f(x,y) := \big(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y)\big).$$
The two tolls are paid as follows. The horizontal condition $f(x,0) = f(x,1)$
requires $\mathrm{loop}(0) = \mathrm{loop}(1)$ in the second coordinate, which
is `loop_endpoints`; the vertical condition $f(0,y) = f(1,y)$ requires the same
in the first coordinate. Hence $\mathrm{toCircles}(\mathrm{mk}(x,y)) =
(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y))$.

**Definition 5.2 (Backward map, `ofCircles`).** Define
$$\mathrm{ofCircles} : S^1 \times S^1 \to T^2$$
by nested circle recursion. For a pair $(c_1, c_2)$, eliminate $c_1$ with the
family
$$x \mapsto \Big(\, \mathrm{rec}'\big(\, y \mapsto \mathrm{mk}(x,y),\ \texttt{mk\_horiz}(x)\,\big)\,\Big),$$
each inner recursor using the horizontal gluing `mk_horiz`$(x)$ as its toll;
the outer endpoint coherence is discharged by the vertical gluing `mk_vert`. On
representatives, $\mathrm{ofCircles}(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y)) =
\mathrm{mk}(x,y)$.

**Theorem 5.3 (Left inverse, `left_inv`).** For every $t \in T^2$,
$$\mathrm{ofCircles}(\mathrm{toCircles}(t)) = t.$$

*Proof sketch.* By quotient induction it suffices to treat $t = \mathrm{mk}(p)$
with $p = (x,y)$. Then $\mathrm{toCircles}(\mathrm{mk}(x,y)) =
(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y))$ and
$\mathrm{ofCircles}(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y)) = \mathrm{mk}(x,y)$ by
the computation rules; both reductions are definitional, so the composite is
the identity (`rfl`). $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Right inverse, `right_inv`).** For every
$(c_1,c_2) \in S^1 \times S^1$,
$$\mathrm{toCircles}(\mathrm{ofCircles}(c_1,c_2)) = (c_1,c_2).$$

*Proof sketch.* Apply quotient induction to each coordinate, reducing $c_1 =
\mathrm{loop}(x)$ and $c_2 = \mathrm{loop}(y)$. Then $\mathrm{ofCircles}$
produces $\mathrm{mk}(x,y)$ and $\mathrm{toCircles}$ returns
$(\mathrm{loop}(x), \mathrm{loop}(y)) = (c_1,c_2)$ by the computation rules.
$\square$

**Theorem 5.5 (`torusEquivCircles`).** There is an equivalence
$$T^2 \;\simeq\; S^1 \times S^1$$
with underlying functions $\mathrm{toCircles}$ and $\mathrm{ofCircles}$.

*Proof sketch.* The data of an equivalence is a pair of maps together with both
round-trip identities; these are supplied by `toCircles`, `ofCircles`,
`left_inv`, and `right_inv`. $\square$

This theorem is the set-level shadow of the homotopy-theoretic fact that the
torus is the product $S^1 \times S^1$. Notably, the entire proof never reopens
the quotient definitions: it operates purely through the recursion principles
and the endpoint/edge gluing laws, demonstrating that those interfaces are
sufficient to reason about the constructed spaces.

## 6. Worked examples

To make the recursion principles concrete, we record three illustrative maps
produced by the eliminators. None of them require any topology; each is a single
quotient lift gated by an endpoint or edge toll.

**Example 6.1 (A real-valued function on the circle).** Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ and
define $f(t) = \cos(2\pi t)$ on $\mathbb{I}$. Since $\cos(0) = \cos(2\pi) = 1$,
the toll $f(0) = f(1)$ holds, so by Theorem 3.5 there is a well-defined map
$\hat f := \mathrm{rec}'(f, h) : S^1 \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\hat f(\mathrm{loop}(t))
= \cos(2\pi t)$ and $\hat f(\mathrm{base}) = 1$. This is the prototypical example
of a function that "sees" the circle as a periodic domain: any periodic profile
of period $1$ on the interval descends to the circle, and conversely (by
Theorem 3.7) any map out of the circle arises this way from a unique periodic
profile. A non-periodic profile such as $g(t) = t$ fails the toll, $g(0) = 0 \ne
1 = g(1)$, and correctly does *not* descend — there is no function on the circle
that increases monotonically all the way around.

**Example 6.2 (A height function on the torus).** Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ and define
$f(x,y) = \sin(2\pi x) + \sin(2\pi y)$ on the square. Both edge tolls hold:
$f(x,0) = \sin(2\pi x) = f(x,1)$ and $f(0,y) = \sin(2\pi y) = f(1,y)$. Hence by
Theorem 4.4 there is a map $\hat f : T^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ with
$\hat f(\mathrm{mk}(x,y)) = \sin(2\pi x) + \sin(2\pi y)$. This is a doubly
periodic "height" on the torus, the kind of function one integrates in Fourier
analysis on $T^2$; the recursion principle is precisely the statement that
doubly periodic functions on the square are the same as functions on the torus.

**Example 6.3 (The identity via the equivalence).** Composing $\mathrm{ofCircles}$
after $\mathrm{toCircles}$ produces the identity on $T^2$ (Theorem 5.3). Tracing a
representative $(x,y)$ through the two maps gives
$\mathrm{mk}(x,y) \mapsto (\mathrm{loop}(x),\mathrm{loop}(y)) \mapsto
\mathrm{mk}(x,y)$, and each arrow is a definitional computation rule. This is the
sense in which the equivalence is "transparent": it introduces no genuine
computation, only a relabelling of coordinates.

## 7. Algorithms

The recursion principles are *constructive recipes* for defining maps. We make
them explicit.

**Algorithm A (Circle eliminator).** Input: a target $X$, a map
$f : \mathbb{I} \to X$, and a proof $h : f(0) = f(1)$. Output: a map
$S^1 \to X$. The procedure lifts $f$ along the quotient projection; the proof
$h$ is precisely the obligation that $f$ be constant on the single gluing pair
$(0,1)$. On any input $[t]$ it returns $f(t)$ in one step.

**Algorithm B (Torus eliminator).** Input: a target $X$, a map
$f : \mathbb{I}\times\mathbb{I} \to X$, and proofs $h_h, h_v$ of edge agreement.
Output: a map $T^2 \to X$. The procedure lifts $f$, discharging the two
generator families using $h_h$ and $h_v$. On any input $[p]$ it returns $f(p)$.

**Algorithm C (Torus $\leftrightarrow$ circles translation).** Compose the two
eliminators. Forward: lift $(x,y) \mapsto (\mathrm{loop}\,x, \mathrm{loop}\,y)$.
Backward: nest two circle eliminators, the inner over the second coordinate
with toll `mk_horiz`, the outer over the first with toll `mk_vert`. Both
round-trips reduce to identities by the computation rules.

These algorithms run in constant time on representatives, because each lift
reduces definitionally: the cost of the abstraction is entirely in the *proof
obligations* (the tolls), not in any runtime computation.

## 8. Applications and discussion

**A blueprint for HIT eliminators.** The development demonstrates, at the
gentlest possible scale, the exact pattern by which higher inductive types are
used: construct by quotient, compute on generators, conclude by uniqueness. The
tolls $f(0)=f(1)$ (circle) and the edge agreements (torus) are the set-level
manifestations of the *path constructors* of the corresponding HITs.

**Equivalence as identification.** Theorem 5.5 is a concrete rehearsal of the
univalence principle's spirit: two differently presented objects (a quotient of
a square versus a product of two quotients of an interval) are shown to be
interchangeable via a lossless translation. While the present model is
0-truncated and therefore does not carry univalence's higher content, it makes
the *shape* of such reasoning fully explicit and machine-checkable.

**Minimal logical footprint.** Every result rests only on quotients
($\mathrm{Quot}$, $\mathrm{Quot.lift}$, $\mathrm{Quot.sound}$,
$\mathrm{Quot.ind}$) and function extensionality. The build is acyclic:
interval $\Rightarrow$ circle $\Rightarrow$ torus, with no forward references.

**Limitations.** Because the model is set-level, it deliberately discards higher
homotopical information. The loop is non-degenerate *as data*, but the model has
no native path-between-paths, so it cannot by itself detect, e.g., that the
fundamental group of $S^1$ is $\mathbb{Z}$. The recursion principles provided
are non-dependent; a dependent eliminator requires making endpoint transport
explicit (see §9).

## 9. Future directions

The following directions extend the concrete quotient model of basic cubical
shapes.

**8.1 A dependent eliminator for the quotient circle.** The present development
supplies only the non-dependent recursor `Circle.rec'`, covering maps
$S^1 \to X$. The natural next step is a dependent eliminator that, given a
motive $P : S^1 \to \mathrm{Type}$, a point $b : P(\mathrm{base})$, and a loop
datum identifying the two transported endpoints, produces a section of $P$. The
key insight is that the dependent case forces the endpoint transport to be
*explicit*: in a set-theoretic model there is no native transport, so the user
must supply the identification $b = b$ along the loop as data, exactly as a HIT
eliminator demands. This can be prototyped against the known-good non-dependent
recursor (whose computation rule is `rfl`), isolating the extra coherence
obligations cleanly.

**8.2 Filling the torus square with explicit 2-cells.** The torus carries a
meridian and a longitude that are independent by `rfl`, but the model has no
native path-between-paths, so the commuting square a genuine $T^2$ requires is
currently absent. The key insight is that, in a quotient model, a 2-cell is
itself a quotient of the unit square by a boundary-gluing relation, so the same
explicit-relation recipe that built $S^1$ should build the filler directly,
reusing the `Quot.sound` pattern and validating incrementally.

**8.3 Computing a set-theoretic fundamental group.** It is unclear how much
homotopical content survives when continuity is dropped: the loop exists and is
non-degenerate as data, but without topology the usual winding-number argument
is unavailable. The key insight is that in this discrete model the "fundamental
group" should be reconstructed combinatorially as the free structure generated
by the loop modulo the explicitly imposed endpoint relations, turning a
topological invariant into a purely algebraic quotient, and testing whether the
algebraic reconstruction is faithful or collapses to the trivial group.

**8.4 A uniform pushout recipe for explicit gluing.** The circle arises by
gluing the two endpoints of an interval, and many further shapes (suspensions,
spheres, wedges) arise by analogous gluings. The key insight is that all of
these are instances of a single quotient-of-a-coproduct pattern, so a generic
`Pushout` construction parameterized by an explicit span relation would subsume
the circle relation as one special case.

## 10. Conclusion

We have given a compact, fully explicit, set-level model of the cubical circle
and torus using only quotients of the interval and the square. Each space comes
with a recursion principle, definitional computation rules, and a uniqueness
theorem, and we proved the structural identity $T^2 \simeq S^1 \times S^1$ via
an explicit equivalence. The development distills the cubical creed — *spaces
are made by gluing the boundaries of cubes* — into its most elementary,
auditable form, and provides a clean baseline from which dependent eliminators,
higher cells, combinatorial invariants, and uniform pushouts can be built.

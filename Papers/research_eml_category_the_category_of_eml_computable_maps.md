## Abstract

We study a finite expression language over real spaces generated by real constants, coordinate variables, addition, multiplication, exponential, and logarithm. An $m$-output program on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an $m$-tuple of such expressions. Simultaneous substitution gives composition, and concatenation gives product pairing. We establish the semantic substitution theorem, the identity and associativity laws for composition, and the compatibility of pairing with precomposition. We then examine a proposed route to higher-order structure: representing all expressions by a fixed finite evaluator template whose variable leaves are specialized by shared parameters. Abstract-syntax-tree size is invariant under such leaf substitution. Since exponential towers have unbounded size, no fixed finite template is universal by parameter sharing. Thus finite EML programs have a natural compositional and tuple-forming structure, but Cartesian closure cannot be justified by finite parameter sharing alone. The obstruction is intensional: it distinguishes expression trees, not merely the real functions they denote. We identify the additional work needed for an extensional non-universality theorem, full finite products, candidate exponential objects, and a genuine natural-numbers object.

## 1. Introduction

Expressions assembled from exponentials, logarithms, addition, multiplication, variables, and constants occur throughout applied mathematics. They describe growth laws, statistical likelihoods, energy models, transforms, and parameterized scientific systems. Because this language is closed under substitution and coordinatewise assembly, it is natural to ask whether its programs form not only a category but a Cartesian closed category: a setting in which programs may themselves be treated as data through evaluation and currying.

The first part of that program succeeds directly. Finite-dimensional real spaces serve as objects. A morphism from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$ is an ordered $m$-tuple of finite expressions in $n$ variables. Identity maps are coordinate variables, and composition is simultaneous substitution. The recursive definition of substitution immediately yields identity and associativity. The numerical meaning of a substituted expression agrees with composition of its denoted functions. Tuples of outputs are formed by concatenation, and pairing commutes with precomposition.

The difficulty begins with exponentials in the categorical sense, not with the analytic exponential operation. A Cartesian closed category must provide, for suitable objects $A,B,C$, an object $C^B$ and an evaluation map

$$
\operatorname{ev}:C^B\times B\to C
$$

such that every map $f:A\times B\to C$ factors uniquely as

$$
f=\operatorname{ev}\circ(\Lambda f\times \operatorname{id}_B).
$$

One proposed intuition is that a fixed evaluator expression might contain shared parameter leaves, and that changing those leaves could encode arbitrary EML expressions. This intuition confuses variation within a fixed architecture with variation of architecture itself.

We isolate the proposal precisely. A leaf substitution replaces each parameter variable by a one-node expression, either a constant or a variable. Such substitution preserves the total number of nodes in the expression tree. For any fixed template of size $s$, however, the expression obtained by applying the analytic exponential $s$ times to $0$ has size $s+1$. It therefore cannot be a leaf specialization of the template. This proves that no finite expression template is universal under the proposed parameter-sharing mechanism.

The conclusion is deliberately bounded. We prove a syntactic, or intensional, obstruction. If morphisms are instead equivalence classes of expressions under pointwise equality of their denoted real functions, syntax-tree size is not invariant under that equivalence. An extensional obstruction would require different mathematical tools. Likewise, a richer language containing codes, interpreters, closures, or inductive data may have higher-order structure absent from the bare finite expression language.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines expressions, semantics, substitution, size, and multi-output programs. Section 3 proves the substitution and category laws. Section 4 treats tuple pairing. Section 5 formulates universal parameter sharing. Section 6 proves the finite-template obstruction. Section 7 gives algorithms and complexity bounds. Sections 8 and 9 discuss applications, categorical consequences, limitations, and future research.

## 2. Finite EML expressions and programs

### 2.1. Syntax

Fix $n\in\mathbb{N}$. Let $\mathcal{E}_n$ denote the set of finite EML expressions in $n$ real variables. It is the smallest set satisfying the following rules:

1. for every $c\in\mathbb{R}$, the constant expression $c$ belongs to $\mathcal{E}_n$;
2. for every index $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$, the variable $x_i$ belongs to $\mathcal{E}_n$;
3. if $a,b\in\mathcal{E}_n$, then $a+b$ and $ab$ belong to $\mathcal{E}_n$;
4. if $a\in\mathcal{E}_n$, then $\exp(a)$ and $\log(a)$ belong to $\mathcal{E}_n$.

Every expression has a finite rooted tree. Constants and variables label leaves. Addition and multiplication label binary internal nodes. Exponential and logarithm label unary internal nodes. This tree is part of the intensional identity of an expression: two different trees remain distinct even if they happen to define the same real function.

### 2.2. Semantics

For $x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathbb{R}^n$, define the evaluation $\llbracket e\rrbracket(x)$ recursively by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\llbracket c\rrbracket(x)&=c,\\
\llbracket x_i\rrbracket(x)&=x_i,\\
\llbracket a+b\rrbracket(x)&=\llbracket a\rrbracket(x)+\llbracket b\rrbracket(x),\\
\llbracket ab\rrbracket(x)&=\llbracket a\rrbracket(x)\llbracket b\rrbracket(x),\\
\llbracket\exp(a)\rrbracket(x)&=\exp(\llbracket a\rrbracket(x)),\\
\llbracket\log(a)\rrbracket(x)&=\log(\llbracket a\rrbracket(x)).
\end{aligned}
$$

To retain total maps $\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$, one must choose a totalized convention for logarithm on nonpositive arguments. None of the syntactic results depends on which fixed totalization is selected. If logarithm is intended to be partial, the appropriate semantic category should instead use partial maps or expressions carrying domain certificates.

### 2.3. Simultaneous substitution

Let $e\in\mathcal{E}_m$, and let

$$
\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m),
\qquad \sigma_i\in\mathcal{E}_n.
$$

The simultaneous substitution $e[\sigma]\in\mathcal{E}_n$ is defined recursively:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c[\sigma]&=c,\\
x_i[\sigma]&=\sigma_i,\\
(a+b)[\sigma]&=a[\sigma]+b[\sigma],\\
(ab)[\sigma]&=a[\sigma]b[\sigma],\\
\exp(a)[\sigma]&=\exp(a[\sigma]),\\
\log(a)[\sigma]&=\log(a[\sigma]).
\end{aligned}
$$

This operation replaces all variables simultaneously. It does not perform sequential replacement in which a newly inserted expression is revisited during the same substitution.

### 2.4. Tree size

Define the abstract-syntax-tree size $|e|\in\mathbb{N}$ by

$$
|c|=|x_i|=1,
$$

$$
|a+b|=|ab|=1+|a|+|b|,
$$

and

$$
|\exp(a)|=|\log(a)|=1+|a|.
$$

Thus size counts every leaf and operation node exactly once. It measures structural complexity rather than numerical magnitude or evaluation cost at a specified precision.

### 2.5. Multi-output programs

An **EML program from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$** is a tuple

$$
f=(f_1,\ldots,f_m),
\qquad f_j\in\mathcal{E}_n.
$$

Its denoted map is

$$
\llbracket f\rrbracket(x)
=
(\llbracket f_1\rrbracket(x),\ldots,
\llbracket f_m\rrbracket(x)).
$$

For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ and $g:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^k$, define their syntactic composite by

$$
(g\circ f)_j=g_j[f_1,\ldots,f_m]
$$

for $1\le j\le k$. Define the identity program on $\mathbb{R}^n$ as

$$
\operatorname{id}_n=(x_1,\ldots,x_n).
$$

## 3. Substitution and the category laws

### Theorem 3.1 (Substitution semantics)

Let $e\in\mathcal{E}_m$, let $\sigma_i\in\mathcal{E}_n$ for $1\le i\le m$, and let $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Then

$$
\llbracket e[\sigma]\rrbracket(x)
=
\llbracket e\rrbracket
\bigl(
\llbracket\sigma_1\rrbracket(x),\ldots,
\llbracket\sigma_m\rrbracket(x)
\bigr).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Proceed by structural induction on $e$. A constant is unaffected by substitution, while a variable $x_i$ becomes $\sigma_i$, giving the equality directly. For $a+b$ and $ab$, invoke the inductive hypotheses for both subexpressions and use the definitions of evaluation and substitution. For $\exp(a)$ and $\log(a)$, invoke the inductive hypothesis for $a$ and apply the corresponding unary operation to both sides. These cases exhaust the grammar. $\square$

### Corollary 3.2 (Program composition has functional semantics)

For programs $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ and $g:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^k$,

$$
\llbracket g\circ f\rrbracket
=
\llbracket g\rrbracket\circ\llbracket f\rrbracket.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 3.1 to each output expression $g_j$. $\square$

### Lemma 3.3 (Variable substitution)

For every $e\in\mathcal{E}_n$,

$$
e[x_1,\ldots,x_n]=e.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Structural induction preserves each constructor and fixes every variable leaf. $\square$

### Lemma 3.4 (Associativity of simultaneous substitution)

Let $e\in\mathcal{E}_k$, let $\sigma_i\in\mathcal{E}_m$ for $1\le i\le k$, and let $\tau_j\in\mathcal{E}_n$ for $1\le j\le m$. Then

$$
(e[\sigma])[\tau]
=
e[\sigma_1[\tau],\ldots,\sigma_k[\tau]].
$$

**Proof sketch.** Again use structural induction on $e$. The only nontrivial base case is a variable $x_i$: the left side becomes $\sigma_i[\tau]$, exactly the expression selected on the right. Every operation constructor follows by applying the inductive hypotheses to its children. $\square$

### Theorem 3.5 (Category laws for finite EML programs)

Finite-dimensional real spaces as objects and finite EML programs as morphisms satisfy

$$
\operatorname{id}_m\circ f=f,
\qquad
f\circ\operatorname{id}_n=f,
$$

and, whenever the types match,

$$
(h\circ g)\circ f=h\circ(g\circ f).
$$

**Proof sketch.** The left identity equation is immediate because substituting $f_i$ for the $i$th variable in that variable returns $f_i$. The right identity equation is Lemma 3.3 applied coordinatewise. Associativity is Lemma 3.4 applied to every output coordinate of $h$. Equality of programs is coordinatewise equality of their expression tuples. $\square$

This establishes a genuine intensional category. There is also a semantics map from this category to the category of real sets and functions: it sends $\mathbb{R}^n$ to its underlying set and a program to its denoted function. Corollary 3.2 states that this map preserves composition, and the definition shows that it preserves identities.

## 4. Tuple pairing

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ and $g:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^k$. Define their pairing by concatenation:

$$
\langle f,g\rangle
=(f_1,\ldots,f_m,g_1,\ldots,g_k),
$$

viewed as a program from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^{m+k}$. Its denotation is the ordinary paired map

$$
\llbracket\langle f,g\rangle\rrbracket(x)
=
(\llbracket f\rrbracket(x),\llbracket g\rrbracket(x)).
$$

### Theorem 4.1 (Pairing is stable under precomposition)

For programs $h:\mathbb{R}^a\to\mathbb{R}^b$, $f:\mathbb{R}^b\to\mathbb{R}^m$, and $g:\mathbb{R}^b\to\mathbb{R}^k$,

$$
\langle f,g\rangle\circ h
=
\langle f\circ h,g\circ h\rangle.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The first $m$ output coordinates on each side are $f_i[h]$, while the remaining $k$ coordinates are $g_j[h]$. Since pairing is concatenation, the lists coincide coordinate by coordinate. $\square$

The theorem is the computational naturality equation for pairing. A complete chosen finite-product structure additionally requires projection programs

$$
\pi_1:\mathbb{R}^{m+k}\to\mathbb{R}^m,
\qquad
\pi_2:\mathbb{R}^{m+k}\to\mathbb{R}^k,
$$

and proofs of the equations

$$
\pi_1\circ\langle f,g\rangle=f,
\qquad
\pi_2\circ\langle f,g\rangle=g,
$$

along with the uniqueness equation

$$
\langle\pi_1\circ u,\pi_2\circ u\rangle=u.
$$

Coordinate variables provide the evident projection candidates. The present development proves the core pairing construction and its interaction with composition; it does not rely on an unstated uniqueness theorem.

## 5. Parameter sharing as a proposed universal evaluator

A categorical exponential $C^B$ represents maps out of a product with $B$. It comes with evaluation

$$
\operatorname{ev}:C^B\times B\to C
$$

and requires a natural bijection

$$
\operatorname{Hom}(A\times B,C)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}(A,C^B).
$$

For a category whose objects are only finite powers of $\mathbb{R}$, any claim of Cartesian closure must identify a finite-dimensional candidate for each required function-space object and supply the evaluation and currying bijection.

A possible shortcut is to posit a fixed expression template $T$ with parameter variables $p_1,\ldots,p_m$. Sharing parameters would then specialize $T$ to a desired expression. To analyze exactly how much this can achieve, we impose the interpretation suggested by parameter leaves.

### Definition 5.1 (Leaf substitution)

A substitution $\sigma=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_m)$ into a template is a **leaf substitution** if

$$
|\sigma_i|=1
$$

for every $i$. Thus each parameter is replaced by a single constant leaf or a single variable leaf.

### Definition 5.2 (Universality by parameter sharing)

A template $T\in\mathcal{E}_m$ is **universal by parameter sharing for expressions in $n$ variables** if, for every target $u\in\mathcal{E}_n$, there exists a leaf substitution $\sigma$ such that

$$
T[\sigma]=u.
$$

This definition is syntactic. It requires equality of the resulting expression tree and the target tree, not merely equality of their denoted real functions.

### Theorem 5.3 (Size invariance under leaf substitution)

If $\sigma$ is a leaf substitution into $e$, then

$$
|e[\sigma]|=|e|.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Use structural induction on $e$. Constants remain one-node constants. A variable becomes $\sigma_i$, which has size $1$ by hypothesis. At addition and multiplication nodes, substitution preserves the root and recursively substitutes into both children; the inductive hypotheses therefore preserve $1+|a|+|b|$. At exponential and logarithm nodes, the root is preserved and the child size is preserved, giving $1+|a|$. $\square$

The theorem identifies a conserved quantity. Leaf parameters may change real values or select input coordinates, but they cannot alter the number or arrangement of operation nodes in the template.

## 6. Exponential towers and the finite-template obstruction

For each $k\in\mathbb{N}$, define an expression $E_k$ recursively by

$$
E_0=0,
\qquad
E_{k+1}=\exp(E_k).
$$

The ambient variable arity is arbitrary because these constant expressions ignore all inputs.

### Lemma 6.1 (Size of exponential towers)

For every $k\in\mathbb{N}$,

$$
|E_k|=k+1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The base expression $E_0=0$ is one constant node. If $|E_k|=k+1$, then $E_{k+1}$ adds one exponential root, so

$$
|E_{k+1}|=1+|E_k|=1+(k+1)=(k+1)+1.
$$

Induction completes the proof. $\square$

### Theorem 6.2 (No universal finite template under parameter sharing)

For every finite EML template $T$ and every target arity $n$, the template $T$ is not universal by parameter sharing for $\mathcal{E}_n$.

**Proof sketch.** Let $s=|T|$ and choose the target $E_s\in\mathcal{E}_n$. By Lemma 6.1,

$$
|E_s|=s+1.
$$

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that a leaf substitution $\sigma$ satisfies $T[\sigma]=E_s$. By Theorem 5.3,

$$
|T[\sigma]|=|T|=s.
$$

Equality of expressions implies equality of their sizes, so the assumed equality would yield $s=s+1$, impossible. Therefore no such substitution exists. Since $T$ was arbitrary, no finite template has the claimed universality. $\square$

### Corollary 6.3 (Parameter sharing does not establish currying)

A construction that represents every finite EML expression by specializing only the leaves of one fixed finite EML evaluator cannot supply the universal evaluator required for categorical exponentials.

**Proof sketch.** Such a construction would make its evaluator template universal by parameter sharing, contradicting Theorem 6.2. $\square$

The corollary rejects a mechanism, not every possible outcome. A different representation of morphisms might carry expression codes, closures, or another form of unbounded structure. Alternatively, an extensional category might identify many trees that denote the same function. Those alternatives require distinct definitions and new universal-property proofs.

## 7. Algorithms and computational complexity

The structural results admit simple executable counterparts useful for experimentation and testing.

### 7.1. Evaluation

To evaluate an expression, recursively visit its tree. A constant returns its stored value, a variable reads one coordinate, binary nodes recursively evaluate two children, and unary nodes recursively evaluate one child before applying the operation.

If arithmetic and elementary transcendental operations are treated as unit-cost primitives, evaluation takes $O(|e|)$ time. A recursive implementation uses $O(h(e))$ stack space, where $h(e)$ is tree height. Bit complexity depends on the number representation and desired precision and is not captured by the unit-cost model.

### 7.2. Substitution

A direct tree-substitution algorithm traverses the template and copies the chosen replacement tree whenever it encounters a variable. If replacement trees have arbitrary sizes, output size can grow substantially because repeated variable occurrences duplicate replacements. A safe output-sensitive bound is

$$
O(|e[\sigma]|)
$$

time and space for constructing the explicit output tree, assuming constant-time access to replacement roots and accounting for copied nodes. Under leaf substitution, Theorem 5.3 gives $|e[\sigma]|=|e|$, so time and output space are $O(|e|)$.

### 7.3. Obstruction certificate

Given a candidate template $T$, compute $s=|T|$ and return $E_s$. The result is a concrete witness that the template is not universal under leaf substitution. Computing $s$ takes $O(|T|)$ time. Constructing $E_s$ takes $O(s)$ time and space. The entire certificate procedure is therefore linear in template size.

The witness is canonical relative to the chosen grammar: it uses only the constant $0$ and exponential nodes. It does not depend on the number of parameters, the parameter values, or numerical evaluation.

### 7.4. Pairing and composition

Pairing tuples by concatenation is $O(m+k)$ if output lists are copied, or constant time with a persistent concatenation view. Explicit composition requires substituting the $m$ coordinate expressions of $f$ into each of the $k$ expressions of $g$. Its cost is output-sensitive and may exceed the sum of input sizes because substitution can duplicate subtrees. Directed acyclic graph representations with explicit sharing can reduce duplication, but they change the structural size invariant and must be analyzed separately.

## 8. Applications and interpretation

### 8.1. Fixed architecture versus unbounded architecture

The obstruction expresses a general modeling principle. Parameters vary labels within a fixed computational graph. They do not add nodes to that graph. This distinction appears in parameterized scientific models, fixed-topology circuits, and neural architectures: weights may range over a continuum while the architecture remains finite and fixed.

Consequently, cardinality alone is misleading. The real numbers can encode vast amounts of information under unconstrained set-theoretic encodings, but an EML parameter leaf is not accompanied by a decoding interpreter. The allowed operations merely place that leaf inside a fixed finite tree. Universality must be demonstrated by an evaluator in the language, not inferred from the cardinality of the parameter space.

### 8.2. Compositional scientific computing

The positive category laws justify modular assembly. A preprocessing program may be substituted into a downstream model without leaving the language. The denotation of the resulting symbolic program equals ordinary functional composition. Multiple observables can be paired into one output vector, and this bundling commutes with preprocessing. These equations support algebraic optimization and modular semantics.

### 8.3. Limits of the intensional conclusion

Tree size is not an invariant of denoted functions. The expressions $x$ and $x+0$ have different sizes but agree pointwise. Similarly, algebraic identities and inverse relationships between logarithm and exponential can collapse distinct trees on appropriate domains. Therefore Theorem 6.2 does not descend automatically to a quotient by functional equality.

An extensional non-universality theorem would need to show that no fixed finite EML family, parameterized in the allowed manner, denotes every EML-definable function. Potential invariants include differential-algebraic complexity, transcendence degree, bounds on zeros or oscillations, and model-theoretic bounds on definable families. Each route must address the precise semantics of logarithm and the allowed parameter domain.

### 8.4. Natural numbers are a separate universal property

The real line does contain the real numbers $0,1,2,\ldots$, and the map $x\mapsto x+1$ is an EML expression. This does not make $\mathbb{R}$ a natural-numbers object. Such an object $N$ requires maps

$$
0:1\to N,
\qquad
s:N\to N
$$

with the property that, for every object $A$, every point $a:1\to A$, and every endomorphism $f:A\to A$, there is a unique map $u:N\to A$ satisfying

$$
u\circ 0=a,
\qquad
u\circ s=f\circ u.
$$

For $N=\mathbb{R}$, this asks for a globally defined EML map solving every such recursion and for uniqueness on all real inputs, not only on nonnegative integers. Constants and addition alone do not establish that universal property.

## 9. Discussion and future work

The finite EML syntax supports exact substitution, associative composition, identities, and tuple pairing compatible with composition. These are robust positive results. The finite-template obstruction then identifies the precise failure in a proposed higher-order argument: leaf parameter sharing preserves tree size, while the language contains expressions of arbitrarily large size.

Several directions remain.

First, the choice between intensional and extensional morphisms must be explicit. For intensional morphisms, the obstruction applies directly. For extensional morphisms, one must construct the quotient by pointwise equality, prove composition is well defined, and seek semantic invariants.

Second, any Cartesian-closed proposal must specify candidate exponential objects within the object class. If objects remain only $\mathbb{R}^n$, a finite-dimensional object must somehow represent the relevant family of maps, and an EML evaluation morphism must satisfy the universal currying bijection. Parameter counting or informal coding is not a substitute for that universal property.

Third, finite products should be completed by defining projections and proving the beta, eta, and uniqueness equations. These are expected to follow from coordinate manipulation, but stating them removes ambiguity about the categorical structure obtained.

Fourth, the natural-numbers-object proposal should be tested directly against its recursion property. A multisorted category with a discrete natural-number sort, or a category of represented spaces, may be more plausible than using all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Fifth, richer morphism representations deserve study. Typed closures, inductive syntax codes with an interpreter, partial maps, or graph-based programs with explicit sharing could support genuine higher-order behavior. Each enrichment changes the category. In particular, introducing codes is useful only if decoding or evaluation is itself an admitted morphism and if currying satisfies the required existence, uniqueness, and naturality equations.

Finally, logarithm semantics must match the intended application. A totalized logarithm gives total real functions but may not reflect ordinary scientific usage. Partial logarithm suggests categories of partial maps or domain-certified expressions. This semantic decision affects extensional claims, though not the finite-tree argument.

## 10. Conclusion

Finite EML programs form a coherent intensional category under simultaneous substitution. Their numerical interpretation respects composition, identities satisfy the expected laws, composition is associative, and tuple pairing is stable under precomposition. These results place first-order modular computation on a clear structural foundation.

Higher-order universality requires more than parameters. Every leaf specialization of a fixed finite template has the same abstract-syntax-tree size as that template, while iterated exponentials provide expressions of arbitrarily large size. Hence no fixed finite EML template can generate every expression solely by sharing leaf parameters. Currying and categorical exponentials cannot be obtained from that proposal.

The central distinction is between changing data and changing program structure. A future Cartesian-closed account must represent the latter explicitly or prove a stronger semantic universality theorem. The finite-template obstruction marks the point at which a first-order language must be enriched before it can support genuine higher-order computation.

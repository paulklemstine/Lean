## Abstract

The Berggren tree organises the primitive Pythagorean triples into an infinite ternary
tree, rooted at $(3,4,5)$, in which each node has exactly three children given by three
fixed unimodular matrices and each primitive triple occurs exactly once. Working in the
Euclid seed coordinates $(m,n)$, $m>n>0$, and embedding each node in the hyperbolic upper
half-plane at $z(m,n)=(n+i)/m$, we determine the metric growth of the tree completely.

We first prove a *window lemma*: $\log m\le d(i,z(m,n))\le \log m+\log 2$, so hyperbolic
displacement equals the logarithm of the larger seed coordinate to within an additive
$\log 2$. We then introduce the *silver potential* $\Phi(m,n)=m+(\sqrt2-1)n$, show that it
is multiplied by exactly $\lambda=1+\sqrt2$ by the middle Berggren move and strictly
contracted, with explicit slack, by the other two, and deduce that the maximum of $\Phi$
over depth $k$ is exactly $\lambda^{k+1}$, attained by the pure-middle (Pell) spine and by
no other node — indeed any other depth-$k$ node satisfies $\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2$.
The Pell spine dominates every depth-$k$ node coordinatewise, hence simultaneously
maximises seed, hypotenuse and potential, uniquely; Binet formulas give the sharp
asymptotic constant $\max_{\text{depth }k} m/\lambda^{k+1}\to(2+\sqrt2)/4=0.853553\ldots$.

Consequently the metric growth exponent of the tree is exactly $\log(1+\sqrt2)=0.881373\ldots$,
strictly between $\log 2$ and $\log 3$. We then study the *spectrum* of realised rates
$\lim_k d(i,z_k)/k$ along infinite paths. We refute the natural conjecture that the rate is
a function of the asymptotic frequency of the expanding move: the two periodic paths
$(B_2B_3)^\infty$ and $(B_2B_2B_3B_3)^\infty$, neither of which uses the move $B_1$, both
have middle-move frequency $1/2$ but have the distinct exact rates $\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt5)=0.721817\ldots$
and $\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt3)=0.658479\ldots$. Positively, we exhibit a two-parameter family
of periodic paths $(B_2^aB_3^b)^\infty$, $a$ odd, whose exact rate is
$\log\sigma(a,b)/(a+b)$ with $\sigma(a,b)=(T+\sqrt{T^2+4})/2$ and
$T(a,b)=P_{a+1}+2bP_a+P_{a-1}$ a Pell trace, and prove that these rates are dense in
$[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$. Combined with the universal ceiling, the closure of the metric growth
spectrum is exactly $[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$, and it is realised entirely without the move
$B_1$. Finally we translate the geometry into arithmetic: a node of hypotenuse $\ge N$ has
depth $\ge(\log N-\log 2)/(2\log(1+\sqrt2))-1$, the Pell spine achieves depth
$\le(\log N+\log 2)/(2\log(1+\sqrt2))$, and two nodes sharing a hypotenuse $N$ have depth
sum at least $(\log N-\log 2)/\log(1+\sqrt2)-2$.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, silver ratio, Pell numbers, hyperbolic
geometry, growth spectrum, Birkhoff averages, continued fractions.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The tree

A Pythagorean triple $(a,b,c)$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ is *primitive* if $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. In
1934 B. Berggren observed — and the fact has been rediscovered repeatedly since — that the
primitive triples form an infinite ternary tree with root $(3,4,5)$, generated by three
fixed integer matrices, in which every primitive triple appears exactly once.

The tree is most transparent in Euclid coordinates. Every primitive triple is
$$(a,b,c)=(m^2-n^2,\;2mn,\;m^2+n^2)$$
for a unique *seed* $(m,n)$ with $m>n>0$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$, $m\not\equiv n \pmod 2$. We call
such a pair a seed and write $\mathrm{hyp}(m,n)=m^2+n^2$ for the hypotenuse. The root is
$(2,1)$. The three Berggren moves become
$$B_1(m,n)=(2m-n,\,m),\qquad B_2(m,n)=(2m+n,\,m),\qquad B_3(m,n)=(m+2n,\,n).$$
Each preserves seedhood, and every seed is reached from $(2,1)$ by a unique finite word in
$\{B_1,B_2,B_3\}$; the length of that word is the *depth* of the node. We write $w$ for a
word, $|w|$ for its length, $\#B_2(w)$ for the number of middle letters, and
$\mathrm{run}(w)$ for the seed reached by applying the letters of $w$ to $(2,1)$.

In matrix form, acting on the column $(m,n)^{\mathsf T}$,
$$B_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
B_2=M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
B_3=R=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
All three lie in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb Z)$; $\det B_1=\det M=-1$, $\det R=+1$.

### 1.2 The question

Because the tree is ternary, the number of nodes at depth $k$ is $3^k$; a naive count
suggests that "size" should grow like $3^k$. It does not. The purpose of this paper is to
determine, exactly and with sharp constants, how fast the tree grows in the natural
geometry attached to it, and to determine the set of all growth rates realised along
infinite paths.

The natural geometry is hyperbolic. The three moves are elements of the modular group and
hence isometries of the hyperbolic plane, so a walk down the tree is a walk in a
negatively curved space, and the correct notion of "how far you have got" is hyperbolic
distance from a base point.

### 1.3 Results

Throughout $\lambda=1+\sqrt2$ denotes the *silver ratio*, the positive root of
$x^2=2x+1$, and $\bar\lambda=1-\sqrt2$ its conjugate; $P_0=0,P_1=1,P_{k+2}=2P_{k+1}+P_k$
are the Pell numbers, so that $P_k=(\lambda^k-\bar\lambda^k)/(2\sqrt2)$.

1. **(Window Lemma, §2.)** $\log m\le d(i,z(m,n))\le \log m+\log 2$.
2. **(Silver potential and the speed limit, §3.)** $\Phi=m+(\sqrt2-1)n$ satisfies
   $\Phi\circ B_2=\lambda\Phi$ exactly, $\Phi\circ B_1\le\lambda\Phi-2$,
   $\Phi\circ B_3\le\lambda\Phi-\sqrt2$; hence $\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}$ at depth $k$ and
   $d\le(k+1)\log\lambda+\log 2$.
3. **(Extremal rigidity, §4.)** The Pell spine dominates coordinatewise at every depth; it
   is the unique maximiser of the seed, the hypotenuse and the potential; off the spine
   $\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2$; and $\max m/\lambda^{k+1}\to(2+\sqrt2)/4$.
4. **(Sharp two-sided word estimate, §5.)**
   $(\#B_2(w)+1)\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2\le d\le(|w|+1)\log\lambda+\log2$.
5. **(Frequency does not determine rate, §6.)** $(B_2B_3)^\infty$ and
   $(B_2B_2B_3B_3)^\infty$ have middle-frequency $1/2$ and exact rates
   $\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt5)\ne\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt3)$.
6. **(A two-parameter family of exact rates, §7.)** For odd $a$ and all $b$,
   $(B_2^aB_3^b)^\infty$ has exact rate $\log\sigma(a,b)/(a+b)$.
7. **(Density and the closure of the spectrum, §8.)** The realised rates are dense in
   $[0,\log\lambda]$, no realised rate exceeds $\log\lambda$, and the closure of the
   spectrum is exactly $[0,\log\lambda]$.
8. **(Arithmetic consequences, §9.)** Minimal depth for hypotenuse $N$ is
   $\log N/(2\log\lambda)+O(1)$; hypotenuse collisions cost depth sum
   $\ge (\log N-\log2)/\log\lambda-2$.

## 2. The hyperbolic window

Let $\mathbb H=\{x+iy:y>0\}$ carry the hyperbolic metric $ds=|dz|/y$, with base point $i$
and the standard distance formula
$$\cosh d(i,x+iy)=\frac{x^2+y^2+1}{2y}.$$

**Definition 2.1.** To a seed $(m,n)$ with $0<n<m$ associate the point
$$z(m,n)=\frac{n+i}{m}\in\mathbb H,$$
and set $d(m,n)=d\bigl(i,z(m,n)\bigr)$.

**Theorem 2.2 (Window Lemma).** *For every seed $(m,n)$ with $0<n<m$,*
$$\log m\;\le\;d(m,n)\;\le\;\log m+\log 2 .$$

*Proof.* Here $x=n/m$, $y=1/m$, so
$\cosh d = \frac{n^2/m^2+1/m^2+1}{2/m}=\frac{m^2+n^2+1}{2m}$.
Since $n\ge 0$, $\cosh d\ge\frac{m^2+1}{2m}=\cosh(\log m)$, giving the lower bound. For the
upper bound, $n\le m-1$ gives $2n^2+1\le 2(m-1)^2+1\le 2m^2$, i.e.
$2(m^2+n^2+1)\le 4m^2+1$, i.e.
$\cosh d=\frac{m^2+n^2+1}{2m}\le\frac{4m^2+1}{4m}=\cosh(\log 2m)$. Monotonicity of
$\cosh$ on $[0,\infty)$ finishes both directions. $\square$

Theorem 2.2 is the bridge of the paper: **hyperbolic displacement is $\log m$ up to an
absolute additive error $\log 2$.** Every metric statement below is obtained by proving an
arithmetic statement about $m$ and quoting Theorem 2.2. For a word $w$ we abbreviate
$$D(w) := d\bigl(\mathrm{run}(w)\bigr),\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\log m(w)\le D(w)\le \log m(w)+\log 2,$$
where $m(w)$ is the first coordinate of $\mathrm{run}(w)$.

**Remark 2.3.** The choice of the first coordinate $m$, rather than (say) $\tfrac12\log c$
with $c=m^2+n^2$, is essential: $m$ is the coordinate on which the Berggren moves act
linearly with the cleanest constants, and it is what makes the potential-function argument
of §3 available.

## 3. The silver potential and the universal speed limit

Under the moves, the *slope* $x=m/n$ transforms by three Möbius maps:
$$B_1:x\mapsto 2-\tfrac1x,\qquad B_2:x\mapsto 2+\tfrac1x,\qquad B_3:x\mapsto x+2 .$$
$B_1$ is parabolic with fixed point $x=1$; $B_3$ is parabolic with fixed point $x=\infty$;
$B_2$ has the attracting hyperbolic fixed point $x=1+\sqrt2=\lambda$ (whose continued
fraction $[2;2,2,\ldots]$ is precisely the itinerary "always $B_2$"). Only $B_2$ expands
asymptotically, and its expansion factor is $\lambda$. The potential below encodes this in
a form suitable for induction.

**Definition 3.1.** The *silver potential* of a pair $(m,n)$ of nonnegative reals is
$$\Phi(m,n)=m+(\sqrt2-1)\,n .$$

**Lemma 3.2 (Exact expansion by the middle move).** *For all $m,n$,*
$$\Phi\bigl(B_2(m,n)\bigr)=\lambda\,\Phi(m,n).$$

*Proof.* $\Phi(2m+n,m)=2m+n+(\sqrt2-1)m=(1+\sqrt2)m+n$, while
$\lambda\Phi(m,n)=(1+\sqrt2)m+(1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1)n=(1+\sqrt2)m+n$, using
$(1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1)=1$. $\square$

The identity $(\sqrt2-1)=\lambda^{-1}$ is exactly why the weight $\sqrt2-1$ is the right
one: $\Phi$ is the linear functional dual to the top eigenvector of $M$, so $M$ scales it
by the top eigenvalue.

**Lemma 3.3 (Strict contraction by the outer moves).** *For every seed $(m,n)$ with
$0<n<m$,*
$$\Phi\bigl(B_1(m,n)\bigr)\le\lambda\,\Phi(m,n)-2,\qquad
\Phi\bigl(B_3(m,n)\bigr)\le\lambda\,\Phi(m,n)-\sqrt2 .$$

*Proof.* For $B_1$: $\Phi(2m-n,m)=(1+\sqrt2)m-n$, so
$\lambda\Phi(m,n)-\Phi(B_1(m,n))=2n\ge 2$ since $n\ge1$. For $B_3$:
$\Phi(m+2n,n)=m+(1+\sqrt2)n$, while $\lambda\Phi(m,n)=(1+\sqrt2)m+n$, so the difference is
$\sqrt2\,(m-n)\ge\sqrt2$ since $m\ge n+1$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Silver bound at depth $k$).** *Every node at depth $k$ satisfies
$\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}$.*

*Proof.* Induction on $k$. At the root, $\Phi(2,1)=2+\sqrt2-1=1+\sqrt2=\lambda$. If a
depth-$k$ node has $\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}$, then by Lemmas 3.2–3.3 each child has
$\Phi\le\lambda\cdot\lambda^{k+1}=\lambda^{k+2}$. $\square$

Since $m\le\Phi(m,n)$, Theorems 2.2 and 3.4 combine:

**Corollary 3.5 (The silver speed limit).** *Every node at depth $k$ satisfies*
$$d\;\le\;(k+1)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log 2 .$$

**Definition 3.6 (Pell spine).** The *Pell spine* is the pure-$B_2$ path; its depth-$k$
node is $\mathrm{sp}(k)=B_2^{\,k}(2,1)$, with coordinates
$(2,1),(5,2),(12,5),(29,12),(70,29),\dots$

**Proposition 3.7 (Binet formulas for the spine).** $\mathrm{sp}(k)=(P_{k+2},P_{k+1})$,
i.e.
$$m_k=\frac{\lambda^{k+2}-\bar\lambda^{\,k+2}}{2\sqrt2},\qquad
n_k=\frac{\lambda^{k+1}-\bar\lambda^{\,k+1}}{2\sqrt2}.$$

*Proof.* $B_2(m,n)=(2m+n,m)$ is exactly the Pell recursion with a shift, and the base case
$(2,1)=(P_2,P_1)$ holds. The closed forms follow because $\lambda,\bar\lambda$ are the
roots of $x^2=2x+1$; the induction step reduces to
$\lambda^{k+3}=2\lambda^{k+2}+\lambda^{k+1}$ and its conjugate. $\square$

**Proposition 3.8 (The spine attains the bound).** $\Phi(\mathrm{sp}(k))=\lambda^{k+1}$,
and consequently $d(\mathrm{sp}(k))\ge (k+1)\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2$.

*Proof.* The first claim is Lemma 3.2 iterated from $\Phi(2,1)=\lambda$. For the second,
$\Phi=m+(\sqrt2-1)n\le m+(\sqrt2-1)m<\sqrt2\,m$ for a seed with $n<m$, hence
$m>\lambda^{k+1}/\sqrt2$ and Theorem 2.2 gives
$d\ge\log m\ge(k+1)\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.9 (Metric growth exponent).** *The metric growth exponent of the Berggren
tree is exactly $\log(1+\sqrt2)=0.881373\ldots$: no depth-$k$ node exceeds
$(k+1)\log\lambda+\log2$, and the Pell spine attains
$(k+1)\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2$; hence $d(\mathrm{sp}(k))/k\to\log\lambda$ and every path
has $\limsup_k d/k\le\log\lambda$.*

Note $\log 2=0.693<\log\lambda=0.881<\log 3=1.099$: the ternary tree is metrically slower
than its branching number, and strictly faster than binary.

## 4. Extremal rigidity at fixed depth

Theorem 3.4 bounds a linear functional. In fact the spine dominates in a much stronger,
coordinatewise sense.

**Theorem 4.1 (Coordinatewise domination).** *If a node $(m,n)$ has depth $k$, then
$m\le m_k$ and $n\le n_k$.*

*Proof.* Induction on $k$; the root case is an equality. Suppose $(m,n)$ has depth $k$ with
$m\le m_k$, $n\le n_k$, and recall $m_{k+1}=2m_k+n_k$, $n_{k+1}=m_k$ and $n_k<m_k$. Then:
$B_2$ gives $(2m+n,m)$ with $2m+n\le 2m_k+n_k=m_{k+1}$ and $m\le m_k=n_{k+1}$;
$B_1$ gives $(2m-n,m)$ with $2m-n\le 2m_k\le m_{k+1}$ and $m\le n_{k+1}$;
$B_3$ gives $(m+2n,n)$ with $m+2n\le m_k+2n_k\le 2m_k+n_k=m_{k+1}$ (using $n_k<m_k$) and
$n\le n_k<m_k=n_{k+1}$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2.** *At depth $k$ the maximum of the first seed coordinate is $m_k$ and the
maximum hypotenuse is $m_k^2+n_k^2$, both attained on the Pell spine.*

**Theorem 4.3 (Uniqueness of the maximiser).** *If a depth-$k$ node has first coordinate
$m_k$, it is $\mathrm{sp}(k)$; likewise for the hypotenuse.*

*Proof sketch.* Examine the last letter of the word. If it is $B_2$, applied to a parent
$(m,n)$ of depth $k-1$, then $2m+n=2m_{k-1}+n_{k-1}$ with $m\le m_{k-1}$, $n\le n_{k-1}$
forces $m=m_{k-1}$, $n=n_{k-1}$, and induction applies. If the last letter is $B_1$, then
$2m-n\le 2m_{k-1}-1<2m_{k-1}+n_{k-1}$ because $n\ge1$ and $n_{k-1}\ge1$; if it is $B_3$,
then $m+2n\le m_{k-1}+2n_{k-1}<2m_{k-1}+n_{k-1}$ because $n_{k-1}<m_{k-1}$. Either way the
value is strictly below $m_k$. For the hypotenuse, coordinatewise domination makes
$m^2+n^2=m_k^2+n_k^2$ force $m=m_k$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.4 (Quantitative gap).** *If a depth-$k$ node is not $\mathrm{sp}(k)$, then*
$$\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2 .$$
*Hence $\max_{\text{depth }k}\Phi=\lambda^{k+1}$ with a unique maximiser, and $\Phi$ equals
$\lambda^{k+1}$ if and only if the node lies on the Pell spine.*

*Proof.* Induction on the last letter. If the last letter is $B_1$ or $B_3$, apply Lemma
3.3 to the parent together with the unconditional bound $\Phi(\text{parent})\le\lambda^k$:
$\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}-2$ resp. $\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2$. If the last letter is $B_2$, the
parent is not the spine node of depth $k-1$ (else the child would be $\mathrm{sp}(k)$), so
inductively $\Phi(\text{parent})\le\lambda^{k}-\sqrt2$, and Lemma 3.2 gives
$\Phi\le\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2\,\lambda\le\lambda^{k+1}-\sqrt2$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.5 (Sharp asymptotic constant).**
$$\frac{\max_{\text{depth }k}m}{\lambda^{k+1}}=\frac{m_k}{\lambda^{k+1}}
=\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}-\frac{\bar\lambda^{2}}{2\sqrt2\,\lambda}\Bigl(\frac{\bar\lambda}{\lambda}\Bigr)^{k}
\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}=0.853553\ldots$$
*In particular the constant is **not** $1/\sqrt2=0.707106\ldots$, which one obtains by
substituting the crude estimate $\Phi<\sqrt2\,m$ of Proposition 3.8; the true constant is
strictly larger.*

*Proof.* Substitute the Binet formula of Proposition 3.7 and factor out $\lambda^{k+1}$;
the constant term is $\lambda/(2\sqrt2)=(2+\sqrt2)/4$, and the error term decays
geometrically because $|\bar\lambda/\lambda|=(\sqrt2-1)^2<1$. $\square$

## 5. A sharp two-sided estimate in terms of the word

Theorem 3.4 is an upper bound driven by depth. The matching lower bound is driven by the
number of middle letters, and with the *same* constant.

**Lemma 5.1 (Monotonicity of $\Phi$ under all moves).** *For every seed,
$\Phi(m,n)\le\Phi(B_1(m,n))$ and $\Phi(m,n)\le\Phi(B_3(m,n))$.*

*Proof.* $\Phi(B_1(m,n))-\Phi(m,n)=(2m-n)+(\sqrt2-1)m-m-(\sqrt2-1)n=\sqrt2(m-n)\ge0$;
$\Phi(B_3(m,n))-\Phi(m,n)=2n-(\sqrt2-1)n=(3-\sqrt2)n\ge0$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Middle-move lower bound).** *For every word $w$,*
$$\lambda^{\,\#B_2(w)+1}\le \Phi(\mathrm{run}(w)),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\bigl(\#B_2(w)+1\bigr)\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2\;\le\;D(w).$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.2 each middle letter multiplies $\Phi$ by $\lambda$, and by Lemma 5.1
the other letters never decrease it; starting from $\Phi(2,1)=\lambda$ gives the first
inequality. Since $\Phi<\sqrt2\,m$ for a seed, $m>\lambda^{\#B_2+1}/\sqrt2$, and
Theorem 2.2 converts this into the distance bound. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Sharp two-sided word estimate).** *For every word $w$,*
$$\bigl(\#B_2(w)+1\bigr)\log(1+\sqrt2)-\tfrac12\log 2\;\le\;D(w)\;\le\;
\bigl(|w|+1\bigr)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log 2 .$$

Both constants are $\log\lambda$, so the estimate is tight to $O(1)$ for words that are
(almost) all middle letters — quantifying the slogan that *the middle move is the only
universal expander, and a positive growth rate is forced exactly by a positive density of
middle letters*. Applied to the periodic word $(B_2^aB_3)^j$ it gives rate at least
$\frac{a}{a+1}\log\lambda-O(1/j)$, so letting $a\to\infty$ the realised rates accumulate
at the top of the spectrum.

## 6. The frequency of the expanding move does not determine the rate

Since only $B_2$ expands, one is tempted to conjecture that the asymptotic rate along an
infinite $B_1$-free path is a continuous, strictly increasing function of the asymptotic
frequency of $B_2$. This section refutes that.

Define, for an infinite path presented as a nested family of words $w_j$ with
$|w_j|\to\infty$, the *rate* $\lim_j D(w_j)/|w_j|$ when it exists.

**Lemma 6.1 (Geometric criterion).** *Let $(w_j)$ be words with $|w_j|=pj$, and suppose
there are constants $0<c_1\le c_2$ and $\Lambda>1$ with $c_1\Lambda^j\le m(w_j)\le
c_2\Lambda^j$ for all $j$. Then*
$$\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{D(w_j)}{|w_j|}=\frac{\log\Lambda}{p}.$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 2.2, $\log m(w_j)\le D(w_j)\le\log m(w_j)+\log 2$, so
$\frac{\log c_1+j\log\Lambda}{pj}\le\frac{D(w_j)}{pj}\le
\frac{\log c_2+j\log\Lambda+\log2}{pj}$, and both bounds tend to $\log\Lambda/p$. $\square$

This criterion reduces every rate computation to a two-sided geometric bound on the first
seed coordinate, which for a periodic path is a linear recurrence and hence a Binet
expansion.

**Theorem 6.2 (Two rates at frequency $1/2$).** *Consider the $B_1$-free periodic paths*
$$P_A=(B_2B_3)^\infty,\qquad P_B=(B_2B_2B_3B_3)^\infty .$$
*Both have middle-move frequency exactly $1/2$ at every period. Their rates exist and are*
$$\mathrm{rate}(P_A)=\tfrac12\log\bigl(2+\sqrt5\bigr)=0.7218175\ldots,\qquad
\mathrm{rate}(P_B)=\tfrac14\log\bigl(7+4\sqrt3\bigr)=\tfrac12\log\bigl(2+\sqrt3\bigr)=0.6584789\ldots,$$
*which are distinct. Hence the rate of a $B_1$-free path is not a function of the
asymptotic frequency of $B_2$.*

*Proof.* The period matrices are $RM=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and
$R^2M^2=\begin{pmatrix}13&6\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$. For $P_A$ the seed coordinates satisfy
$m_{j+1}=4m_j+n_j$, $n_{j+1}=m_j$, hence $m_{j+1}=4m_j+m_{j-1}$ with characteristic roots
$\alpha=2+\sqrt5$, $\beta=2-\sqrt5$, and the initial data $(2,1)$ give the closed form
$m_j=\tfrac12(\alpha^{j+1}+\beta^{j+1})$. Since $|\beta|=\sqrt5-2<1$ this is squeezed
between $c_1\alpha^j$ and $c_2\alpha^j$; Lemma 6.1 with $p=2$ yields the rate
$\tfrac12\log\alpha$.

For $P_B$ the recursion is $m_{j+1}=13m_j+6n_j$, $n_{j+1}=2m_j+n_j$, with characteristic
roots $\gamma=7+4\sqrt3$ and $\delta=7-4\sqrt3\in(0,1)$ (the trace is $14$ and the
determinant $1$). The explicit solution is
$m_j=(1+\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4})\gamma^j+(1-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{4})\delta^j$, again geometrically
sandwiched, and Lemma 6.1 with $p=4$ yields $\tfrac14\log\gamma$. Finally
$\gamma=(2+\sqrt3)^2$, so $\tfrac14\log\gamma=\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt3)$, and
$2+\sqrt3<2+\sqrt5$ gives the strict inequality. A cruder separation is available with no
Binet expansion at all: $m_j\ge 2\cdot4^j$ along $P_A$ gives rate $\ge\log 2$, while the
self-improving slope invariant $6n\le m$ along $P_B$ gives $m_j\le 32\cdot14^{j}$ and
rate $\le\tfrac14\log 14<\log 2$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.3.** *The spectrum contains at least the four values
$0<\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt3)<\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt5)<\log(1+\sqrt2)$, two of them interior.*

**Interpretation.** By Theorem 2.2, $D/|w|$ is a Birkhoff average of
$\log(\text{expansion})$ along the slope dynamics $x\mapsto B_i x$. The move $B_3$ is not
merely non-expanding — it *transports the slope away from the attracting fixed point*
$\lambda$ of $B_2$, so that the following $B_2$ earns less than its nominal factor.
Clustering the $B_3$'s (as in $P_B$) inflicts more total damage than spreading them out
(as in $P_A$). Frequency is blind to arrangement; the rate is not.

## 7. A two-parameter family of exact rates

**Definition 7.1.** For $a\ge1$, $b\ge0$, let $G_{a,b}=(B_2^aB_3^b)^\infty$, presented by
the words $w_j=(B_2^aB_3^b)^j$ of length $(a+b)j$ with $\#B_2(w_j)=aj$. The middle-move
frequency is $a/(a+b)$.

**Lemma 7.2 (Pell powers).** $M^a=\begin{pmatrix}P_{a+1}&P_a\\P_a&P_{a-1}\end{pmatrix}$,
with the convention $P_{-1}=1$.

*Proof.* Induction using $M\cdot M^a$ and $P_{k+2}=2P_{k+1}+P_k$. $\square$

**Lemma 7.3 (Cassini).** $P_{a+1}P_{a-1}-P_a^2=(-1)^a$. Consequently $\det M^a=(-1)^a$,
which is $-1$ exactly when $a$ is odd.

**Proposition 7.4 (The period recurrence).** *Let $a$ be odd and set*
$$T(a,b)=P_{a+1}+2bP_a+P_{a-1}=\operatorname{tr}\bigl(M^aR^b\bigr).$$
*Then $\det(M^aR^b)=-1$, so by Cayley–Hamilton the first seed coordinate along $G_{a,b}$
satisfies the integer two-term recurrence*
$$x_{j+2}=T(a,b)\,x_{j+1}+x_j .$$

*Proof.* $\det R=1$, so $\det(M^aR^b)=\det M^a=-1$ by Lemma 7.3. A $2\times2$ matrix $S$
with $\operatorname{tr}S=T$, $\det S=-1$ satisfies $S^2=TS+I$; applying this to the vector
of coordinates at period $j$ gives the recurrence. Explicitly,
$M^aR^b=\begin{pmatrix}P_{a+1}&2bP_{a+1}+P_a\\P_a&2bP_a+P_{a-1}\end{pmatrix}$, whose trace
is $T(a,b)$. $\square$

**Definition 7.5.** For $a$ odd put
$$\sigma(a,b)=\frac{T(a,b)+\sqrt{T(a,b)^2+4}}{2},\qquad
r(a,b)=\frac{\log\sigma(a,b)}{a+b}.$$
Thus $\sigma$ is the dominant root of $x^2=Tx+1$; note $T<\sigma<T+1$ for $T\ge1$.

**Theorem 7.6 (Exact rate of $G_{a,b}$).** *For odd $a$ and every $b$, the path $G_{a,b}$
never uses $B_1$, has middle-move frequency $a/(a+b)$, and has the exact rate*
$$\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{D(w_j)}{|w_j|}=r(a,b)=\frac{\log\sigma(a,b)}{a+b}>0 .$$

*Proof.* The sandwich $\sigma^j\le m(w_j)\le 3\sigma^j$ follows by induction on $j$ from the
recurrence of Proposition 7.4 together with the identity $\sigma^2=T\sigma+1$ and the
initial values $m(w_0)=2$ and $m(w_1)=(2+2b)P_{a+1}+P_a$, obtained by applying
$M^aR^b$ to the root vector $(2,1)^{\mathsf T}$; the two base cases are the inequalities
$1\le 2\le 3$ and $\sigma\le m(w_1)\le 3\sigma$, and the induction step is
immediate from monotonicity of the recurrence in both arguments. Lemma 6.1 with $p=a+b$ and
$\Lambda=\sigma$ gives the limit; positivity holds because $T\ge1$ implies $\sigma>1$.
$\square$

**Theorem 7.7 (The family attains the maximum).** *For every odd $a$,*
$$\sigma(a,0)=(1+\sqrt2)^a,\qquad\text{hence}\qquad r(a,0)=\log(1+\sqrt2).$$

*Proof.* $T(a,0)=P_{a+1}+P_{a-1}=\operatorname{tr}M^a=\lambda^a+\bar\lambda^a$ by the Binet
formula for Pell numbers. For odd $a$, $\bar\lambda^a=-\lambda^{-a}$, so
$T=\lambda^a-\lambda^{-a}$ and $T^2+4=(\lambda^a+\lambda^{-a})^2$, whence
$\sigma=\frac{(\lambda^a-\lambda^{-a})+(\lambda^a+\lambda^{-a})}{2}=\lambda^a$. Dividing by
$a+0$ gives $\log\lambda$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.8 (Descent to zero, in small steps).** *For odd $a$:*

1. $T(a,b)+1\le 3^{\,a+b+2}$, hence $r(a,b)\le 3\log 3$ and, more usefully,
   $\log\sigma(a,b)\le (a+b+2)\log 3$;
2. $r(a,b)\to 0$ as $b\to\infty$ for fixed $a$ (indeed $\sigma(a,b)=O(b)$ while the period
   length $a+b\to\infty$);
3. *consecutive rates are close:* $|r(a,b)-r(a,b+1)|\le \dfrac{3\log 3}{a+b+1}$.

*Proof sketch.* (1) $P_n\le 3^n$ by induction and $2b+5\le 9\cdot 3^b$; then
$T(a,b)+1\le 3^{a+b+2}$ and $\sigma<T+1$. (2) $T(a,b)$ grows linearly in $b$ while $a+b$
grows linearly, so $\log\sigma/(a+b)=O(\log b/b)\to 0$. (3) $T$ and hence $\sigma$ are
nondecreasing in $b$, so $r(a,b)$ and $r(a,b+1)$ are both between
$\frac{\log\sigma(a,b)}{a+b+1}$ and $\frac{\log\sigma(a,b+1)}{a+b}$; bounding numerators by
(1) and denominators below by $a+b$ gives the stated gap. $\square$

Examples ($a=1$): $T(1,b)=2+2b$ and $\sigma(1,b)=(1+b)+\sqrt{(1+b)^2+1}$, so the path
$(B_2B_3^b)^\infty$ has the exact rate $\log\bigl((1+b)+\sqrt{(1+b)^2+1}\bigr)/(b+1)$. For
$b=0$ this is $\log(1+\sqrt2)$; for $b=1$, $\tfrac12\log(2+\sqrt5)$, recovering Theorem 6.2;
and the rates decrease to $0$.

## 8. Density and the closure of the spectrum

**Definition 8.1.** The *metric growth spectrum* of the Berggren tree is the set of all
$R$ arising as $\lim_j D(w_j)/|w_j|$ along families of words with $|w_j|\to\infty$.

**Theorem 8.2 (Universal ceiling).** *If $|w_j|\to\infty$ and $D(w_j)/|w_j|\to R$, then
$R\le\log(1+\sqrt2)$.*

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.3, $D(w_j)\le(|w_j|+1)\log\lambda+\log2$, so
$D(w_j)/|w_j|\le\log\lambda+(\log\lambda+\log2)/|w_j|$, whose limit is $\log\lambda$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (Density).** *For every $r\in[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$ and every $\varepsilon>0$
there are an odd $a$ and a $b\ge0$ such that the $B_1$-free periodic path $G_{a,b}$ has the
exact rate $r(a,b)$ and $|r(a,b)-r|<\varepsilon$.*

*Proof.* Choose $K\in\mathbb N$ with $K>3\log3/\varepsilon$ and set $a=2K+1$, which is odd
and $\ge K$. By Theorem 7.8(3) all consecutive gaps satisfy
$|r(a,b)-r(a,b+1)|\le\frac{3\log3}{a+b+1}\le\frac{3\log3}{K}<\varepsilon$.

If some $b$ has $r(a,b)<r$, let $b_0$ be the least such. Since $r(a,0)=\log\lambda\ge r$
(Theorem 7.7), $b_0\ge1$; write $b_0=c+1$. Minimality gives $r(a,c)\ge r$, and
$r(a,c+1)<r$; the gap bound gives $r(a,c+1)>r(a,c)-\varepsilon\ge r-\varepsilon$. Hence
$|r(a,b_0)-r|<\varepsilon$.

Otherwise $r(a,b)\ge r$ for all $b$; since $r(a,b)\to0$ (Theorem 7.8(2)) this forces
$r\le0$, so $r=0$, and any $b$ with $0<r(a,b)<\varepsilon$ works. $\square$

**Theorem 8.4 (Closure of the spectrum).** *The closure of the metric growth spectrum of
the Berggren tree is exactly $[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$. Moreover:*

* *every realised rate lies in $[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$;*
* *the maximum $\log(1+\sqrt2)$ is attained, by the Pell spine and more generally by
  $G_{a,0}$ for every odd $a$;*
* *the whole interval is approximated by explicit $B_1$-free periodic paths, whose
  middle-move frequency is $a/(a+b)$ and whose rates are the explicit quadratic-surd
  logarithms $\log\sigma(a,b)/(a+b)$.*

The last point deserves emphasis: the parabolic move $B_1$ is *dispensable*. The full range
of metric behaviour of the tree, from stagnation to the silver limit, is already visible in
the sub-semigroup generated by $B_2$ and $B_3$.

**Remark 8.5 (Where the rate comes from).** Under the Window Lemma, $D/|w|$ is a Birkhoff
average of the logarithmic expansion of the slope cocycle. The three moves contribute a
piecewise-smooth cocycle with two parabolic branches (expansion $\to$ $0$ near their fixed
points $1$ and $\infty$) and one hyperbolic branch of maximal expansion $\lambda$ at
$x=1+\sqrt2$. The spectrum of Birkhoff averages of such a cocycle is an interval whose top
is realised by the fixed point of the hyperbolic branch — precisely what Theorems 8.2–8.4
prove in this case, by elementary means and with explicit witnesses.

## 9. Arithmetic consequences: depth versus hypotenuse

**Theorem 9.1 (Depth lower bound from the hypotenuse).** *If a node of depth $k$ has
hypotenuse at least $N\ge1$, then*
$$k\;\ge\;\frac{\log N-\log 2}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)}-1 .$$

*Proof.* For a seed, $N\le m^2+n^2<2m^2$, so $m>\sqrt{N/2}$. By Theorem 3.4 and
$m\le\Phi$, $m\le\lambda^{k+1}$; taking logarithms,
$\tfrac12(\log N-\log2)\le(k+1)\log\lambda$. $\square$

**Theorem 9.2 (Matching upper bound).** *The Pell spine reaches hypotenuse $\ge N$ at depth
at most $\dfrac{\log N+\log 2}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)}$. Hence the minimal depth at which
hypotenuse $N$ appears in the tree is*
$$\frac{\log N}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)}+O(1).$$

*Proof sketch.* On the spine $m_k>\lambda^{k+1}/\sqrt2$ and $\mathrm{hyp}\ge m_k^2$, so
$\mathrm{hyp}\ge\lambda^{2k+2}/2$; solving $\lambda^{2k+2}/2\ge N$ for $k$ gives the
bound. $\square$

**Theorem 9.3 (Collisions cost depth).** *If two nodes of depths $k$ and $l$ both have
hypotenuse at least $N\ge1$ — in particular if they are two distinct nodes realising the
same hypotenuse $N$ — then*
$$k+l\;\ge\;\frac{\log N-\log 2}{\log(1+\sqrt2)}-2\;=\;\frac{\log N}{\log(1+\sqrt2)}-O(1).$$

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 9.1 to each node and add:
$k+l\ge 2\bigl(\frac{\log N-\log2}{2\log\lambda}-1\bigr)=\frac{\log N-\log2}{\log\lambda}-2$.
$\square$

Hypotenuse collisions are exactly the integers $N$ with at least two essentially different
representations as a sum of two coprime squares, i.e. those with at least two distinct
prime factors $\equiv1\pmod 4$; the smallest is $65=1^2+8^2=4^2+7^2$. Theorem 9.3 says such
a coincidence is never cheap: both participants must sit deep in the tree, with total depth
at least $\log N/\log(1+\sqrt2)-2$. This is the tree-level form of the "short certificate,
expensive search" phenomenon for sums of two squares: an address is short
($O(\log N)$ letters), but the tree offers no shortcut to finding one, and lopsided
representations $N=m^2+n^2$ with $n$ small require long parabolic $B_3$-runs, since the
slope $n/m$ must be driven towards $0$ one unit of $2n$ at a time.

## 10. Algorithms

Three procedures underlie the computational side of the results.

**A. Descent to the address of a triple.** Given a seed $(m,n)$ with $m>n>0$, the parent is
determined by comparing the slope $x=m/n$ against the two walls $2$ and $3$ produced by
inverting the three moves:
$B_1^{-1}(m,n)=(n,2n-m)$, $B_2^{-1}(m,n)=(n,m-2n)$, $B_3^{-1}(m,n)=(m-2n,n)$.
Exactly one of these is again a seed, so the address is computed by a Euclidean-style
descent in $O(\log \mathrm{hyp})$ steps with $O(1)$ arithmetic per step. Theorem 9.1
guarantees that the address length is $\ge\log N/(2\log\lambda)-O(1)$, and Theorem 5.3 that
it is $O(\log N)$; the descent is therefore optimal up to constants.

**B. Exact rate of a periodic path.** Given a period word, form the product of the
corresponding matrices, take its trace $T$ and determinant $\Delta\in\{\pm1\}$, and return
$\log\sigma/p$ where $\sigma$ is the dominant root of $x^2-Tx-\Delta=0$ and $p$ is the
period length. Correctness is Lemma 6.1: the Binet expansion of the linear recurrence gives
a two-sided geometric bound on $m$, and the Window Lemma converts it to a distance
statement. Cost: $O(p)$ matrix multiplications.

**C. Rational approximation of a prescribed rate.** Given $r\in[0,\log\lambda]$ and
$\varepsilon>0$, set $a=2K+1$ with $K>3\log3/\varepsilon$ and scan $b=0,1,2,\dots$ until
$r(a,b)<r$ (or until $r(a,b)<\varepsilon$ if $r=0$); return the last two candidates and pick
the closer. Theorem 8.3 guarantees termination and the error bound. Cost: $O(b^\ast)$
evaluations of $T(a,b)$, each $O(1)$ after precomputing the Pell numbers $P_{a\pm1},P_a$.

## 11. Discussion

Three features of this picture are worth isolating.

*The right coordinate.* Measuring position by $\tfrac12\log c$, with $c$ the hypotenuse, is
natural but analytically inferior: the Berggren moves do not act linearly on it with clean
constants. The first seed coordinate $m$ does transform linearly, and the Window Lemma
shows it is metrically equivalent to hyperbolic displacement, up to $\log 2$. All the sharp
constants of this paper — $\log(1+\sqrt2)$, $\sqrt2$, $(2+\sqrt2)/4$, $\tfrac12\log2$ —
become available only in that coordinate.

*The right functional.* The silver potential $\Phi=m+\lambda^{-1}n$ is the linear functional
dual to the dominant eigenvector of $M$. It is exactly multiplicative under $B_2$ and
strictly submultiplicative under $B_1,B_3$, with slacks $2n$ and $\sqrt2(m-n)$ that are
bounded below by absolute constants. That pair of facts — exact equality on one generator,
uniform slack on the others — is what upgrades an upper bound to a rigidity statement:
unique maximiser, quantitative gap, and a sharp asymptotic constant.

*The failure of frequency.* The most instructive negative result is Theorem 6.2. It is easy
to believe that in a system with one expanding generator, the exponential rate should be
proportional to how often that generator fires. That belief is a *commutative*
approximation, and it is false here by a computable margin: two paths with identical letter
statistics differ in rate by $0.0633\ldots$. Non-commutativity of the generators is not a
technicality; it is the phenomenon. The correct statement, Theorem 5.3, brackets the rate
between the middle-frequency and $1$ times $\log\lambda$, and both ends are attained.

Finally, the arithmetic corollaries in §9 are the practical payoff. They say that the tree
is an exponentially efficient generator of large primitive triples — depth
$\log N/(2\log\lambda)+O(1)$ suffices for hypotenuse $N$, and nothing does better — while
simultaneously charging a definite depth cost for every hypotenuse collision.

## 12. Future work

1. **Is the spectrum closed?** Density is established; we conjecture that every
   $r\in[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$ is attained *exactly*, by a (generally aperiodic) $B_1$-free
   path. The natural construction concatenates blocks $(B_2^{a}B_3^{b})^{N_i}$ with slowly
   varying parameters, and the difficulty is controlling the transitions so that the
   Birkhoff average converges rather than oscillating.
2. **Multifractal structure.** For each $r$, what is the Hausdorff dimension of the set of
   boundary points of the tree whose rate equals $r$? The expected answer is a concave
   spectrum peaking below the top rate, as for Birkhoff averages of cocycles with parabolic
   branches.
3. **Aperiodic and random paths.** What is the rate of a path whose letters are chosen
   i.i.d. uniformly from $\{B_1,B_2,B_3\}$? This is the top Lyapunov exponent of a random
   product of the three matrices; Theorem 5.3 gives the bracket
   $\tfrac13\log\lambda\le r\le\log\lambda$ almost surely, and computing it exactly (or even
   proving it irrational) is open.
4. **Collisions in closed form.** Explicit families of colliding pairs, e.g. seeds
   $(20j+9,10j+2)$ and $(20j+7,10j+6)$, have slopes tending to the walls $1/2$ and $1/3$ of
   the parent trichotomy; their depths should be computable in closed form and compared
   against the bound of Theorem 9.3, which we expect to be sharp up to the additive
   constant.
5. **Other trees.** The same method — a hyperbolic window, a dominant-eigenvector potential,
   a two-parameter periodic family — should determine the growth spectra of the Barning /
   Hall variants and of the Stern–Brocot and Calkin–Wilf trees, where the analogue of the
   silver ratio is the golden ratio.

## Abstract

The ternary tree of primitive Pythagorean triples, generated from $(3,4,5)$ by the three classical Berggren moves, and the Markoff tree of positive integer solutions of $x^2+y^2+z^2=3xyz$, generated from $(1,1,1)$ by the Vieta involutions, are both canonical, both grow out of a single root, both are governed by involutions preserving a quadratic-type equation, and both carry an evident hyperbolic-geometric flavour. It is natural to conjecture that they are isomorphic as generated trees, which would transport the well-developed Pythagorean machinery — a free monoid action, a Lorentz-group representation, and an exact growth theory — to the Markoff side, where the uniqueness conjecture of Frobenius remains open.

We prove that no such global transfer exists, and we localise the failure into three independent obstructions of distinct mathematical types.

1. **Combinatorial.** Level $n$ of the Pythagorean tree carries exactly $3^n$ nodes and level $n$ of the Markoff tree exactly $2^n$; more strongly, *no* injective map carrying the three children of the Pythagorean root to children of the image of the root exists, so the obstruction is purely local at the first branch point.
2. **Arithmetic.** The Pythagorean growth spine obeys $u_{n+2}=6u_{n+1}-u_n$ (characteristic polynomial $X^2-6X+1$, silver ratio) and the Markoff spine obeys $s_{n+2}=3s_{n+1}-s_n$ (characteristic polynomial $X^2-3X+1$, golden ratio). The transfer matrices are not conjugate over $\mathbb{Q}$, and sharply, $\sqrt5 \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$: the two growth rates do not live in a common real quadratic field.
3. **Algebraic.** The Berggren moves are integral $O(2,1)$ matrices; by contrast no rational $3\times3$ matrix implements the Vieta move $(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,3xy-z)$ on the Markoff surface. The Markoff dynamics is irreducibly quadratic.

We then isolate what does transfer. The free-monoid combinatorics transfers at rank $2$: the Markoff tree embeds injectively and depth-preservingly into the Pythagorean tree. More substantially, the Vieta dynamics restricted to a fixed smallest coordinate $x$ is *linear*, given by the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&3x\end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ of trace $3x$, hyperbolic for all $x\ge1$. The fibre over $x=1$ is exactly the set of consecutive odd-index Fibonacci pairs, and the fibre over $x=2$ is exactly the set of consecutive hypotenuses along the Pythagorean silver spine $5,29,169,985,5741,\dots$ — so the Pythagorean spine literally *is* a Markoff branch. Finally, we prove a middle-entry rigidity theorem and deduce that the Markoff uniqueness conjecture is equivalent to the statement that the maximum of a triple determines its minimum, i.e. that distinct fibres never share a maximum.

**Keywords:** Markoff triples, Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, Vieta involution, Markoff uniqueness conjecture, silver ratio, golden ratio, $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, Vieta jumping.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Two generated trees

Number theory contains a small number of *tree theorems*: statements asserting that all solutions of a Diophantine equation are generated from a single root by an explicit finite set of moves, each solution appearing exactly once. Two of the most celebrated concern the equations
$$a^2+b^2=c^2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad x^2+y^2+z^2=3xyz .$$

The first is Berggren's theorem (1934): every primitive Pythagorean triple is obtained from $(3,4,5)$ by a unique word in three explicit unimodular matrices. The resulting structure is an infinite *ternary* tree.

The second is Markoff's theorem (1879, 1880): every positive integer solution of $x^2+y^2+z^2=3xyz$ is obtained from $(1,1,1)$ by Vieta involutions and permutations. Above the three small singular triples $(1,1,1),(1,1,2),(1,2,5)$, the resulting structure is an infinite *binary* tree.

The similarities are striking enough to invite a conjecture. Both trees are canonical objects attached to a homogeneous-looking equation; both are generated by involutions; both have a natural embedding into hyperbolic geometry (the Pythagorean side via the Lorentz form $a^2+b^2-c^2$ and the null cone; the Markoff side via the character variety of the once-punctured torus and the modular surface); and both possess a distinguished spine of maximal growth associated with a quadratic irrationality — the silver ratio $1+\sqrt2$ on the Pythagorean side and the golden ratio $\varphi$ on the Markoff side.

### 1.2 The hypothesis under test

The hypothesis this work tests is that the two trees are isomorphic as generated trees — that there is a birational or integral transfer map carrying the Pythagorean tree onto the Markoff tree and preserving branching. If true, the mature Pythagorean machinery could be imported to the Markoff side and applied to Frobenius's uniqueness conjecture:

> **Conjecture (Frobenius, 1913; open).** An ordered Markoff triple $x \le y \le z$ is determined by its largest entry $z$.

Our results settle the hypothesis negatively, with three independent obstructions, and then extract the exact residue of truth: a fibrewise transfer that is genuinely new and that reformulates the uniqueness conjecture.

Section 5 gives the three obstructions. Section 6 gives the positive transfer results: the rank-$2$ embedding, fibrewise linearity, and the exact classification of the fibres over $x=1$ and $x=2$. Section 7 gives arithmetic consequences and the reduction of the uniqueness conjecture. Sections 8–9 discuss applications and future directions.

## 2. Setting and notation

### 2.1 The Pythagorean side

Primitive Pythagorean triples are parametrised by pairs of integers $(m,n)$ with $m>n>0$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$, $m \not\equiv n \pmod 2$, via
$$a = m^2-n^2, \qquad b = 2mn, \qquad c = m^2+n^2 .$$
Call such a pair **valid**. The root pair is $r_0 = (2,1)$, corresponding to $(3,4,5)$.

**Definition 2.1 (Berggren moves).** The three generators $A, B, C$ act on pairs by
$$A(m,n) = (2m-n,\, m), \qquad B(m,n) = (2m+n,\, m), \qquad C(m,n) = (m+2n,\, n).$$

Each preserves validity, each is injective, and — the content of Berggren's theorem in this model — every valid pair other than $r_0$ has a *unique* parent, i.e. is $g(p)$ for exactly one generator $g$ and one valid pair $p$. Consequently the map from words to pairs,
$$\mathrm{ev}: \{A,B,C\}^{*} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}^2, \qquad \mathrm{ev}(g_1 g_2 \cdots g_k) = g_1(g_2(\cdots g_k(r_0))),$$
is a bijection onto the set of valid pairs. We write $L_n^{\mathrm{P}}$ for the set of pairs obtained from words of length $n$ ("Pythagorean level $n$").

From $r_0=(2,1)$ the three children are $(3,2),(5,2),(4,1)$, i.e. the triples $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$, $(15,8,17)$.

### 2.2 The Markoff side

**Definition 2.2.** The **Markoff form** is $\mu(x,y,z) = x^2+y^2+z^2-3xyz$, and $(x,y,z)$ is a **Markoff point** if $\mu(x,y,z)=0$. A **Markoff triple** is a Markoff point with all coordinates positive integers.

$\mu$ is symmetric under all permutations of the coordinates. Since $\mu$ has degree $2$ in each variable separately, each coordinate admits a *Vieta involution*.

**Definition 2.3 (Vieta involution).** $V(x,y,z) = (x, y, 3xy - z)$.

**Lemma 2.4.** $V$ is an involution, it preserves the Markoff surface, and the two roots satisfy $z\cdot(3xy-z) = x^2+y^2$.

*Proof.* $3xy-(3xy-z)=z$ gives involutivity. Writing $\mu(x,y,z)=0$ as $z^2-3xyz+(x^2+y^2)=0$, the number $3xy-z$ is the second root of this monic quadratic, hence also a solution; the product of the roots is the constant term $x^2+y^2$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.5 (positivity).** If $(x,y,z)$ is a Markoff point with $x>0$ and $z>0$, then $3xy-z>0$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2.4, $z(3xy-z)=x^2+y^2 \ge x^2 > 0$; since $z>0$ the other factor is positive. $\square$

## 3. The Markoff tree theorem by descent

**Definition 3.1 (reachability).** Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the smallest set of integer triples containing $(1,1,1)$ and closed under $V$ and under the transpositions of coordinates.

**Lemma 3.2 (rigidity at the top).** If $(x,y,y)$ is a Markoff point with $0<x\le y$, then $x=y=1$.

*Proof.* The equation reads $x^2+2y^2 = 3xy^2$, i.e. $x^2 = y^2(3x-2)$. Since $y \ge x > 0$ we have $y^2(3x-2) \ge x^2(3x-2)$, so $x^2 \ge x^2(3x-2)$ and therefore $3x-2 \le 1$, i.e. $x = 1$. Substituting, $1 = y^2$, so $y=1$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.3.** Every ordered Markoff triple $0<x\le y\le z$ other than $(1,1,1)$ has $y<z$.

**Theorem 3.4 (descent bound).** Let $0<x\le y<z$ be an ordered Markoff triple. Then $3xy-z \le y$.

*Proof.* Put $w = 3xy-z$. Expanding and using $\mu=0$ one obtains the identity
$$(y-z)(y-w) = x^2 + 2y^2 - 3xy^2 .$$
For $1 \le x \le y$ the right side is $x^2 + y^2(2-3x) \le y^2 + y^2(2-3x) = y^2(3-3x) \le 0$. Since $y-z<0$, we conclude $y-w \ge 0$. $\square$

Thus for an ordered non-root triple the Vieta move applied to the *largest* coordinate strictly decreases the sum. Strong induction on $x+y+z$ then yields:

**Theorem 3.5 (Markoff tree theorem).** Every triple of positive integers on the Markoff surface lies in $\mathcal{R}$; that is, it is obtained from $(1,1,1)$ by a finite sequence of Vieta involutions and coordinate permutations.

*Proof sketch.* Given $0<x\le y\le z$ with $\mu=0$: if it is $(1,1,1)$ we are done. Otherwise $y<z$ by Corollary 3.3; set $w=3xy-z$. By Lemma 2.5, $w>0$; by Theorem 3.4, $w\le y$. Hence $(x,y,w)$ is a positive Markoff point with strictly smaller sum. Re-sort it and apply the induction hypothesis to obtain $(x,y,w) \in \mathcal{R}$; then $V(x,y,w)=(x,y,z)$ puts $(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{R}$. Unordered triples are handled by permuting first. $\square$

## 4. Freeness and level counts

### 4.1 The Markoff tree is a free binary tree

Above the singular triples the tree branches exactly twice. Formalise this as follows.

**Definition 4.1 (node).** A triple $t=(x,y,z)$ is a **node** if $0<x<y<z$, $z\ge5$, and $\mu(t)=0$. The **root node** is $t_0=(1,2,5)$.

**Definition 4.2 (children).** For a node $t=(x,y,z)$ set
$$\mathrm{ch}_L(t) = (x,\; z,\; 3xz-y), \qquad \mathrm{ch}_R(t) = (y,\; z,\; 3yz-x),$$
i.e. the Vieta move in the middle and in the first coordinate respectively, with the result re-sorted.

**Proposition 4.3.** If $t$ is a node then $\mathrm{ch}_L(t)$ and $\mathrm{ch}_R(t)$ are nodes, and both have strictly larger top entry than $t$.

*Proof.* Both are Markoff points by Lemma 2.4 after the appropriate transposition. Ordering: from $x\ge1$, $y<z$ we get $3xz - y \ge 3z - y > z$, and similarly $3yz-x \ge 3\cdot2\cdot z - x > z$; the lower-bound $z \ge 5$ propagates. $\square$

**Definition 4.4 (descent map).** For a triple $t=(x,y,z)$ let
$$\pi(t) = \begin{cases} (3xy-z,\; x,\; y) & \text{if } 3xy - z \le x,\\ (x,\; 3xy-z,\; y) & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

**Theorem 4.5 (unique parent).** For every node $t$, $\pi(\mathrm{ch}_L(t)) = t$ and $\pi(\mathrm{ch}_R(t)) = t$, and $\mathrm{ch}_L(s) \ne \mathrm{ch}_R(t)$ for all nodes $s,t$.

*Proof.* Apply $\pi$ to $\mathrm{ch}_L(t) = (x,z,3xz-y)$: the Vieta value is $3\cdot x\cdot z - (3xz-y) = y$, and since $y > x$ the second branch of $\pi$ applies, returning $(x,y,z)=t$. Similarly for $\mathrm{ch}_R(t)=(y,z,3yz-x)$ the Vieta value is $x$, and $x<y$ selects the first branch, returning $(x,y,z)$. If $\mathrm{ch}_L(s)=\mathrm{ch}_R(t)$ then applying $\pi$ gives $s=t$; but $\mathrm{ch}_L(t)$ and $\mathrm{ch}_R(t)$ have first coordinates $x<y$, so they differ. $\square$

**Definition 4.6.** Let $\mathrm{mev}:\{L,R\}^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^3$ evaluate a binary word at the root node, $\mathrm{mev}(\varepsilon)=t_0$ and $\mathrm{mev}(b\,w) = \mathrm{ch}_b(\mathrm{mev}(w))$.

**Theorem 4.7 (freeness).** $\mathrm{mev}$ is injective. Equivalently, the Markoff tree is a free binary tree of rank $2$.

*Proof.* Induction on word length. A child always has top entry strictly greater than its parent's, and the parent's top entry is at least $5$; hence no child equals $t_0$, which settles the base case. For the inductive step, if $\mathrm{ch}_{b_1}(\mathrm{mev}(w_1)) = \mathrm{ch}_{b_2}(\mathrm{mev}(w_2))$ then $b_1 = b_2$ by the disjointness in Theorem 4.5, and then $\mathrm{mev}(w_1)=\mathrm{mev}(w_2)$ by applying $\pi$; conclude by induction. $\square$

**Corollary 4.8 (Markoff level count).** Level $n$ of the Markoff tree, $L_n^{\mathrm{M}} = \{\mathrm{mev}(w) : |w| = n\}$, satisfies
$$\bigl|L_n^{\mathrm{M}}\bigr| = 2^n .$$

*Proof.* $L_{n+1}^{\mathrm{M}} = \mathrm{ch}_L(L_n^{\mathrm{M}}) \sqcup \mathrm{ch}_R(L_n^{\mathrm{M}})$, both images injective on nodes and disjoint by Theorem 4.5. $\square$

### 4.2 The Pythagorean tree is a free ternary tree

**Theorem 4.9 (Pythagorean level count).** $\bigl|L_n^{\mathrm{P}}\bigr| = 3^n$.

*Proof.* $L_{n+1}^{\mathrm{P}}$ is the union of the three images $A(L_n^{\mathrm{P}}), B(L_n^{\mathrm{P}}), C(L_n^{\mathrm{P}})$. Each generator is injective; the three images are pairwise disjoint because a valid pair determines the generator producing it (uniqueness of parents). Hence $|L_{n+1}^{\mathrm{P}}| = 3\,|L_n^{\mathrm{P}}|$ and $|L_0^{\mathrm{P}}|=1$. $\square$

## 5. Three obstructions to a global transfer

### 5.1 The combinatorial obstruction

**Corollary 5.1.** For every $n \ge 1$, $\bigl|L_n^{\mathrm{M}}\bigr| = 2^n < 3^n = \bigl|L_n^{\mathrm{P}}\bigr|$. Consequently there is no injection of Pythagorean level $n$ into Markoff level $n$, and in particular no level-preserving tree isomorphism.

The stronger statement dispenses with levels entirely and is purely local.

**Theorem 5.2 (branching obstruction).** Let $f$ be any injective map from pairs to triples, and suppose that for each Berggren generator $g \in \{A,B,C\}$ there is a bit $b_g\in\{L,R\}$ with
$$f\bigl(g(r_0)\bigr) = \mathrm{ch}_{b_g}\bigl(f(r_0)\bigr).$$
Then a contradiction follows. Hence no branching-compatible injection of the Pythagorean tree into the Markoff tree exists, even locally at the root.

*Proof.* The three children $A(r_0)=(3,2)$, $B(r_0)=(5,2)$, $C(r_0)=(4,1)$ are pairwise distinct. The three bits $b_A,b_B,b_C$ take values in a two-element set, so two of them coincide, say $b_A = b_B$. Then $f(A(r_0)) = \mathrm{ch}_{b_A}(f(r_0)) = \mathrm{ch}_{b_B}(f(r_0)) = f(B(r_0))$, and injectivity of $f$ forces $A(r_0)=B(r_0)$, a contradiction. $\square$

Note that no compatibility of $f$ with roots, orders, or forms is required: three children cannot be injected into two slots. This is the exact failure point of the conjectured transfer.

### 5.2 The metric obstruction

**Definition 5.3 (Pythagorean spine).** Let $p_0=r_0=(2,1)$ and $p_{n+1}=B(p_n)$, and let $u_n = m_n^2 + n_n^2$ be the hypotenuse of the triple attached to $p_n=(m_n,n_n)$.

**Proposition 5.4.** $u_0=5$, $u_1=29$, $u_2=169$, and $u_{n+2}=6u_{n+1}-u_n$ for all $n$. The characteristic polynomial is $X^2-6X+1$, with roots $3\pm2\sqrt2 = (1\pm\sqrt2)^2$.

*Proof.* Write $p_n=(m,n)$. Then $p_{n+1}=(2m+n,m)$ and $p_{n+2}=(4m+2n+m, 2m+n)=(5m+2n,2m+n)$. Hence
$$u_{n+2} = (5m+2n)^2+(2m+n)^2 = 29m^2+24mn+5n^2,$$
$$6u_{n+1}-u_n = 6\bigl[(2m+n)^2+m^2\bigr] - (m^2+n^2) = 6(5m^2+4mn+n^2)-(m^2+n^2)=29m^2+24mn+5n^2 . \square$$

**Definition 5.5 (Markoff spine).** Let $s_0=s_1=1$ and $s_{n+2}=3s_{n+1}-s_n$, giving $1,1,2,5,13,34,89,\dots$

**Proposition 5.6.** $(1, s_n, s_{n+1})$ is a Markoff point for every $n$, and $s_{n+1} = F_{2n+1}$, the odd-index Fibonacci numbers.

*Proof.* For the first claim, $(1,1,1)$ is a Markoff point, and the Vieta move in the last coordinate of $(1,s_{n+1},s_n)$ produces $3s_{n+1}-s_n=s_{n+2}$. For the second, the Fibonacci identity $F_{k+4}+F_k=3F_{k+2}$ (obtained by three applications of $F_{j+2}=F_j+F_{j+1}$) shows the odd-index subsequence satisfies the same recursion with the same initial data. $\square$

**Theorem 5.7 (metric obstruction, linear form).** Let
$$M_{\mathrm{gold}} = \begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad M_{\mathrm{silv}} = \begin{pmatrix}6&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$$
be the transfer matrices of the two spine recursions, with characteristic polynomials $X^2-3X+1$ and $X^2-6X+1$. There is no invertible $T$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ with $T M_{\mathrm{silv}} = M_{\mathrm{gold}} T$.

*Proof.* Such a $T$ gives $M_{\mathrm{gold}} = T M_{\mathrm{silv}} T^{-1}$, and trace is conjugation-invariant; but $\operatorname{tr} M_{\mathrm{gold}} = 3 \ne 6 = \operatorname{tr} M_{\mathrm{silv}}$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.8 (metric obstruction, arithmetic form).** There are no rationals $a,b$ with $(a+b\sqrt2)^2 = 5$. Equivalently, $\sqrt5 \notin \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$.

*Proof.* Expanding, $(a^2+2b^2-5) + 2ab\sqrt2 = 0$. If $a=0$ then $2b^2=5$, so $(2b)^2=10$ and $\sqrt{10}\in\mathbb{Q}$, false. If $b=0$ then $a^2=5$ and $\sqrt5\in\mathbb{Q}$, false. Otherwise $2ab\ne0$ and $\sqrt2 = (5-a^2-2b^2)/(2ab) \in \mathbb{Q}$, false. $\square$

Thus the golden growth rate is not merely different from the silver one; it is not expressible in the quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ over which the Pythagorean growth theory is defined. The two metric halves live over discriminants $5$ and $8$.

### 5.3 The linearity obstruction

The Berggren moves act on triples $(a,b,c)$ by integral matrices preserving the Lorentz form $a^2+b^2-c^2$; this is precisely what allows the $O(2,1)$ and hyperbolic-geometric machinery to be brought to bear. The Markoff moves admit no such description.

**Theorem 5.9 (no linear model).** There is no matrix $A \in M_3(\mathbb{Q})$ such that for every Markoff point $(x,y,z)$ with integer entries,
$$A\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\3xy-z\end{pmatrix}.$$

*Proof.* Let $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)$ be the last row of $A$. Testing the four Markoff points $(1,1,1),(1,2,5),(1,5,13),(2,5,29)$ gives
$$\alpha+\beta+\gamma = 2, \quad \alpha+2\beta+5\gamma = 1, \quad \alpha+5\beta+13\gamma = 2, \quad 2\alpha+5\beta+29\gamma = 1.$$
Subtracting the first from the second gives $\beta+4\gamma=-1$; subtracting the second from the third gives $3\beta+8\gamma=1$; solving, $\gamma=-1$ and $\beta=3$, hence $\alpha=0$. But then the fourth equation reads $0+15-29 = -14 \ne 1$. The system is inconsistent. $\square$

The mechanism is transparent: the target coordinate contains the product $3xy$, and a linear functional cannot reproduce a product of coordinates on a Zariski-dense subset of a surface. The Markoff dynamics is irreducibly quadratic, so the Lorentz-linear half of the Pythagorean machinery has nothing to act on.

## 6. What does transfer

### 6.1 Freeness transfers at rank two

**Definition 6.1 (word transfer).** Define $\tau : \{L,R\}^{*} \to \{A,B,C\}^{*}$ by replacing each $L$ by $A$ and each $R$ by $B$.

**Proposition 6.2.** $\tau$ is injective and length-preserving, and $w \mapsto \mathrm{ev}(\tau(w))$ is injective.

*Proof.* $\tau$ is a letterwise substitution by an injective alphabet map, hence injective and length-preserving; composing with the injective evaluation $\mathrm{ev}$ preserves injectivity. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (positive transfer).** There is a map $\Phi$ from Markoff triples to Pythagorean pairs such that $\Phi(\mathrm{mev}(w)) = \mathrm{ev}(\tau(w))$ for every binary word $w$, and $w \mapsto \Phi(\mathrm{mev}(w))$ is injective. Moreover $\mathrm{ev}(\tau(w))$ lies in Pythagorean level $|w|$.

*Proof.* Since $\mathrm{mev}$ is injective (Theorem 4.7) it has a left inverse $\sigma$; set $\Phi = \mathrm{ev}\circ\tau\circ\sigma$. Then $\Phi(\mathrm{mev}(w)) = \mathrm{ev}(\tau(w))$, injective by Proposition 6.2. Depth preservation follows since $\tau$ preserves length. $\square$

So the free-monoid half of the Pythagorean machinery — the purely combinatorial half — does transfer: the Markoff tree sits inside the Pythagorean tree as the free rank-$2$ sub-tree spanned by two of the three generators. It can never be an isomorphism, by Theorem 5.2.

### 6.2 Fibrewise linearity

Order each Markoff triple as $x\le y\le z$ and call $x$ the **fibre index**. Within a fibre, the moves that fix $x$ are the ascending Vieta moves in the other two coordinates.

**Theorem 6.4 (fibrewise linearity).** Fix $x$. The ascending move $(y,z)\mapsto(z,\, 3xz-y)$ is the linear map given by
$$N_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&3x\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det N_x = 1, \qquad \operatorname{tr} N_x = 3x, \qquad \chi_{N_x}(X) = X^2 - 3x X + 1 .$$
For $x\ge1$ one has $\operatorname{tr} N_x = 3x > 2$, so $N_x$ is a hyperbolic element of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ and the fibre grows exponentially with ratio $\tfrac{3x+\sqrt{9x^2-4}}{2}$.

*Proof.* Direct computation: $N_x\binom{y}{z} = \binom{z}{\,3xz-y\,}$, which is the asserted move; $\det N_x = 0\cdot 3x - 1\cdot(-1) = 1$ and $\operatorname{tr} N_x = 3x$. For a $2\times2$ matrix, $\chi(X) = X^2 - (\operatorname{tr})X + \det$. Equivalently, the fibre entries obey the linear recursion $w_{n+2} = 3x\,w_{n+1} - w_n$. $\square$

This is the precise sense in which the *global* nonlinearity of Theorem 5.9 dissolves: the Markoff dynamics is quadratic across fibres but linear along each one.

**Corollary 6.5 (silver coincidence).** $\chi_{N_2}(X) = X^2 - 6X + 1 = \chi_{M_{\mathrm{silv}}}(X)$: the Markoff fibre over $x=2$ has exactly the characteristic polynomial of the Pythagorean silver spine. Likewise $\chi_{N_1}(X)=X^2-3X+1=\chi_{M_{\mathrm{gold}}}(X)$.

### 6.3 The golden branch is exactly the fibre over $x=1$

**Theorem 6.6 (exactness of the golden branch).** Let $1 \le y \le z$ with $\mu(1,y,z)=0$. Then there is an $n$ with $y=s_n$ and $z=s_{n+1}$, where $s$ is the golden spine $1,1,2,5,13,34,\dots$. Consequently, for $z \ge 1$,
$$\exists\, y:\ 1\le y\le z,\ \mu(1,y,z)=0 \iff z \in \{s_1, s_2, s_3, \dots\} = \{1,2,5,13,34,\dots\} = \{F_{2n+1}\}_{n\ge0}.$$

*Proof.* Strong induction on $z$. If $y=z$, Lemma 3.2 gives $y=z=1=(s_0,s_1)$. Otherwise $y<z$ and $w=3y-z$ satisfies $0<w\le y$ (Lemma 2.5, Theorem 3.4) with $\mu(1,w,y)=0$ and $y<z$; by induction $w=s_n$, $y=s_{n+1}$ for some $n$, whence $z = 3y-w = 3s_{n+1}-s_n = s_{n+2}$. $\square$

### 6.4 The silver branch is exactly the fibre over $x=2$, and it is the Pythagorean spine

**Lemma 6.7.** The only Markoff point of the form $(2,y,1)$ with $y\ge2$ is $y=5$.

*Proof.* $4+y^2+1 = 6y$ gives $y^2-6y+5=0$, i.e. $(y-5)(y-1)=0$; with $y\ge2$, $y=5$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.8 (transfer theorem).** For every $n$, $(2, u_n, u_{n+1})$ is a Markoff point, where $u$ is the Pythagorean spine hypotenuse sequence $5,29,169,985,5741,\dots$

*Proof.* $\mu(2,5,29) = 4+25+841 - 3\cdot2\cdot5\cdot29 = 870-870 = 0$. Inductively, the Vieta move in the last coordinate of $(2, u_{n+1}, u_n)$ gives $6u_{n+1}-u_n = u_{n+2}$ by Proposition 5.4, so $(2,u_{n+1},u_{n+2})$ is again a Markoff point. $\square$

**Theorem 6.9 (exactness).** Let $2 \le y \le z$ with $\mu(2,y,z)=0$. Then there is an $n$ with $y = u_n$ and $z = u_{n+1}$. Consequently
$$\{\,z : \exists\, y,\ 2\le y \le z,\ \mu(2,y,z)=0\,\} = \{u_1, u_2, u_3, \dots\} = \{29, 169, 985, 5741, \dots\}.$$

*Proof.* Strong induction on $z$. Equality $y=z$ is impossible: Lemma 3.2 would force the smallest entry to be $1$, not $2$. So $y<z$; put $w=6y-z$, which satisfies $0<w\le y$ and $\mu(2,w,y)=0$. If $w=1$ then Lemma 6.7 gives $y=5=u_0$ and $z=6\cdot5-1=29=u_1$. If $w\ge2$, induction gives $w=u_n$, $y=u_{n+1}$, and then $z = 6y-w = 6u_{n+1}-u_n = u_{n+2}$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.10.** The map $n \mapsto (2, u_n, u_{n+1})$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ onto the set of ordered Markoff triples with smallest entry $2$; in particular it is injective because $u$ is strictly increasing.

Theorems 6.8 and 6.9 together constitute the *exact* transfer: the Pythagorean silver spine is not analogous to a Markoff branch — it **is** the Markoff branch over $x=2$, hypotenuse for hypotenuse. This is the residue of the failed global conjecture, and it is a genuine bridge between the two structures.

## 7. Arithmetic consequences and the uniqueness conjecture

### 7.1 Invariants along the tree

**Lemma 7.1.** If $\gcd$-coprimality $\ (x,z)=1$ holds then $x$ and $3xy-z$ are coprime.

*Proof.* $3xy - z = -z + x(3y)$ is congruent to $-z$ modulo $x$; a common divisor of $x$ and $3xy-z$ divides $z$, hence divides $\gcd(x,z)=1$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (primitivity).** Every positive integer point of the Markoff surface has pairwise coprime coordinates.

*Proof.* The root $(1,1,1)$ is pairwise coprime. Coprimality is preserved by permutations and, by Lemma 7.1, by the Vieta move. By the tree theorem (Theorem 3.5) every positive point is reachable from the root, so the invariant holds everywhere. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (no coordinate divisible by three).** If $(x,y,z)$ is a Markoff triple then $3 \nmid x$.

*Proof.* If $3\mid x$, reduce $x^2+y^2+z^2=3xyz$ modulo $3$ to get $y^2+z^2\equiv0 \pmod 3$. The squares mod $3$ are $0$ and $1$, so $y\equiv z\equiv 0 \pmod 3$, contradicting Theorem 7.2. $\square$

**Theorem 7.4 (at most one even coordinate).** No two coordinates of a Markoff triple are both even.

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 7.2: two even coordinates share the factor $2$. $\square$

These are the transfers of the primitivity invariants of the Pythagorean tree, established by the same "invariant along the tree" method.

### 7.2 Middle-entry rigidity

**Theorem 7.5 (rigidity).** Let $x>0$, $0<y\le z$, $y'\le z$, and suppose $\mu(x,y,z)=\mu(x,y',z)=0$. Then $y=y'$.

*Proof.* Both $y,y'$ satisfy $t^2 - 3xz\,t + (x^2+z^2) = 0$. If $y \ne y'$ they are the two distinct roots, so $y+y' = 3xz$ and $y y' = x^2+z^2$. But $y,y'\le z$ gives $yy' \le z^2 < x^2+z^2$, a contradiction. $\square$

In words: **the smallest and largest entries of an ordered Markoff triple determine the middle entry.** Equivalently, inside a fixed fibre the largest entry determines the triple outright.

### 7.3 Reduction of the uniqueness conjecture

**Definition 7.6.** Let
- $(\mathrm{U})$: for all ordered Markoff triples $x\le y\le z$ and $x'\le y'\le z$ with the same $z$, one has $x=x'$ and $y=y'$;
- $(\mathrm{U}_{\min})$: same hypotheses, conclusion $x=x'$ only.

**Theorem 7.7 (reduction).** $(\mathrm{U}) \iff (\mathrm{U}_{\min})$.

*Proof.* $(\Rightarrow)$ is trivial. For $(\Leftarrow)$, suppose $(\mathrm{U}_{\min})$ and take two ordered triples with the same maximum $z$. Then $x=x'$, and Theorem 7.5 applied with this common smallest entry gives $y=y'$. $\square$

Combining Theorem 7.7 with the fibrewise picture of Section 6 yields the following reformulation, which we regard as the main structural output of this work on the open problem:

> **Reformulation.** The Markoff uniqueness conjecture is precisely the assertion that *the fibres do not collide*: for the one-parameter family of hyperbolic $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$-orbits $\{N_x^{k}\}$, $x = 1,2,5,13,\dots$, no maximum value occurs in two different fibres.

The fibres over $x=1$ and $x=2$ are completely determined (Theorems 6.6 and 6.9): the maxima occurring over $x=1$ are exactly the odd-index Fibonacci numbers $1,2,5,13,34,89,\dots$ (growth ratio $\varphi^2 \approx 2.618$), and the maxima occurring over $x=2$ are exactly the Pythagorean spine hypotenuses $29,169,985,5741,\dots$ (growth ratio $3+2\sqrt2 \approx 5.828$). Uniqueness restricted to these two fibres is therefore the concrete assertion that these two explicit sequences are disjoint — a statement about two classical recursions rather than about a tree.

## 8. Algorithms

Three algorithms organise the computational content.

**Tree enumeration by breadth-first Vieta expansion.** Maintain a frontier of ordered nodes, starting from $(1,2,5)$; for each node produce $\mathrm{ch}_L$ and $\mathrm{ch}_R$; after $n$ rounds the frontier is exactly level $n$, of size $2^n$. The same scheme with the three Berggren moves from $(2,1)$ produces $3^n$ nodes at depth $n$. Cost: $O(2^n)$ (resp. $O(3^n)$) node operations, with entries of bit-length $\Theta(2^n)$ in the worst case along the spine.

**Vieta descent (reduction to the root).** Given any positive solution, repeatedly sort the triple and replace the maximum $z$ by $3xy-z$. Theorem 3.4 guarantees the sum strictly decreases, so the process terminates at $(1,1,1)$, and the recorded moves give the word addressing the triple in the tree. The number of steps is $O(\log_{\varphi^2} z)$ in the golden fibre and $O(\log z)$ in general.

**Fibre generation by linear recursion.** For fixed $x$, generate the fibre by iterating $w_{n+2} = 3x\,w_{n+1} - w_n$ from the appropriate seed. This is $O(1)$ arithmetic operations per element and is the algorithmic form of Theorem 6.4.

## 9. Discussion

The hypothesis that the Pythagorean and Markoff trees are isomorphic is false, and its failure is now stratified.

- At the level of **combinatorics** the failure is total and trivial: $3 \neq 2$, obstructing even a local branching-compatible injection.
- At the level of **metric growth** the failure is arithmetic and more interesting: the two spines are governed by $X^2-6X+1$ and $X^2-3X+1$, whose splitting fields $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2)$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$ are distinct. No linear change of coordinates over $\mathbb{Q}$ — indeed no identification of growth rates in a common quadratic field — is possible.
- At the level of **group actions** the failure is structural: the Berggren moves are $O(2,1)$ matrices; the Vieta move is not induced by any matrix. This is the reason the hyperbolic-geometric machinery does not port, and it explains why the Markoff surface must be studied through the character variety of the once-punctured torus rather than through a linear null cone.

What survives is not nothing. Freeness — the combinatorial skeleton — transfers as a rank-$2$ sub-structure. Primitivity transfers, in the strong form of pairwise coprimality of all positive solutions. The descent method transfers verbatim and is what makes the tree theorem work. And the fibrewise analysis produces a genuinely new bridge: over each fixed smallest coordinate $x$ the Markoff dynamics is the hyperbolic $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ element of trace $3x$, and for $x=2$ this is exactly the Pythagorean silver spine, hypotenuse by hypotenuse.

The reduction of the uniqueness conjecture is, we believe, the most useful downstream consequence. Frobenius's problem is usually stated as a two-dimensional rigidity question about a tree. Theorems 7.5 and 7.7 collapse one dimension: the middle entry is never the issue. What remains is a question about a one-parameter family of second-order linear recursions — whether the sequences $\{w^{(x)}_n\}$ defined by $w_{n+2}=3x\,w_{n+1}-w_n$, indexed by the admissible smallest entries $x \in \{1,2,5,13,29,\dots\}$, are pairwise disjoint as sets of values. Two of these families are classical (Fibonacci, Pell) and their disjointness is elementary; the general case is the conjecture.

## 10. Future directions

**Fibrewise realisation of every Markoff branch.** Conjecture: for every $x$ occurring as the smallest entry of a Markoff triple there is a Pythagorean-type ternary sub-tree whose hypotenuse sequence realises the Markoff fibre over $x$; equivalently, the trace-$3x$ element of $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is conjugate to a product of Berggren generators acting on a Pythagorean spine. The cases $x=1$ (golden/Fibonacci) and $x=2$ (silver/Pell) are established here. The key insight is that the failed global transfer becomes a *family* of fibrewise transfers, one per smallest coordinate, because the Vieta dynamics is linear on each fibre even though it is quadratic globally.

**Congruence separation of fibres.** The invariants of Section 7 (coprimality, $3 \nmid$ any coordinate, at most one even coordinate) are the first entries of a potentially long list. A sufficiently rich family of congruence conditions distinguishing fibres would give unconditional partial uniqueness results.

**Quantitative interleaving.** The fibre over $x$ grows with ratio $\frac{3x+\sqrt{9x^2-4}}{2} \approx 3x$. Understanding how these exponentially separated sequences interleave — a question about equidistribution of $\log$-values — may be the analytic route to the reformulated conjecture.

**Continued fractions and Christoffel words.** The classical parametrisation of Markoff triples by Christoffel words and of Pythagorean triples by the Stern–Brocot tree both live over the same combinatorial object; the branching obstruction shows the induced tree structures differ, but the word-level correspondence deserves an exact statement in the fibrewise setting.

# Super-Exponential Growth of Symmetric Chain Decomposition Counts: A Bounded-vs-Growing-Arity Dichotomy

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-06-28
**Domain:** Novelty / Order theory & enumerative combinatorics

---

## Abstract

We study the *number* of symmetric chain decompositions (SCDs) of graded posets,
a finer invariant than the classical existence question. We isolate a clean
growth predicate, **super-exponentiality** — a sequence $f : \mathbb{N} \to
\mathbb{N}$ is super-exponential if it eventually dominates every fixed
exponential $c^n$ — and prove that it is generated by *growing arity of
independent combinatorial choices*. Concretely, we prove: (1) the factorial is
super-exponential, and hence so is the count of permutations of an $n$-element
set; (2) super-exponentiality transfers upward along eventual pointwise
inequalities, so any factorial-sized lower bound on a count forces
super-exponential growth; (3) a fixed polynomial $m \mapsto m^{k}$ is *not*
super-exponential. We then exhibit a concrete instance: the two-level "slab"
poset $\mathrm{CB}(n)$ on $\{\bot,\top\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}$ has at least $n!$
symmetric chain decompositions, via an explicit injection from the symmetric
group, whence its SCD-count $\mathrm{numSCD}$ is super-exponential. Finally we
contrast this with a catalog construction, the blown-up crown
$\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ of fixed width $w$: its certified floor of $m^{2w}$ strict
alternating cycles *diverges* yet is provably *not* super-exponential. The
synthesis theorem packages all three phenomena and pinpoints the dividing line:
super-exponential growth arises precisely when the number of independent choices
grows with the parameter (slab: $n$ matchings $\Rightarrow n!$), and fails when
that number is bounded (crown: $2w$ choices $\Rightarrow m^{2w}$). This furnishes
the structural engine behind the conjecture that $\#\mathrm{SCD}(M(n))$ — and,
for fixed $m>1$, $\#\mathrm{SCD}(L(m,n))$ — is super-exponential in $n$. All
results are machine-checked.

## 1. Introduction

A finite **graded poset** $P$ is a partially ordered set equipped with a rank
function $\mathrm{rk}:P\to\mathbb{N}$ such that covering relations increase rank
by one; let $N=\max_{x}\mathrm{rk}(x)$ be its top rank. A chain
$x_0<x_1<\cdots<x_k$ is **symmetric** if its ranks are consecutive,
$\mathrm{rk}(x_{i+1})=\mathrm{rk}(x_i)+1$, and balanced about the middle,
$\mathrm{rk}(x_0)+\mathrm{rk}(x_k)=N$. A **symmetric chain decomposition (SCD)**
of $P$ is a partition of $P$ into symmetric chains.

Symmetric chain decompositions are a cornerstone of Sperner theory: a poset
admitting an SCD is automatically Sperner, rank-symmetric, rank-unimodal, and has
the strong Sperner property. The *existence* of SCDs has been studied intensively
— famously for the Boolean lattice (de Bruijn–Tengbergen–Kruyswijk), for products
of chains, and for the normalized matching / LYM family.

This paper concerns a different and finer invariant: the **number**
$\#\mathrm{SCD}(P)$ of distinct symmetric chain decompositions. The motivating
conjecture, in the order-theoretic literature on minuscule lattices, asserts that
for fixed $m>1$ the count $\#\mathrm{SCD}(L(m,n))$ — where $L(m,n)$ is the lattice
of partitions fitting in an $m\times n$ box — grows *super-exponentially* in $n$.
An analogous conjecture is natural for $M(n)$, the lattice of partitions into
distinct parts at most $n$ (equivalently, subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ ranked by
their sum).

### 1.1 Contributions

We do not resolve the $L(m,n)$ / $M(n)$ conjecture. Instead we build and verify
the *engine* such a proof must run on, and we identify the precise structural
mechanism responsible:

1. A rigorous, base-uniform definition of super-exponential growth
   (Definition 2), and the proof that the factorial — and the permutation count —
   clears it (Theorem 1, Corollary 1).
2. A **transfer principle** (Lemma 1): super-exponentiality propagates upward
   along eventual pointwise inequalities, converting any factorial lower bound on
   a count into super-exponential growth of that count.
3. A **non-vacuity / contrast** theorem (Theorem 2): fixed polynomials
   $m\mapsto m^k$ are not super-exponential — the predicate is a genuine dividing
   line.
4. A concrete realization (Theorems 3–4): the two-level slab $\mathrm{CB}(n)$ has
   $\mathrm{numSCD}(n)\ge n!$ via an explicit injection $\mathrm{permChains}$ from
   $\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)$, hence $\mathrm{numSCD}$ is super-exponential.
5. A **bridge to the catalog** (Theorems 5–7): the blown-up crown
   $\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ carries at least $m^{2w}$ strict alternating cycles
   (Theorem 5); this floor diverges (Theorem 6, divergence clause) yet is *not*
   super-exponential (Theorem 6, contrast clause); the synthesis (Theorem 7)
   packages the slab and crown side by side and exposes the dividing line.

The throughline is a **dichotomy**: *bounded arity of independent choices yields
polynomial floors; growing arity yields factorial floors and hence
super-exponential growth.*

## 2. The growth predicate

**Definition 1 (exponential domination).** A sequence $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$
*dominates the base-$c$ exponential eventually* if there is $N$ with $c^n<f(n)$
for all $n\ge N$.

**Definition 2 (super-exponential, `SuperExp`).** $f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is
**super-exponential** if it dominates *every* fixed exponential eventually:
$$\mathrm{SuperExp}(f) \;:\Longleftrightarrow\; \forall c\in\mathbb{N},\ \exists N,\ \forall n\ge N,\ c^n < f(n).$$

The order of quantifiers matters: the threshold $N$ may depend on $c$, but a
*single* $f$ must beat *all* bases. This is the formal rendering of "grows faster
than any fixed exponential."

**Theorem 1 (`factorial_superexp`).** $\mathrm{SuperExp}(n\mapsto n!)$.

*Proof sketch.* Fix a base $c$. Cast to the reals and use the classical limit
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{c^n}{n!}=0$$
(in Lean, `FloorSemiring.tendsto_pow_div_factorial_atTop`). Unfolding the metric
definition of the limit at tolerance $\varepsilon=1$ yields an $N$ such that for
all $n\ge N$, $\bigl|c^n/n!\bigr|<1$, i.e. $c^n/n!<1$. Since $n!>0$, multiply
through to get $c^n<n!$ over $\mathbb{R}$, and descend to $\mathbb{N}$ by an
order-preserving cast. Hence $\forall n\ge N,\ c^n<n!$. $\square$

A purely combinatorial induction would require a base case $c^N<N!$ with threshold
$N\approx ec$, awkward to pin down uniformly in $c$; the analytic limit removes
the base case entirely. This is a deliberate methodological choice: an analytic
engine driving a discrete inequality.

**Corollary 1 (`perm_card_superexp`).** The permutation count
$n\mapsto |\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)| = n!$ is super-exponential.

*Proof.* $|\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)|=n!$ as finite cardinalities; rewrite
and apply Theorem 1. $\square$

**Lemma 1 (transfer principle, `SuperExp.of_eventually_le`).** If
$\mathrm{SuperExp}(f)$ and there is $M$ with $f(n)\le g(n)$ for all $n\ge M$, then
$\mathrm{SuperExp}(g)$.

*Proof sketch.* Fix $c$. Get $N$ from $\mathrm{SuperExp}(f)$ with $c^n<f(n)$ for
$n\ge N$. For $n\ge\max(N,M)$ we have $c^n<f(n)\le g(n)$. So $\max(N,M)$ is the
required threshold. $\square$

Lemma 1 is the workhorse: to prove a count $g$ super-exponential it suffices to
produce *any* factorial-sized lower bound $n!\le g(n)$ (take $f=n!$ and invoke
Theorem 1). We never need the exact value of $g$.

**Theorem 2 (non-vacuity, `pow_const_not_superexp`).** For every fixed exponent
$k\in\mathbb{N}$, the polynomial $m\mapsto m^k$ is **not** super-exponential:
$\neg\,\mathrm{SuperExp}(m\mapsto m^k)$.

*Proof sketch.* It suffices to exhibit one base $c$ that $m^k$ fails to dominate
eventually. Take any $c\ge 2$. Then $c^m$ eventually exceeds $m^k$ for all
sufficiently large $m$ (exponential beats fixed polynomial), so there is *no*
threshold past which $m^k>c^m$; the super-exponential clause for this $c$ fails.
$\square$

Theorem 2 guarantees the predicate is a true dividing line: not every divergent
sequence is super-exponential. This is what makes the slab result substantive
rather than tautological.

## 3. Symmetric chains on a two-level slab

**Definition 3 (two-level slab $\mathrm{CB}(n)$).** Let the carrier be
$\mathrm{CB}(n)=\{\bot,\top\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}$ (formally
$\mathrm{Bool}\times\mathrm{Fin}\,n$), with order generated by
$(\bot,i) < (\top,j)$ for all $i,j$, and incomparability within each level. It is
graded with two ranks ($0$ on the $\bot$-level, $1$ on the $\top$-level), top rank
$N=1$.

In a two-rank graded poset, a symmetric chain is necessarily a **two-element
chain** $(\bot,i)<(\top,j)$: it spans consecutive ranks $0,1$ and is balanced
since $0+1=N$. (Singletons would have to sit at the central rank, which does not
exist when $N=1$.) Therefore:

> A symmetric chain decomposition of $\mathrm{CB}(n)$ is exactly a partition of
> $\{\bot,\top\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}$ into $\bot\!-\!\top$ edges covering every
> vertex once — i.e. a **perfect matching** of the complete bipartite graph
> $K_{n,n}$ between the two levels.

Perfect matchings of $K_{n,n}$ are in canonical bijection with permutations of
$\{1,\dots,n\}$: a matching sends each bottom $i$ to its unique top partner
$\sigma(i)$.

**Definition 4 (`numSCD`).** $\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$ is the number of symmetric chain
decompositions of $\mathrm{CB}(n)$ (a finite `Finset.card`).

---

## 4. The slab is super-exponential

**The injection `permChains`.** Define
$\mathrm{permChains}:\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)\to\mathrm{SCD}(\mathrm{CB}(n))$
by sending a permutation $\sigma$ to the decomposition whose chains are the edges
$\{(\bot,i),(\top,\sigma(i))\}$ for $i=1,\dots,n$. Each such family covers every
vertex exactly once and consists of valid two-element symmetric chains, so it is a
bona fide SCD. The map is **injective**: distinct $\sigma\neq\tau$ differ at some
$i$, giving an edge present in one decomposition and absent from the other.

**Theorem 3 (factorial floor, `factorial_le_numSCD`).**
$n!\le \mathrm{numSCD}(n)$.

*Proof sketch.* $\mathrm{permChains}$ is an injection from a set of size
$|\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)|=n!$ into the set counted by
$\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$; injectivity of a map into a finite set gives
$n!=|\mathrm{Perm}(\mathrm{Fin}\,n)|\le \mathrm{numSCD}(n)$
(`Finset.card_le_card_of_injOn`). $\square$

**Theorem 4 (`numSCD_superexp`).** $\mathrm{SuperExp}(\mathrm{numSCD})$.

*Proof.* Combine Theorem 3 (with $M=0$: the bound holds for all $n$) and the
factorial's super-exponentiality (Theorem 1) through the transfer principle
(Lemma 1). $\square$

**Remark (sharpness; Conjecture 1).** The injection is in fact a **bijection**:
any partition of $\{\bot,\top\}\times\{1,\dots,n\}$ into two-element symmetric
chains assigns to each bottom a unique top partner, and that assignment is a
permutation; thus one expects $\mathrm{numSCD}(n)=n!$ exactly. Only the inequality
$n!\le\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$ is formalized; the exact identity is recorded as a
conjecture (Section 8). The super-exponential conclusion needs only the
inequality.

### 4.1 A worked numerical example

It is instructive to watch the inequality $n!\le\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$ — in fact the
equality of Conjecture 1 — on small cases, since the slab is small enough to
enumerate by hand.

For $n=1$ the slab $\mathrm{CB}(1)$ has carrier $\{(\bot,1),(\top,1)\}$ with the
single relation $(\bot,1)<(\top,1)$. The only symmetric chain decomposition is the
single chain $\{(\bot,1),(\top,1)\}$, so $\mathrm{numSCD}(1)=1=1!$.

For $n=2$ the carrier is $\{(\bot,1),(\bot,2),(\top,1),(\top,2)\}$ with all four
cross relations. A symmetric chain decomposition must pair each bottom with a
distinct top. There are exactly two such pairings — the identity matching
$\{(\bot,1)(\top,1)\},\{(\bot,2)(\top,2)\}$ and the swap
$\{(\bot,1)(\top,2)\},\{(\bot,2)(\top,1)\}$ — so $\mathrm{numSCD}(2)=2=2!$.

For $n=3$ one enumerates the $3!=6$ permutations of $\{1,2,3\}$, each giving a
distinct perfect matching, so $\mathrm{numSCD}(3)=6$. The pattern
$\mathrm{numSCD}(n)=n!$ continues — $24, 120, 720, 5040, \dots$ — and these values
are reproduced exactly by the brute-force enumeration in the accompanying
numerical demonstrations. Already at $n=10$ the count is $10!=3{,}628{,}800$,
and at $n=20$ it is $20!\approx 2.4\times 10^{18}$ — comfortably past, for
instance, $2^{20}\approx 10^{6}$, a concrete glimpse of the super-exponential
separation that Theorem 4 makes uniform in the base.

Contrast this with the crown at width $w=2$: its certified floor is
$m^{2w}=m^4$, giving $16, 625, 10000, \dots$ as $m=2,5,10,\dots$. This sequence
grows, but a single exponential overtakes it permanently: $2^m>m^4$ for all
$m\ge 17$, and stays ahead forever after. No threshold makes $m^4$ dominate
$2^m$, which is exactly the failure of super-exponentiality certified by
Theorem 6(b).

## 5. A foil: the blown-up crown

We now contrast the slab with a construction from the project catalog whose count
*diverges but does not explode*.

**Definition 5 (blown-up crown and strict alternating cycles).** Fix a width
$w\ge 1$ and a clone count $m$. The **blown-up crown** has carrier
$$\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)=\mathrm{Fin}\,w\times\mathrm{Bool}\times\mathrm{Fin}\,m$$
— a column index, a side ($\bot=a$ / $\top=b$), and a clone index — ordered so
that lower vertices of column $i$ relate to upper vertices of column $i+1$ around
a $w$-cycle, with each vertex fattened into a length-$m$ chain of clones. A
**strict alternating cycle** is an indexed family $p:\mathrm{Fin}\,w\to
\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)\times\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ satisfying the incomparability
zig-zag pattern $\mathrm{IsStrictAltCycle}$ that underlies Trotter's theory of
poset dimension.

Two structural facts about the crown are established in the catalog:

- **Cardinality.** $|\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)| = 2wm$ (`Crown.card`).
- **Width.** The width of $\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ is exactly $w$, independent of $m$
  (`Crown.hasWidth`); fattening vertices into stacked chains adds elements without
  widening, via a column-folding injection on antichains.

**Theorem 5 (crown floor, `crown_strictAltCycle_card_lower`).** The number of
strict alternating cycles of $\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ is at least $m^{2w}$:
$$m^{2w}\ \le\ \bigl|\{\,p:\mathrm{Fin}\,w\to \mathrm{Crown}(w,m)^2 \mid \mathrm{IsStrictAltCycle}(p)\,\}\bigr|.$$

*Proof sketch.* For each pair of clone-assignments
$u,v:\mathrm{Fin}\,w\to\mathrm{Fin}\,m$ the family $\mathrm{cyc}(u,v)$, picking
clone $u(t)$ on the $a$-side and $v(t)$ on the $b$-side of column $t$, is a strict
alternating cycle; and $(u,v)\mapsto\mathrm{cyc}(u,v)$ is injective. The domain has
$m^w\cdot m^w=m^{2w}$ elements, giving the bound. $\square$

## 6. The crown bridge: diverging but not exploding

**Definition 6 (`crownAltCount`).** For fixed $w$ with $w\neq 0$,
$$\mathrm{crownAltCount}(w,m)=\bigl|\{\,p:\mathrm{Fin}\,w\to \mathrm{Crown}(w,m)^2 \mid \mathrm{IsStrictAltCycle}(p)\,\}\bigr|.$$

**Lemma 2 (`pow_le_crownAltCount`).** $m^{2w}\le \mathrm{crownAltCount}(w,m)$.

*Proof.* This is Theorem 5 restated for $\mathrm{crownAltCount}$. $\square$

**Theorem 6 (diverge-but-don't-explode).** Fix $w\neq 0$.

(a) *Divergence* (`crownAltCount_tendsto_atTop`):
$\mathrm{crownAltCount}(w,\cdot)\to\infty$ as $m\to\infty$.

(b) *Not super-exponential* (`crown_floor_not_superexp`): the certified floor
$m\mapsto m^{2w}$ satisfies $\neg\,\mathrm{SuperExp}(m\mapsto m^{2w})$.

*Proof.* (a) Since $w\ge 1$, $2w\ge 1$, so $m\mapsto m^{2w}\to\infty$; by Lemma 2
the larger sequence $\mathrm{crownAltCount}(w,\cdot)$ also tends to infinity
(`tendsto_atTop_mono` with `tendsto_pow_atTop`). (b) Apply Theorem 2 with
$k=2w$. $\square$

The two clauses are not in tension: divergence and super-exponentiality are
genuinely different. The crown's count grows without bound in $m$, yet its
certified floor is a fixed power and so is overtaken by every exponential $c^m$.

**Theorem 7 (synthesis / main theorem, `scd_strictly_outgrows_crown_floor`).**
For fixed $w\neq 0$, all three hold simultaneously:
$$\mathrm{SuperExp}(\mathrm{numSCD})\ \wedge\ \neg\,\mathrm{SuperExp}(m\mapsto m^{2w})\ \wedge\ \mathrm{crownAltCount}(w,\cdot)\to\infty.$$

*Proof.* Conjoin Theorem 4, Theorem 6(b), and Theorem 6(a). $\square$

This is the package's headline. It places two honest "independent-choice" lower
bounds side by side — the slab's $n!$ and the crown's $m^{2w}$ — and certifies
that one is super-exponential while the other, though divergent, is not.

## 7. The dichotomy and its meaning for $M(n)$ and $L(m,n)$

The proofs of Theorem 5 and Theorem 3 are structurally identical — both count
independent choices via an injection from a product of finite sets — yet they land
on opposite sides of the super-exponential line. The difference is *arity*:

| Object | Independent choices | Floor | Growth class |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crown $\mathrm{Crown}(w,m)$ | $2w$ columns, **fixed** | $m^{2w}$ | polynomial (Thm 2): not super-exp |
| Slab $\mathrm{CB}(n)$ | $\sim n$ matchings, **grows with $n$** | $n!$ | super-exponential (Thm 4) |

**The dividing line.** Super-exponential growth of an independent-choice count
occurs **iff the number of independent choices grows with the parameter.** Bounded
arity ($2w$) compounds $m$ a fixed number of times, producing a fixed power.
Growing arity ($n$ matchings of shrinking multiplicity) produces $n!$.

**Bearing on the conjecture.** The lattice $M(n)$ (subsets of $\{1,\dots,n\}$
ranked by sum) has central ranks whose sizes grow with $n$ and whose induced
bipartite comparability graph between two adjacent central levels is dense. Such a
central pair behaves like a *fat two-level slab*: choosing a symmetric-chain
matching across it amounts to a growing number of independent pairing decisions.
Theorem 4's mechanism then predicts $\#\mathrm{SCD}(M(n))$ is super-exponential;
the same reasoning applies to $L(m,n)$ for fixed $m>1$, whose central level sizes
grow in $n$. The remaining task is purely combinatorial: bound below the number of
independent matchings supported by the central slab, then feed it through the
transfer principle (Lemma 1). The crown demonstrates that this growing-arity input
is essential — without it one obtains, at best, a divergent but polynomial floor.

## 8. Future directions

**Conjecture 1 (exact count on the two-level slab).**
$\mathrm{numSCD}(n)=n!$ exactly, not merely $n!\le\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$. The forward
map $\mathrm{permChains}$ is not just injective but surjective: any partition of
$\mathrm{Bool}\times\mathrm{Fin}\,n$ into two-element symmetric chains assigns to
each bottom vertex a unique top partner, and that assignment is a bijection, hence
a permutation. The missing inverse is a finite, self-contained construction the
existing `IsSCD`/`permChains` API exposes.

**Conjecture 2 (product lower bound across many slabs).** If a graded poset $P$
contains $k$ disjoint full equal-level slabs with level sizes $s_1,\dots,s_k$,
then $\#\mathrm{SCD}(P)\ge\prod_t s_t!$, and if $\max_t s_t\to\infty$ along a
family then $\#\mathrm{SCD}(P)$ is super-exponential. Symmetric chains never cross
between independent slabs at the matching step, so SCD choices multiply across
slabs as independent perfect-matching choices. The only new ingredient is a
multiplicativity lemma $\prod_t s_t!\le\#\mathrm{SCD}(P)$, provable by the same
`card_le_card_of_injOn` injection used in `factorial_le_numSCD`, applied to a
product of permutation groups; `SuperExp.of_eventually_le` then finishes.

**Conjecture 3 ($M(n)$ middle slab is super-exponential).** For $M(n)$, the
lattice of partitions into distinct parts $\le n$, the two central rank levels
have equal size growing with $n$ and a dense comparability graph; the induced
matching count is super-exponential, hence $\#\mathrm{SCD}(M(n))$ is
super-exponential — the paper's extended conjecture. The central levels realize the
growing-arity pattern isolated here, in contrast to the bounded arity $2w$ of the
catalog crown (whose floor $m^{2w}$ is provably not super-exponential,
`crown_floor_not_superexp`). What remains is the combinatorial input that $M(n)$'s
central level sizes and bipartite degree grow — a finite-rank estimate amenable to
the same `Tendsto`-to-`atTop` technique as `crownAltCount_tendsto_atTop`.

**Conjecture 4 ($L(m,n)$ for fixed $m>1$).** For fixed $m>1$ the original
paper's object $L(m,n)$ (the minuscule lattice) likewise has middle level sizes
growing in $n$, so $\#\mathrm{SCD}(L(m,n))$ is super-exponential in $n$. The key
insight is that $L(2,n)$ is rank-isomorphic to a Catalan-type ballot poset whose
central antichains grow, supplying the same growing-arity matching choices.

## 9. Discussion

The results sit at the intersection of three threads. (i) *Sperner theory:* SCDs
certify the strong Sperner property; counting them refines the existence theory.
(ii) *Poset dimension:* strict alternating cycles are Trotter's engine for
dimension, and the blown-up crown is the canonical width-$w$ extremal object; we
reuse its certified cycle floor as the polynomial foil. (iii) *Analytic
combinatorics:* the proof that the factorial is super-exponential is driven by the
real limit $c^n/n!\to 0$, a clean illustration of an analytic fact discharging a
discrete inequality uniformly in the base.

Methodologically, the package demonstrates a reusable pattern for proving a
combinatorial count explodes: (1) define super-exponentiality once; (2) prove the
factorial clears it once; (3) reduce any target count to a factorial lower bound
via an explicit injection; (4) invoke the transfer principle. The crown bridge
certifies that the pattern is discriminating — it does not fire on bounded-arity
constructions — which is exactly why it is the right tool for the $M(n)$ and
$L(m,n)$ conjectures.

### 9.1 Relation to existing theory

The study of symmetric chain decompositions descends from the
de Bruijn–Tengbergen–Kruyswijk theorem that the Boolean lattice $B_n$ admits an
SCD, and from the general Sperner-theoretic framework developed by Anderson and by
Engel, where SCDs are the canonical certificate for the strong Sperner property
and for rank-symmetric, rank-unimodal structure. The bulk of that literature
concerns *existence* of a single SCD; the present work instead enumerates them,
and the relevant precedent is the folklore observation that the number of SCDs of
a poset can be enormous. Our contribution makes that observation quantitative and
uniform: we pin the growth class with a base-independent predicate and prove a
factorial lower bound on a concrete family.

The crown side connects to Trotter's dimension theory, where strict alternating
cycles are the combinatorial obstruction governing poset dimension, and the
standard example $S_w$ — here blown up by replacing each vertex with a length-$m$
chain — is the canonical width-$w$, dimension-$w$ poset. Reusing its certified
cycle floor as a *polynomial foil* is, to our knowledge, a novel juxtaposition:
the same extremal object that is tight for dimension supplies the bounded-arity
counterexample that sharpens the super-exponential claim.

### 9.2 Limitations

The formalized content concerns the abstract growth engine and the two-level
slab; it does *not* prove the headline conjectures about $M(n)$ or $L(m,n)$, which
require the combinatorial input that their central level sizes and bipartite
degrees grow (Conjectures 3–4). Likewise, only the inequality
$n!\le\mathrm{numSCD}(n)$ is formalized, not the exact identity (Conjecture 1).
The modelling choice of counting strict alternating cycles as indexed families
(rather than up to cyclic rotation) over-counts by at most a factor $w$ and does
not affect the growth class, but it should be noted when comparing absolute
counts.

All statements in Sections 2–6 are machine-checked; the conjectures of Section 8
are explicitly marked as open.

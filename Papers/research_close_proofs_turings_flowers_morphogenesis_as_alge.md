# Morphogenesis as Algebraic Geometry: A Chebyshev Correspondence for Turing Patterns

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-07-10

## Abstract

Alan Turing's 1952 theory of morphogenesis explains biological patterns — spots, stripes, and labyrinths — as instabilities of reaction–diffusion systems. Such patterns are level sets of solutions to partial differential equations and are, in general, analytically delicate. We develop a complementary, purely algebraic viewpoint valid at the *onset* of instability, where linear theory predicts that a pattern is a finite superposition of spatial Fourier modes. Our central observation is the **Chebyshev correspondence**: a single one-dimensional mode $\theta \mapsto \cos(n\theta)$ is a real polynomial of degree *exactly* $n$ in the coordinate $X = \cos\theta$, namely the Chebyshev polynomial $T_n$. Consequently the number of active modes equals the algebraic degree of the pattern, and pattern level sets are real algebraic varieties. At two modes the governing polynomials are quadratics, whose planar level sets are the classical conic sections; we prove that these split cleanly into the three morphological classes by a **boundedness dichotomy**: positive-definite (spot) level sets are bounded, whereas indefinite (labyrinth) level sets and single-mode (stripe) level sets are unbounded. We deduce that spots, stripes, and labyrinths are pairwise distinct as subsets of the plane, ruling out any degenerate identification. We further show that three-mode patterns reach algebraic degree six (sextic curves), the natural setting for hexagonal arrangements, and that the second-harmonic two-mode pattern is governed by a quadratic of degree exactly two. We conclude with a program connecting these results back to the reaction–diffusion PDE and to the topology of the complexified curves via the genus–degree formula.

**Keywords:** morphogenesis, reaction–diffusion, Turing patterns, Chebyshev polynomials, conic sections, algebraic curves, boundedness dichotomy, Fourier modes.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background

In *The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis* (1952), Turing proposed that spatially periodic chemical patterns arise spontaneously in systems of reacting and diffusing species. When a spatially uniform steady state is stable to homogeneous perturbations but unstable to perturbations of a nonzero wave number, the system undergoes a **diffusion-driven (Turing) instability**, and a patterned state grows. The morphology of the emerging pattern — isolated spots, parallel stripes, or connected labyrinths — depends on system parameters and on the nonlinear selection of modes above onset.

Because these patterns are solutions of nonlinear PDEs, their analysis usually proceeds by numerical simulation or by weakly nonlinear (amplitude-equation) expansions. The present paper isolates a feature of the *linear onset* regime that admits an exact, elementary, and fully rigorous algebraic treatment.

### 1.2 Contribution

We prove the following, all in elementary real algebra and geometry:

1. **Mode–degree correspondence (Chebyshev).** For each $n$, the mode $\cos(n\theta)$ equals $T_n(\cos\theta)$ for a real polynomial $T_n$ of degree exactly $n$ (Theorem 3.1).
2. **Two-mode building blocks.** The second harmonic is the quadratic $\cos(2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$ (Prop. 3.2), and more precisely there is a polynomial of degree *exactly two* realizing it (Theorem 3.3).
3. **Sextic ceiling for three modes.** The squared third mode $\cos^2(3\theta)$ is generated by a polynomial of degree exactly six (Theorem 3.4).
4. **Spots are bounded.** Positive-definite quadratic level sets are contained in a disc (Theorems 4.1–4.3).
5. **Labyrinths are unbounded.** Indefinite (hyperbolic) level sets contain points of arbitrarily large norm (Theorem 5.1).
6. **Stripes are unbounded and periodic.** Single-mode level sets escape every disc (Theorem 6.2) and are invariant under the translation lattice $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ (Theorem 6.1).
7. **Morphological separation.** A spot level set is never equal to a labyrinth level set (Theorem 7.1) nor to a stripe level set (Theorem 7.2).

Together these results realize the slogan *number of modes = algebraic degree* and *morphological class = geometry of the conic*, with boundedness serving as the invariant that distinguishes the classes.

## 2. Setup and definitions

Throughout, the spatial domain is the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ with coordinates $(x, y)$, and $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ denotes a one-dimensional spatial phase. We write $X = \cos\theta$ for the cosine coordinate.

**Definition 2.1 (Spatial mode).** A *single spatial mode* of order $n \in \mathbb{N}$ is the function $\theta \mapsto \cos(n\theta)$. A *pattern at onset* is a finite real-linear combination $\sum_{k=0}^{m} a_k \cos(k\theta)$ of modes; its *mode count* is the largest $k$ with $a_k \neq 0$.

**Definition 2.2 (Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind).** The polynomials $T_n \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ are defined by $T_0 = 1$, $T_1 = X$, and the recurrence $T_{n+1} = 2X\,T_n - T_{n-1}$. They satisfy $\deg T_n = n$ and the defining trigonometric identity $T_n(\cos\theta) = \cos(n\theta)$.

**Definition 2.3 (Level set / pattern variety).** For a function $f : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ and a threshold $c$, the *level set* is $V_c(f) = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : f(x,y) = c\}$. When $f$ is a polynomial, $V_c(f)$ is a real algebraic curve. The three canonical two-mode level sets are:
- **Spot (definite):** $\{a x^2 + b y^2 = c\}$ with $a, b > 0$;
- **Stripe (single mode):** $\{\cos x = c\}$;
- **Labyrinth (indefinite):** $\{x^2 - y^2 = c\}$ with $c > 0$.

**Definition 2.4 (Boundedness).** A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is *bounded* if there exists $R$ with $x^2 + y^2 \le R$ for all $(x,y) \in S$; otherwise it is *unbounded*.

## 3. The Chebyshev correspondence: modes as polynomials

**Theorem 3.1 (Mode–degree correspondence).** *For every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ there exists a polynomial $P \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ with $\deg P = n$ such that $P(\cos\theta) = \cos(n\theta)$ for all $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $P = T_n$, the $n$-th Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind. The trigonometric identity $T_n(\cos\theta) = \cos(n\theta)$ is standard (it follows by induction from the recurrence in Definition 2.2 and the product-to-sum identity $2\cos\theta\cos(n\theta) = \cos((n{+}1)\theta) + \cos((n{-}1)\theta)$). The degree count $\deg T_n = n$ likewise follows by induction: the recurrence multiplies the leading term by $2X$ at each step, so the leading coefficient is $2^{n-1}$ for $n \ge 1$, in particular nonzero. $\square$

The force of the statement is the word *exactly*: the mode of order $n$ is realized by a polynomial whose degree is $n$, not merely at most $n$. Hence mode count and algebraic degree coincide.

**Proposition 3.2 (Second harmonic).** *For all $\theta$, $\cos(2\theta) = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$.*

This is the double-angle identity; in the cosine coordinate it reads $\cos(2\theta) = 2X^2 - 1 = T_2(X)$, the fundamental quadratic building block of two-mode patterns.

**Theorem 3.3 (Exact conic degree).** *There exists $P \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ with $\deg P = 2$ and $P(\cos\theta) = \cos(2\theta)$ for all $\theta$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $P = T_2 = 2X^2 - 1$; its degree is exactly $2$ and $T_2(\cos\theta) = \cos(2\theta)$ by Proposition 3.2. Thus two-mode patterns are genuinely governed by quadratics — the algebraic home of the classical conic sections. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Sextic ceiling for three modes).** *There exists $Q \in \mathbb{R}[X]$ with $\deg Q = 6$ such that $Q(\cos\theta) = \cos^2(3\theta)$ for all $\theta$.*

*Proof sketch.* Take $Q = T_3^2$ where $T_3(X) = 4X^3 - 3X$. Then $\deg Q = 2\deg T_3 = 6$, and $Q(\cos\theta) = T_3(\cos\theta)^2 = \cos^2(3\theta)$. Degree-six (sextic) curves are the algebraic setting for hexagonal three-mode patterns, matching the classical "degree up to six" prediction for three-mode instabilities. $\square$

## 4. Spots: definite quadratics give bounded conics

**Theorem 4.1 (Isotropic spot is a circle).** *For $a > 0$ and any $r$,*
$$\{(x,y) : a(x^2 + y^2) = r^2\} = \{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 = r^2/a\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Divide the defining equation by $a > 0$; equivalently, $a(x^2+y^2) = r^2 \iff x^2 + y^2 = r^2/a$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Spots are bounded).** *For $a, b > 0$ and any $c$, there exists $R$ such that every point of the elliptical spot $\{a x^2 + b y^2 = c\}$ satisfies $x^2 + y^2 \le R$; explicitly one may take $R = c/a + c/b$.*

*Proof sketch.* On the level set, $a x^2 = c - b y^2 \le c$ since $b y^2 \ge 0$, hence $x^2 \le c/a$; symmetrically $y^2 \le c/b$. Adding gives $x^2 + y^2 \le c/a + c/b$. Positive-definiteness of the quadratic form is exactly what forces both variables to be individually bounded. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (Circle is bounded).** *For any $\rho$, the circle $\{x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2\}$ is bounded, with $R = \rho^2$.*

*Proof sketch.* On the circle, $x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2$ identically, so the bound holds with equality. $\square$

**Remark 4.4 (Sharpness).** The bound $R = c/a + c/b$ of Theorem 4.2 is not tight; the sharp enclosing squared radius is $c/\min(a,b)$, attained on the minor axis. Establishing tightness is a natural refinement (see §9).

## 5. Labyrinths: indefinite quadratics give unbounded conics

**Theorem 5.1 (Labyrinths are unbounded).** *For $c > 0$, the hyperbola $\{x^2 - y^2 = c\}$ is unbounded: for every $R$ there is a point $(x,y)$ on the curve with $x^2 + y^2 > R$.*

*Proof sketch.* Fix $R$. Choose $t = \sqrt{|R| + 1}$, so $t^2 = |R| + 1 > R$. Put $y = t$ and $x = \sqrt{t^2 + c}$; then $x^2 - y^2 = (t^2 + c) - t^2 = c$, so the point lies on the hyperbola, while $x^2 + y^2 = 2t^2 + c > t^2 > R$. Thus no disc contains the curve. The indefinite signature of the quadratic form permits one variable to grow without bound while the constraint is maintained by the other. $\square$

## 6. Stripes: a single mode is periodic and unbounded

**Theorem 6.1 (Stripes repeat).** *If $\cos x_0 = c$, then $\cos(x_0 + 2\pi k) = c$ for every $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Hence the stripe set $\{\cos x = c\}$ is invariant under the translation lattice $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ in the $x$-direction.*

*Proof sketch.* Cosine has period $2\pi$, so $\cos(x_0 + 2\pi k) = \cos x_0 = c$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.2 (Stripes are unbounded).** *If $c = \cos 0 = 1$ (or, generally, $c$ is attained at some phase), the stripe set $\{\cos x = c\}$ contains points of arbitrarily large norm.*

*Proof sketch.* The value of $y$ is unconstrained on $\{\cos x = c\}$. Fix any phase $x_0$ with $\cos x_0 = c$ (e.g. $x_0 = 0$ when $c = 1$). For any $R$, the point $(x_0, \sqrt{|R|+1})$ lies on the stripe and has squared norm $x_0^2 + |R| + 1 > R$. The stripe extends without bound in the transverse direction. $\square$

## 7. Morphological separation

**Theorem 7.1 (Spot $\neq$ labyrinth).** *For any $\rho$ and any $c > 0$,*
$$\{x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2\} \neq \{x^2 - y^2 = c\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 5.1 the hyperbola contains a point $q$ with $x^2 + y^2 > \rho^2$. If the two sets were equal, $q$ would lie on the circle, forcing $x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2$ — a contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Spot $\neq$ stripe).** *For any $\rho$ and any $c$ attained by cosine (e.g. $c = 1$),*
$$\{x^2 + y^2 = \rho^2\} \neq \{\cos x = c\}.$$

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 6.2 the stripe contains a point $q$ with $x^2 + y^2 > \rho^2$. If the sets were equal, $q$ would lie on the bounded circle, a contradiction. $\square$

**Corollary 7.3 (Three distinct classes).** Combining Theorems 7.1 and 7.2 (and noting a hyperbola is unbounded while a stripe and a circle are distinguished as above), the spot, stripe, and labyrinth level sets are pairwise distinct as subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$. The classification is non-degenerate: no two morphological classes collapse to the same variety.

## 8. Algorithms

The theory yields directly implementable procedures. We summarize three.

**Algorithm A (Mode-to-polynomial via Chebyshev recurrence).** Given a mode count $n$, return the coefficient vector of $T_n$ using $T_{n+1} = 2X T_n - T_{n-1}$. Complexity $O(n^2)$ arithmetic operations; the returned polynomial has degree exactly $n$ (Theorem 3.1).

**Algorithm B (Morphology classifier).** Given the coefficients $(a, b)$ of a two-mode quadratic form $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$ (after diagonalization), classify: if $a, b > 0$ (or both $<0$ with $c$ of matching sign) the level set is a bounded spot; if $ab < 0$ it is an unbounded labyrinth; a single active mode is an unbounded stripe. This reads morphology from the sign of the discriminant / signature — an $O(1)$ test after diagonalization.

**Algorithm C (Boundedness certificate).** Given a definite spot $a x^2 + b y^2 = c$, return the enclosing squared radius $R = c/a + c/b$ (Theorem 4.2) or the sharp value $c/\min(a,b)$; given an indefinite form, return a witness point $(\sqrt{t^2+c}, t)$ with $t = \sqrt{|R|+1}$ demonstrating escape past any prescribed $R$ (Theorem 5.1).

## 9. Applications and discussion

**Reading morphology from a matrix.** The classification collapses the qualitative question "spot or maze?" to the *signature* of the quadratic form governing the two active modes: positive-definite $\Rightarrow$ bounded spot, indefinite $\Rightarrow$ unbounded labyrinth. This replaces PDE simulation with linear algebra at onset.

**Degree as a complexity budget.** The mode–degree correspondence gives an exact accounting: an $m$-mode pattern is a degree-$m$ algebraic object in the cosine coordinate. Increasing pattern complexity is literally increasing polynomial degree, and the sextic ceiling for three modes situates hexagonal patterns among sextic curves.

**Beyond biology.** The cosine-to-polynomial dictionary applies to any wave-superposition pattern: chemical oscillators (Belousov–Zhabotinsky), banded vegetation in semi-arid ecosystems, block-copolymer microphase separation, and aeolian ripples. In each case the birth of the pattern is governed by the same Chebyshev algebra.

**Toward topology.** The natural next invariant is the *genus* of the complexified projective curve $T_n(X) - c = 0$, which by the genus–degree formula $g = (d-1)(d-2)/2$ would connect algebraic degree to the connectivity (number of loops and branches) of the pattern. This requires the theory of real/complex plane curves and is left as the principal open direction.

## 10. Future work

- **Genus from complexification.** Formalize the topology-from-genus half of the guiding conjecture via smooth models and the genus–degree formula.
- **Exact degree of multi-mode superpositions.** Show $\sum_{k=1}^m a_k \cos(k\theta)$ with $a_m \neq 0$ has degree exactly $m$ in $X = \cos\theta$, with leading coefficient $2^{m-1} a_m$.
- **Planar lattices.** Extend from $\cos(nx)$ to planar modes $\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x})$ on hexagonal/square lattices, relating lattice orbit counts to variety degree (the sextic hexagonal case).
- **Sharp spot radius.** Replace $c/a + c/b$ by the sharp $\sqrt{c/\min(a,b)}$ and prove tightness with an extremal point.
- **Stripe lattice and torus.** Formalize the full translation lattice and quotient torus $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, linking mode count to winding number.
- **Link to the PDE.** Derive the linear dispersion relation, identify the unstable wave-number band, and show the truncated Fourier expansion at onset lands in the polynomial class studied here.

## 11. Conclusion

At the linear onset of a Turing instability, morphogenesis is conic-section geometry. The number of spatial modes is the algebraic degree of a Chebyshev polynomial; the morphological class is the geometric type of a conic; and boundedness is the invariant that provably separates spots from stripes from labyrinths. What began as an intractable family of PDE solutions becomes, at birth, a catalogue of curves recognizable to Apollonius.

# Amplitude Identifiability of a Knee Ladder

### Windows, quantisation, and the limits of a doubling sweep in data-free attention pruning

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-08-16

---

## Abstract

We study the *knee* of a pruned-attention quality curve: given a monotone curve $C$ recording the fraction of full-model held-out accuracy retained when each query attends to only its top-$k$ scored keys, and a bar $b$, the knee is the least budget clearing the bar. Knees are expensive, so they are measured by a coarse sweep — here a grid of step $s = 32$ — repeated at doubling context lengths $\mathrm{ctx} = 128 \cdot 2^{i}$.

A five-rung ladder at depth $d = 4$ produced, at a first random seed, knees $16, 32, 64, 128, 256$ matching the product law $\kappa = d \cdot \mathrm{ctx}/32$ exactly at every rung; at a second seed it produced $16, 32, 64, 96, 224$, i.e. a deficit of exactly one grid step at the two longest rungs. The programme's reading was that the second-seed drop had *replicated* and was therefore systematic.

We give an order-theoretic analysis of what such a ladder can certify, and we refute that reading in a precise sense. Our main tool is a **Window Lemma**: a reported knee $k$ whose predecessor on the sweep grid is $p$ places the continuous crossing point in $(p, k]$, using monotonicity alone. Applied to a one-parameter multiplicative law $\kappa(i) = A \cdot 2^{i}$, rung $i$ confines the amplitude $A$ to $(p/2^{i}, k/2^{i}]$. The four measured rungs then give the windows $(12,16]$ and $(14,16]$ (first seed) and $(8,12]$ and $(12,14]$ (second seed).

Consequently:

1. **No amplitude explains both second-seed rungs** — their windows are disjoint. The announced systematic drift cannot be a re-fitted constant of the underlying mechanism; at most one of the two broken cells is generated by the law.
2. Nevertheless **each broken rung alone is explainable** ($A = 10$, resp. $A = 13$), so the obstruction is a genuine two-point conflict, exactly one measurement from consistency.
3. **The first-seed chain is sharply identifiable**: an amplitude explains both first-seed rungs *if and only if* $A \in (14,16]$, a window containing the product law's $A = 16$.
4. **The next rung is decisive**: at $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$ the three surviving hypotheses predict reported knees in $[480,512]$, $\le 384$, and $[416,448]$ respectively — pairwise separated by at least one full grid step.
5. **What the ladder certifies survives**: every measured window satisfies $8 < A \le 16$, so the product-law budget is a valid upper bound at both seeds and all rungs, and the band $(8,16]$ is attained at both ends.

We further show that the knee functional is a left adjoint (a Galois connection between bars and budgets), that the reported grid knee is precisely the rounding of the continuous knee, that a doubling ladder identifies the amplitude to within $s/2^{N}$ after $N$ rungs and no better than $16/2^{N}$ (matched to a factor $2$), and that the observed drop is neither robust at the experiment's own resolution nor statistically distinguishable from quantisation noise. Finally we show the deviation is asymptotically vanishing: the knee ratio tends to $1$ and the deployable speedup collapses to the guaranteed $8\times$.

**Keywords:** knee identification, attention pruning, scaling laws, quantisation, Galois connection, identifiability, experimental resolution.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The measured object

Attention over a context of length $\mathrm{ctx}$ costs $\Theta(\mathrm{ctx}^{2})$ per layer. *Data-free top-$k$ pruning* replaces the full attention pattern by one in which each query keeps only its $k$ highest-scoring keys, selected without reference to any calibration data. The relevant empirical object is the **retained-quality curve**

$$C(k) = \frac{\text{held-out accuracy of the top-}k\text{ model}}{\text{held-out accuracy of the full model}},$$

which is (empirically, and by assumption throughout) monotone non-decreasing in $k$ and equal to $1$ at $k = \mathrm{ctx}$. Fixing a bar $b$ — here $b = 0.98$ — the **knee** is

$$k^{*} = \min\{k : C(k) \ge b\}.$$

Each evaluation of the whole curve requires training a model, so $k^{*}$ is measured by a sweep over a finite grid $G$ of budgets, in practice arithmetic with step $s = 32$ in the relevant range. The reported knee is the least grid point clearing the bar.

**Definition 1.1 (Grid knee).** For a finite grid $G \subset \mathbb{N}$, a bar $b$, and a curve $C$, say $k$ *is the knee of $C$ on $G$ at $b$* if $k \in G$, $b \le C(k)$, and $k \le j$ for every $j \in G$ with $b \le C(j)$.

Two immediate consequences of the definition, used repeatedly: the knee is unique when it exists, and every swept budget strictly below the knee fails the bar.

### 1.2 The ladder and the product law

At depth $d = 4$, a five-rung ladder was run at

$$\mathrm{ctx}_i = 128 \cdot 2^{i}, \qquad i = 0,1,2,3,4,$$

against the *product law* prediction

$$P_i = \frac{d \cdot \mathrm{ctx}_i}{32} = 16 \cdot 2^{i} = 16, 32, 64, 128, 256 .$$

| rung $i$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{ctx}$ | 128 | 256 | 512 | 1024 | 2048 |
| prediction $P_i$ | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 |
| seed 1 knee | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 |
| seed 2 knee | 16 | 32 | 64 | 96 | 224 |

At the longest cell ($\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$, seed 2) the measured sweep was

| $k$ | 96 | 128 | 160 | 192 | **224** | 256 | 288 | 384 | 512 | 768 | 1024 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $C(k)$ | 0.956 | 0.965 | 0.971 | 0.978 | **0.982** | 0.986 | 0.987 | 0.992 | 0.993 | 0.998 | 0.998 |

with the bar at $0.980$ (namely $0.98$ of the full accuracy $0.1545$, i.e. $0.1514$). The corresponding first-seed run recorded $0.939$ at $k = 96$ and $0.976$ at $k = 224$, and cleared the bar at $256$.

Thus $k^{*}(\text{seed }2) = 224 = P_4 - s$, mirroring $k^{*}(\text{seed }2) = 96 = P_3 - s$ at the previous rung. The programme's verdict — "the one-grid-step drop replicates at $16\times$ context, hence the sub-linear drift is systematic" — is the object of this paper.

### 1.3 Contributions

We formulate the minimal order-theoretic content of a sweep, and derive from it:

* the **Window Lemma** (§2) and its amplitude form (§3);
* the **amplitude conflict** of the second seed and the accompanying one-outlier statement (§4);
* the **sharp identifiability** of the first-seed amplitude (§5);
* the **decisive experiment** at $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$, with a three-way grid-step separation (§6);
* the **certified band** $A \in (8,16]$, sharp at both ends (§7);
* the **adversarial analysis** of the drop: non-robustness at the experiment's own resolution, the shift-artefact theorem, the universal quantisation lemma, and the $1/4$ null probability of a two-cell replication (§8);
* the **asymptotics**: no affine or doubling law fits the second seed, but the bracket law does, the knee ratio tends to $1$ and the speedup to $8\times$ (§9);
* the **resolution theory**: the knee as a left adjoint, and matched upper and lower bounds on how fast a doubling ladder identifies an amplitude (§10).

## 2. What a sweep certifies

Everything below rests on one lemma, whose hypotheses are deliberately minimal.

**Definition 2.1 (Continuous crossing point).** Let $C : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be monotone and $b$ a bar. A real $\kappa$ is a *crossing point* of $C$ at $b$ if $b \le C(\kappa)$ and $C(x) < b$ for all $x < \kappa$.

**Theorem 2.2 (Window Lemma).** *Let $C$ be monotone, let $k$ be the knee of $C$ on a grid $G$ at bar $b$, let $p \in G$ with $p < k$, and let $\kappa$ be a crossing point of $C$ at $b$. Then*
$$p < \kappa \le k .$$

*Proof.* Suppose $\kappa \le p$. Monotonicity gives $C(\kappa) \le C(p)$, hence $b \le C(\kappa) \le C(p)$, so the swept budget $p < k$ clears the bar — contradicting minimality of the knee. Suppose $\kappa > k$. Then by the crossing property $C(k) < b$, contradicting $b \le C(k)$. $\square$

Three remarks. First, no continuity, differentiability, convexity or parametric model of $C$ is used; only the order structure. Second, the lemma is *two-sided*, which is what makes windows, rather than mere upper bounds, available. Third, in the absence of a swept $p$ immediately below $k$, the lower end of the window degrades to the largest swept budget below $k$; on an arithmetic grid of step $s$ containing $k - s$, the window is exactly $(k - s, k]$, of width $s$.

**Corollary 2.3 (Grid rounding).** Define the quantisation operator $\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa) = s \lceil \kappa / s \rceil$. If the true crossing point is $\kappa$ and the sweep grid is $s\mathbb{N}$, the reported knee is $\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa)$, and conversely $\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa) = k$ implies $\kappa \in (k - s, k]$.

We shall use the following two-sided bracketing of $\mathrm{Q}_s$ throughout; both halves are immediate from the definition of the ceiling.

**Lemma 2.4 (Bracketing).** *Let $s > 0$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$. (i) If $\kappa \le s n$ then $\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa) \le s n$. (ii) If $s n < \kappa$ then $s(n+1) \le \mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa)$. (iii) Hence if $s n < \kappa \le s(n+1)$ then $\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa) = s(n+1)$.*

## 3. Amplitude windows

The mechanism proposed for the product law derives the knee from the tail amplitude of the (approximately Zipfian) attention-score distribution: the knee is
$$\kappa(\mathrm{ctx}) = A \cdot \frac{d \cdot \mathrm{ctx}}{\delta},$$
a purely multiplicative law with a *single* fitted constant $A$. At $d = 4$, $\delta$ fixed and $\mathrm{ctx} = 128 \cdot 2^{i}$, this reads $\kappa(i) = A \cdot 2^{i}$, with the product law itself corresponding to $A = 16$.

**Definition 3.1 (Explaining a rung).** For reals $p < k$, a rung index $i$, and an amplitude $A$, say *$A$ explains the rung $(p, k, i)$* if
$$p < A \cdot 2^{i} \le k .$$

By Theorem 2.2, if the law with amplitude $A$ generates the data at rung $i$ then $A$ explains that rung; this is the only direction we need, and it makes every subsequent refutation a refutation of the *law*, not of an auxiliary hypothesis.

**Lemma 3.2 (Windows).** *$A$ explains $(p,k,i)$ if and only if $p/2^{i} < A \le k/2^{i}$.*

*Proof.* Divide by $2^{i} > 0$. $\square$

Applying Lemma 3.2 to the four measured rungs (each with $p = k - 32$):

| seed | $\mathrm{ctx}$ | rung $i$ | $p$ | $k$ | window $\left(p/2^{i},\, k/2^{i}\right]$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1024 | 3 | 96 | 128 | $(12, 16]$ |
| 1 | 2048 | 4 | 224 | 256 | $(14, 16]$ |
| 2 | 1024 | 3 | 64 | 96 | $(8, 12]$ |
| 2 | 2048 | 4 | 192 | 224 | $(12, 14]$ |

Note the arithmetic: $64/8 = 8$, $96/8 = 12$, $192/16 = 12$, $224/16 = 14$, $128/8 = 16$, $256/16 = 16$. The window at rung $i$ has width $32/2^{i}$: $4$ at rung $3$, $2$ at rung $4$.

## 4. The second seed admits no amplitude

**Theorem 4.1 (Amplitude Conflict).** *There is no real $A$ explaining both second-seed rungs; that is, no $A$ satisfies*
$$64 < 8A \le 96 \quad\text{and}\quad 192 < 16A \le 224 .$$

*Proof.* The first conjunction gives $A \le 12$; the second gives $A > 12$. $\square$

Equivalently, the windows $(8,12]$ and $(12,14]$ are disjoint — and *adjacent*: they abut exactly at $A = 12$ without sharing it. This is the sharpest possible form of "the second seed drifts": it drifts by different amounts at the two rungs, and no single re-calibration of the mechanism's constant reproduces both.

**Interpretation.** The natural rescue of the product law in the face of a second-seed drop is *"the law is right, only the amplitude fluctuates between seeds"*. Theorem 4.1 forecloses it. Under that rescue, the second seed would be a run of the law with some $A_2 < 16$; but no $A_2$ produces $(96, 224)$ at rungs $(3,4)$. Hence at most one of the two broken cells is a run of the law, and the other is an outlier. The announced "systematic drift" is therefore unavailable as a mechanism-level reading of the data.

**Theorem 4.2 (One outlier suffices).** *Each broken rung is separately explainable: $A = 10$ explains $(64, 96, 3)$, since $64 < 80 \le 96$; and $A = 13$ explains $(192, 224, 4)$, since $192 < 208 \le 224$.*

Thus the conflict of Theorem 4.1 is not the degeneracy of an over-constrained model: it is a genuine two-point obstruction, and removing either point restores consistency. Two live hypotheses remain — "the $1024$ cell is the law" ($A \in (8,12]$) and "the $2048$ cell is the law" ($A \in (12,14]$) — and they are distinguished by exactly one further measurement.

**Corollary 4.3 (The conflict is not a resolution problem).** *Both second-seed windows already have the minimal width the design permits at their rung ($4$ and $2$ respectively), and they are disjoint. No increase of context at either seed can make them overlap; only a third seed can adjudicate.*

**Theorem 4.4 (Cross-seed conflict at the longest cell).** *No amplitude explains both the second-seed and the first-seed rung at $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$, since $(12,14] \cap (14,16] = \emptyset$.*

So the inter-seed effect is real at the level of the mechanism's single constant, even though — by Theorem 4.1 — the second seed possesses no constant of its own.

## 5. Sharp identifiability at the first seed

**Theorem 5.1 (First-seed identifiability).** *An amplitude $A$ explains both first-seed rungs $(96, 128, 3)$ and $(224, 256, 4)$ if and only if*
$$14 < A \le 16 .$$

*Proof.* Explaining rung $3$ means $96 < 8A \le 128$, i.e. $12 < A \le 16$; explaining rung $4$ means $224 < 16A \le 256$, i.e. $14 < A \le 16$. The intersection is $(14,16]$, and conversely any $A$ in $(14,16]$ satisfies both. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2.** *The product law's own amplitude $A = 16$ explains the first-seed chain.* Indeed $8 \cdot 16 = 128 \in (96, 128]$ and $16 \cdot 16 = 256 \in (224, 256]$.

Theorem 5.1 is a genuine identifiability statement: not merely "the data are consistent with the law", but "the data determine the constant, two-sidedly, to relative precision $2/16 = 12.5\%$". Moreover, by the resolution theory of §10 this is exactly the precision the design allows at rung $4$: the first seed extracted all the information the grid contains.

## 6. The decisive experiment

The three surviving hypotheses — first-seed amplitude $A_0 \in (14,16]$, second-seed-at-$1024$ amplitude $A_1 \in (8,12]$, second-seed-at-$2048$ amplitude $A_2 \in (12,14]$ — make sharply different predictions at the next rung $i = 5$, i.e. $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$, on the same step-$32$ grid. Write $\mathrm{Q}(x) = \mathrm{Q}_{32}(x)$.

**Theorem 6.1 (First-seed prediction).** *If $14 < A_0 \le 16$ then $480 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_0 \cdot 2^{5}) \le 512$.*

*Proof.* $A_0 \cdot 32 > 448 = 32 \cdot 14$, so by Lemma 2.4(ii) $\mathrm{Q}(A_0 \cdot 32) \ge 32 \cdot 15 = 480$. Also $A_0 \cdot 32 \le 512 = 32 \cdot 16$, so by Lemma 2.4(i) $\mathrm{Q}(A_0 \cdot 32) \le 512$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.2 (Second-seed predictions).** *If $8 < A_1 \le 12$ then $\mathrm{Q}(A_1 \cdot 2^{5}) \le 384$. If $12 < A_2 \le 14$ then $416 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_2 \cdot 2^{5}) \le 448$.*

*Proof.* $A_1 \cdot 32 \le 384 = 32\cdot 12$ gives the first by Lemma 2.4(i). For the second, $A_2 \cdot 32 > 384$ gives $\mathrm{Q} \ge 416$ by Lemma 2.4(ii), and $A_2 \cdot 32 \le 448$ gives $\mathrm{Q} \le 448$ by Lemma 2.4(i). $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Three-way separation).** *Under the hypotheses above,*
$$\mathrm{Q}(A_1 \cdot 2^{5}) + 32 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_2 \cdot 2^{5}) \quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{Q}(A_2 \cdot 2^{5}) + 32 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_0 \cdot 2^{5}).$$

*Proof.* Combine $\mathrm{Q}(A_1 \cdot 32) \le 384$, $416 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_2 \cdot 32) \le 448$, and $480 \le \mathrm{Q}(A_0 \cdot 32)$. $\square$

The three predicted intervals $[\,\cdot\,, 384]$, $[416,448]$, $[480,512]$ are therefore pairwise separated by at least one full grid step. This matters because — as §8 shows — the $224$-versus-$256$ reading at $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$ is *not* robust at the grid's resolution. Rung $5$ is the first cell of the programme where a single run adjudicates between the competing hypotheses at the resolution the grid itself provides:

* a report $\le 384$ refutes the $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$ window;
* a report in $[416,448]$ confirms it and marks the $\mathrm{ctx} = 1024$ cell as the outlier;
* a report $\ge 480$ refutes *both* second-seed windows in favour of the first-seed amplitude, i.e. says the second-seed drops were noise all along.

## 7. What the ladder certifies

**Theorem 7.1 (Universal upper bound).** *Every amplitude explaining any one of the four measured rungs satisfies $A \le 16$.*

*Proof.* The four upper constraints are $8A \le 96$, $16A \le 224$, $8A \le 128$, $16A \le 256$, i.e. $A \le 12, 14, 16, 16$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Universal lower bound).** *Every amplitude explaining any one of the four measured rungs satisfies $A > 8$.*

*Proof.* The four lower constraints are $8A > 64$, $16A > 192$, $8A > 96$, $16A > 224$, i.e. $A > 8, 12, 12, 14$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Sharpness).** *The band $(8, 16]$ is attained at both ends by the data: for every $0 < \varepsilon < 4$, the amplitude $8 + \varepsilon$ explains the second-seed $\mathrm{ctx} = 1024$ rung, and $A = 16$ explains the first-seed $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$ rung. Hence no narrower band contains all four measured windows.*

**Deployment reading.** The product-law budget $d \cdot \mathrm{ctx} / 32$ corresponds to $A = 16$; by Theorem 7.1 the true crossing point never exceeds it, at either seed and at every measured rung. The law's *safety* claim ("this budget suffices to hold $98\%$ of full accuracy") is therefore intact — it is only the law's *exactness* that fails, and only at the second seed and long context. Writing the speedup of top-$k$ attention against full attention as $\mathrm{ctx}/k$, the certified band $A \in (8,16]$ says the attainable speedup lies between $8\times$ and $16\times$; the guaranteed figure is $8\times$, and the measured second-seed knee at $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$ gives $2048/224 = 64/7 \approx 9.14\times$.

## 8. Adversarial analysis of the drop

Theorems 4.1–4.4 take the reported knees at face value. It is equally important to ask how much the reported $224$ is worth.

### 8.1 Resolution

The margin at the reported knee is $0.982 - 0.980 = 0.002$; the deficit at the preceding swept budget $192$ is $0.980 - 0.978 = 0.002$. Call $\rho = 0.002$ the *resolution* of the round.

**Definition 8.1 (Robust knee).** $k$ is a *$\rho$-robust knee* of $C$ on $G$ at $b$ if $k$ is the knee of every curve $\tilde C$ with $\sup_{j\in G} |\tilde C(j) - C(j)| \le \rho$.

**Theorem 8.2 (Non-robustness).** *The reported knee $224$ is not a $\rho$-robust knee at $\rho = 0.002$: an upward perturbation of size $\rho$ at $k = 192$ makes $192$ clear the bar, moving the knee.*

Since the round's own measured inter-seed spread at the deciding budget is $0.006 > \rho$, the reading $k^{*} = 224$ is a reading of the grid at the experiment's noise floor, not a certified quantity.

### 8.2 The drop is what the recorded noise predicts

At $k = 224$ the first seed scored $0.976$, a deficit of $0.004$ from the bar, while the measured inter-seed spread at that budget was $0.006$.

**Theorem 8.3 (Shift artefact).** *Let $C_1$ be the first-seed curve with $C_1(224) = 0.976$, and let $C'$ satisfy $C'(224) \ge C_1(224) + 0.006$. Then the knee of $C'$ on the sweep grid is at most $224$.*

*Proof.* $C'(224) \ge 0.982 > 0.980 = b$, so $224$ clears the bar and the knee, being minimal among passing grid points, is at most $224$. $\square$

In words: *any* upward perturbation of the first-seed curve of the size the experiment itself records already reproduces the "drop". The second seed's $224$ is therefore predicted by the noise level measured at the previous rung and carries no information beyond it. (An explicit witness: if additionally $C_1(192) \le 0.973$, the shifted curve $C_1 + 0.006$ has knee exactly $224$.)

### 8.3 Quantisation manufactures one-step drops everywhere

**Theorem 8.4 (Universal one-step fluctuation).** *For every grid step $s > 0$, every $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exist crossing points $\kappa_1, \kappa_2 \ge 0$ with $|\kappa_1 - \kappa_2| \le \varepsilon$ and*
$$\mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa_2) = s n, \qquad \mathrm{Q}_s(\kappa_1) = s(n+1).$$

*Proof.* Take $\kappa_2 = sn$ and $\kappa_1 = sn + \tfrac{1}{2}\min(\varepsilon, s)$. Then $\lceil \kappa_2/s\rceil = n$, while $n < \kappa_1/s \le n + 1/2 < n+1$ forces $\lceil \kappa_1/s \rceil = n+1$; and $|\kappa_1 - \kappa_2| = \tfrac{1}{2}\min(\varepsilon,s) \le \varepsilon$. $\square$

With $s = 32$, the case $n = 3$ is the pair $(96, 128)$ recorded at $\mathrm{ctx} = 1024$ and the case $n = 7$ is the pair $(224, 256)$ recorded at $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$. The two "independent replications" are two instances of a single lemma about rounding; the phenomenon is available at every grid multiple, for arbitrarily small underlying differences.

### 8.4 Two cells cannot establish "systematic"

Under a null model in which each of the two broken rungs independently drops or does not with probability $1/2$, the observed pattern "drop at both" is one of four equally likely outcomes:

$$\Pr[\text{drop at both}] = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25 > 0.05 .$$

The replication therefore fails to reach any conventional significance level. A third seed at $\mathrm{ctx} = 1024$ would move the null probability to $1/8$; this is a second, independent reason why the highest-value open measurement is a third seed at the earlier rung rather than a longer run.

### 8.5 The sign is forced; the magnitude is not

The second-seed curve lies uniformly above the first-seed curve ($0.956$ vs $0.939$ at $k = 96$; $0.982$ vs $0.976$ at $k = 224$). This *entails* the direction of the effect.

**Theorem 8.5 (Domination forces the sign).** *If $C_1(j) \le C_2(j)$ for all $j \in G$, and $k_1, k_2$ are the respective knees on $G$ at bar $b$, then $k_2 \le k_1$.*

*Proof.* $b \le C_1(k_1) \le C_2(k_1)$, so $k_1$ is a passing grid point for $C_2$; minimality gives $k_2 \le k_1$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.6 (Domination does not force a drop).** *There exist monotone curves with $C_1(j) < C_2(j)$ for all $j$ whose knees on the sweep grid coincide.* (For instance the step curves equal to $0.90$ and $0.91$ for $k \le 224$ and to $0.99$ and $0.995$ thereafter, both with knee $256$.)

So the observation that "the whole second-seed curve sits higher" already predicts $k^{*}(\text{seed }2) \le k^{*}(\text{seed }1)$ as a theorem, and the empirical content of the round is confined to the *magnitude* of the gap — which, by Theorem 8.2, is known only at the grid's resolution.

## 9. Asymptotics of the ladder

Write $P_i = 16\cdot 2^{i}$ for the prediction, $K_i$ for the second-seed knee, and $s = 32$.

**Theorem 9.1 (Bounded additive drift).** *For every rung, $P_i - K_i \in \{0, s\}$: the deficit is either nothing or exactly one grid step. In particular the bracket law*
$$P_i - 32 \;\le\; K_i \;\le\; P_i$$
*holds at every rung and at both seeds.*

**Theorem 9.2 (No affine law).** *There are no reals $a, b$ with $K_i = a\,\mathrm{ctx}_i + b$ for $i \le 4$.*

*Proof.* $K_0 = 16$ at $\mathrm{ctx} = 128$ and $K_1 = 32$ at $\mathrm{ctx} = 256$ force $a = 1/8$, $b = 0$; then $i = 3$ predicts $128$, but $K_3 = 96$. $\square$

**Theorem 9.3 (No doubling law).** *If $f$ satisfies $f(2n) = 2f(n)$ for all $n$ and $f(1024) = 96$, then $f(2048) = 192 \ne 224$.*

So the second-seed chain is not a scaling law of either standard shape; the bracket of Theorem 9.1 is the strongest invariant that survives.

**Theorem 9.4 (Sub-linearity of the drift).** *For $i \ge 3$,*
$$\frac{K_i}{P_i} = 1 - 2\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i} \xrightarrow[i \to \infty]{} 1 .$$
*Numerically $K_3/P_3 = 3/4$ and $K_4/P_4 = 7/8$: the relative correction halves at every doubling.*

*Proof.* For $i \ge 3$, $K_i = 16\cdot 2^{i} - 32$, so $K_i/P_i = 1 - 32/(16\cdot 2^{i}) = 1 - 2\cdot 2^{-i}$. $\square$

**Theorem 9.5 (The speedup window collapses).** *With speedup defined as $\mathrm{ctx}_i / K_i$,*
$$\frac{\mathrm{ctx}_i}{K_i} = \frac{8}{1 - 2\left(\tfrac12\right)^{i}} \xrightarrow[i\to\infty]{} 8 .$$

*Proof.* $\mathrm{ctx}_i = 128 \cdot 2^{i} = 8 P_i$, so $\mathrm{ctx}_i/K_i = 8 P_i/K_i$, and apply Theorem 9.4. $\square$

At $\mathrm{ctx} = 2048$ this gives $2048/224 = 64/7 < 9.15$, and any knee in the two-seed bracket $(192, 256]$ yields a speedup in $[8, 32/3)$. Theorem 9.5 says the excess above the guaranteed $8\times$ halves at every further doubling: the observed $9.1\times$ best case is a transient. If — as the bracket law suggests — the deficit remains bounded by one grid step forever, then the "systematic sub-linear break" is a vanishing correction to an asymptotically exact law, which is simultaneously the strongest available rescue of the product law and a deflation of the deployment upside.

## 10. Resolution theory of a doubling sweep

### 10.1 The knee is a left adjoint

**Definition 10.1.** For a monotone curve $c : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ and a bar $b$, put $\mathrm{Knee}_c(b) = \inf\{k \in \mathbb{N} : b \le c(k)\}$.

**Theorem 10.2 (Adjunction).** *If $c$ is monotone and the bar is attained by some budget, then for every $k$,*
$$\mathrm{Knee}_c(b) \le k \iff b \le c(k).$$
*Hence $\mathrm{Knee}_c \dashv c$ is a Galois connection between bars and budgets.*

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) The infimum is attained, so $b \le c(\mathrm{Knee}_c(b)) \le c(k)$ by monotonicity. ($\Leftarrow$) $k$ belongs to the set whose infimum is taken. $\square$

Formal consequences, each of which is an empirical-sounding fact about the experiment:

* **Monotonicity in the bar.** Raising the bar cannot lower the knee (a left adjoint is monotone).
* **Antitonicity in the curve.** If $c \le c'$ pointwise then $\mathrm{Knee}_{c'}(b) \le \mathrm{Knee}_c(b)$: this is Theorem 8.5 in grid-free form, and the reason "the second-seed curve sits higher" forces the direction of the drop.
* **Only upper bounds are certifiable.** The right-to-left reading of the adjunction is the only inference a passing sweep point supports: a passing budget bounds the knee above. Lower bounds require *failing* points, and hence the grid's granularity.

**Theorem 10.3 (Grid knee = rounding).** *For monotone $c$ with the bar attained, $k$ is the knee of $c$ on the grid $G$ at $b$ if and only if $k \in G$, $\mathrm{Knee}_c(b) \le k$, and $k \le j$ for every $j \in G$ with $\mathrm{Knee}_c(b) \le j$ — i.e. $k$ is the least grid point at or above the continuous knee.*

Thus the measured object carries no information about the curve beyond $\mathrm{Knee}_c(b)$, and every dispute about one grid step is a dispute about a rounding.

### 10.2 Upper bound: doubling ladders identify amplitudes

**Theorem 10.4 (Rung resolution).** *If $A$ and $A'$ both explain the rung $(k - s, k, i)$ then $|A - A'| < s/2^{i}$.*

*Proof.* Both $A2^{i}$ and $A'2^{i}$ lie in the interval $(k-s, k]$ of length $s$, so $|A - A'|2^{i} < s$. $\square$

**Theorem 10.5 (Exact identifiability in the limit).** *If $A$ and $A'$ explain every rung $i \in \mathbb{N}$ of a step-$s$ doubling ladder, then $A = A'$.*

*Proof.* Otherwise $|A - A'| > 0$; choose $n$ with $2^{n} > s/|A - A'|$ and contradict Theorem 10.4 at rung $n$. $\square$

An unbounded doubling ladder of *fixed-step* sweeps therefore determines a real constant exactly — an infinite-precision conclusion from a coarse grid, purchased by geometric growth of the measured quantity.

### 10.3 Lower bound: the rate is optimal up to a factor 2

**Theorem 10.6 (Indistinguishability).** *For every $N$ there is an amplitude $A \ne 16$ with $|A - 16| = 16/2^{N}$ which reproduces the product law's reported knee at every rung $j = 1, \dots, N$ of the step-$32$ sweep; namely $A = 16 - 16/2^{N}$.*

*Proof sketch.* The product law's reported knee at rung $j+1$ is $\mathrm{Q}_{32}(16\cdot 2^{j+1}) = 32\cdot 2^{j}$. For $A = 16 - 16/2^{N}$ and $j < N$,
$$A\cdot 2^{j+1} = 32\cdot 2^{j} - 32\cdot \frac{2^{j}}{2^{N}},$$
and since $0 < 2^{j}/2^{N} < 1$ this lies in the half-open interval $(32(2^{j}-1),\, 32\cdot 2^{j}]$; by Lemma 2.4(iii) it rounds to $32 \cdot 2^{j}$, the same reported knee. $\square$

**Corollary 10.7 (Matched rate).** *After $N$ doublings, a step-$32$ ladder pins the amplitude to a window of width $32/2^{N}$ and to no window narrower than $16/2^{N}$. The bounds match to a factor $2$; identification is geometric in the number of doublings and no faster.*

**Consequences for the ladder at hand.**

* The first-seed window $(14,16]$ has width $2 = 32/2^{4}$, exactly the rung-$4$ resolution: the $16\times$ cell extracted everything the design allows.
* Separating $A = 16$ from $A = 15.5$ — the finest distinction the first-seed data leaves open at the low end of its window — needs resolution below $1/2$, i.e. $32/2^{i} < 1/2$, i.e. $i \ge 6$, i.e. $\mathrm{ctx} = 128\cdot 2^{6} = 8192$: two further doublings.
* The second-seed conflict is, by contrast, *not* a resolution problem (Corollary 4.3): both windows are already as narrow as their rungs permit and they are disjoint.

## 11. Algorithms

The analysis is entirely constructive and yields three short procedures.

**(A) Knee extraction from a sweep.** Given a list of (budget, retained) pairs sorted by increasing budget and a bar $b$, return the first budget whose retained value is $\ge b$, together with its predecessor. Cost $O(|G|)$. The predecessor is not decorative: it is the lower end of the window and hence half the information in the measurement.

**(B) Amplitude window from a rung.** Given $(p, k, i)$, return the half-open interval $(p/2^{i},\, k/2^{i}]$. Intersecting windows across rungs is then interval intersection, with the intersection empty exactly when the rungs are incompatible; this reduces the whole of §§4–5 to $O(R)$ arithmetic over $R$ rungs.

**(C) Prediction and adjudication at the next rung.** Given a window $(\ell, u]$ and a target rung $i$ with grid step $s$, the reported knee lies in $[\mathrm{Q}_s(\ell + \epsilon),\, \mathrm{Q}_s(u\,2^{i})]$; concretely, the infimum of the prediction is $s\lceil \ell 2^{i}/s\rceil + s$ if $\ell 2^{i}$ is a grid multiple, and $s\lceil \ell 2^{i}/s \rceil$ otherwise, and the supremum is $s\lceil u2^{i}/s\rceil$. Two hypotheses are *decidable at rung $i$* when their predicted intervals are disjoint; the earliest such rung is found by scanning $i$ upwards. For the three hypotheses of §6 the earliest decisive rung is $i = 5$, i.e. $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$.

## 12. Discussion

### 12.1 What was actually shown

The round's claim was that a one-grid-step deficit at the second seed had replicated across a doubling of context and was therefore systematic. Three independent arguments deflate it, and they are of increasing strength.

*Statistical.* Two cells give a null probability of $1/4$ — not significance.

*Metrological.* The reading is not robust at the round's own $0.002$ resolution, and the drop is reproduced by shifting the first-seed curve by the inter-seed spread the round itself measured; the phenomenon is available at every grid multiple by pure quantisation.

*Structural, and the strongest.* Even granting the readings exactly, no single amplitude of the proposed mechanism produces both of them. This is not a statement about noise but about the model: the second-seed pair is *incompatible with the law it was supposed to perturb*. One can salvage the mechanism at either broken rung but not at both.

The positive counterpart is equally sharp: the first-seed chain identifies the amplitude to $(14,16]$, the tightest window the design permits; the safety bound $A \le 16$ holds at every measured rung and both seeds; the certified band $(8,16]$ is attained at both ends; and the deviation, whatever its source, is asymptotically invisible.

### 12.2 Methodological lessons

1. **Report windows, not knees.** A sweep result is an interval $(p, k]$. Discarding $p$ throws away the lower bound, which is where all the refutation power lives — the entire argument of §4 uses lower ends.
2. **Grid steps are not measurements.** A one-step difference is a difference in a rounding; Theorem 8.4 makes it available for arbitrarily small underlying changes.
3. **Test the mechanism, not the fit.** "Does the law with *some* constant reproduce these points?" is a sharper question than "is each point close to the prediction?", and it can be answered with interval arithmetic before any further compute is spent.
4. **Design for separation.** The value of the next experiment is the pairwise separation of the competing predictions at the grid's resolution. By that criterion the $\mathrm{ctx}=4096$ run is worth much more than another cell where the hypotheses' predictions differ by less than a grid step.

### 12.3 Limitations

The analysis assumes a monotone retained-quality curve. Non-monotone curves — plausible for very small budgets where pruning can act as a regulariser — would replace "the knee" by "the first crossing", and the Window Lemma's lower bound would then rely on the sweep's failing points only, which is still sufficient for everything above but requires care in restating. All statements concern the specific mechanism $\kappa = A\,d\,\mathrm{ctx}/\delta$; a law with a second free parameter (say, an additive offset) would not be refuted by Theorem 4.1 — but such a law is also not identifiable from four rungs at the available resolution, and the affine version is refuted independently by Theorem 9.2. Finally, "systematic" was never formalised by the original claim; we have interpreted it in the strongest reasonable way, as "generated by a single amplitude", and refuted it there.

## 13. Future directions

**C1. The amplitude-conflict conjecture (highest value).** A third seed at $\mathrm{ctx} = 1024$ reports a knee $k^{*}$ with $k^{*}/8 \in (12,14]$ — i.e. $k^{*} \in \{112, 128\}$ on a step-$16$ grid — rather than $96$. Equivalently, the second-seed $\mathrm{ctx}=1024$ cell, not the $\mathrm{ctx}=2048$ cell, is the outlier of the Amplitude Conflict Theorem. The two second-seed rungs are not two instances of one drifting constant: their windows $(8,12]$ and $(12,14]$ are disjoint, so exactly one of them can be generated by a multiplicative law and the "systematic" reading is provably unavailable. The conflict is a two-point obstruction, and one more measurement at the earlier rung resolves which point is anomalous. Since the obstruction is established and each rung is separately explainable, the experiment is a genuine fork with two live branches rather than a confirmation run.

**C2. The decisive-context conjecture.** A single second-seed run at $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$ reports a knee in $[416, 448]$. A report $\le 384$ instead refutes the $\mathrm{ctx}=2048$ amplitude window, and a report $\ge 480$ refutes both second-seed windows in favour of the first-seed amplitude. The three surviving hypotheses have predictions at the next rung that are pairwise separated by at least one full grid step — unlike the $224$-versus-$256$ reading, which is not robust at its own resolution. This is the first cell of the programme where the grid can actually adjudicate, and the separation theorem needs no new theory; only the run is missing.

**C3. The vanishing-drift conjecture.** For every seed, $k^{*}(\mathrm{ctx})\big/\big(d\,\mathrm{ctx}/32\big) \to 1$ as $\mathrm{ctx}\to\infty$, and the additive deficit $d\,\mathrm{ctx}/32 - k^{*}$ stays bounded by one grid step at every rung; concretely, at $\mathrm{ctx} = 4096$ and $8192$ the second-seed deficit is again exactly $32$, never $64$. A bounded additive drift against a geometrically growing knee is asymptotically invisible: the ratio law is exact in the limit and the deployable speedup collapses to the guaranteed $8\times$. What the programme calls a systematic sub-linear break would then be a *vanishing correction to an exact law* — the strongest possible rescue of the product law, and one that also predicts the $9.1\times$ best case is a transient.

**C4. Resolution-optimal design.** Given a fixed compute budget, choose grid steps per rung to minimise the width of the final intersected amplitude window. The matched-rate result says the window shrinks like $s/2^{N}$ with fixed $s$; refining $s$ at the *shortest* rungs is cheap and may dominate the gain from an extra doubling. A formal treatment would trade off run cost against window width and produce an optimal schedule.

**C5. Beyond one parameter.** Characterise the class of laws $\kappa(i) = A\,2^{i} + B$ (or $A\,2^{\alpha i}$) identifiable from $R$ rungs at step $s$, and determine the minimal $R$ at which the second-seed data become refutable in that larger class as well.

## 14. Conclusion

A coarse sweep measures a window, not a number. Taking that seriously converts a five-rung ladder of expensive experiments into interval arithmetic on a single constant, and the interval arithmetic settles what the ladder can and cannot say. It cannot say that the second seed's one-grid-step deficit is systematic: no amplitude of the proposed mechanism produces both broken rungs, so at most one of them is the law. It can say, sharply, that the first seed identifies the amplitude to $(14,16]$; that every measured rung certifies $8 < A \le 16$, hence a guaranteed $8\times$ and at most $16\times$ attention speedup; that the deviation, if the bracket law holds, vanishes in ratio; and that a doubling ladder learns a constant geometrically, at a rate matched to within a factor of $2$. It also says exactly which experiment to run next, and why that run — unlike the last one — will be decisive.

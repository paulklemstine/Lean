# Discrete Homotopy Groups of Quasisymmetric Cubical Sets: A Synthetic Model of the Fundamental Group

## Abstract

We develop a synthetic, purely combinatorial model of the discrete fundamental
group $\pi_1^{A}$ of a $1$-truncated cubical set, in the spirit of the homotopy
theory of cubical sets and the discrete homotopy theory of graphs. Given the
$1$-skeleton of a cubical set together with its two-dimensional cells, we choose
a spanning tree of the $1$-skeleton and *define* the discrete fundamental group
to be the group presented by the edge generators, modulo the relations that
collapse the tree edges and impose the boundary words of the filled squares.
Discrete homotopy of loops is precisely the congruence generated by these
relations. We prove four structural results. First, a collapse criterion: if
every edge generator is trivial in $\pi_1^{A}$, the group is trivial. Second, a
functoriality (monotonicity) theorem: an inclusion of relation sets
$R_1 \subseteq R_2$ — geometrically, filling additional squares — induces a
canonical *surjective* homomorphism $\pi_1^{A}(R_1) \twoheadrightarrow
\pi_1^{A}(R_2)$ preserving edge generators, so that filling can only create
null-homotopies and never destroy them. Third, we compute the smallest
nontrivial example: the boundary loop of a single *hollow* square is a
nontrivial element, and the group is free of rank one, i.e. isomorphic to
$\mathbb{Z}$, detected by an explicit winding-number homomorphism. Fourth, we
prove the collapse: *filling* the square with a $2$-cell trivializes the entire
group, and the filling map is a surjection onto the trivial group. Together
these results reproduce, in a fully rigorous synthetic setting, the qualitative
content of discrete homotopy theory: two-dimensional cells are the mechanism by
which loops become null-homotopic. We conclude with numerical illustrations,
algorithms for computing the invariant on small complexes, and a program of
higher-dimensional conjectures.

## 1. Introduction

The fundamental group is the most classical invariant distinguishing spaces up
to homotopy: it records the equivalence classes of based loops under continuous
deformation, with concatenation as the group operation. In the continuous
setting this invariant is defined analytically, through maps of intervals and
homotopies between them. Yet the spaces that appear in computation — grids,
meshes, digital images, discretized configuration spaces — are combinatorial
objects assembled from cells glued along faces. It is therefore natural to seek
a *synthetic* fundamental group: an invariant defined by finite combinatorial
data that nevertheless captures the homotopical content of the space.

**Cubical sets** are a particularly convenient class of such combinatorial
spaces. Instead of simplices (points, segments, triangles, tetrahedra) they are
built from *cubes*: vertices, edges, squares, solid cubes, and higher
hypercubes. Because a product of cubes is again a cube, cubical sets interact
smoothly with products and mapping spaces, which makes them the natural setting
for higher-dimensional rewriting, for digital topology (pixels and voxels are
literally cubes), and for the constructive homotopy theory that has recently
found applications in the semantics of type theory.

This paper isolates the one-dimensional heart of the theory and gives it a
complete, self-contained algebraic treatment. We work with $1$-truncated cubical
sets — vertices, edges, and squares — and we define the **discrete fundamental
group** $\pi_1^{A}$ through a group presentation obtained by the classical
spanning-tree / van Kampen bookkeeping. The definition is elementary, but the
theorems it supports are precisely the ones a good invariant should satisfy: a
collapse criterion, functoriality under cell attachment, a nontrivial base
computation, and an explicit example of a loop that is nontrivial when a square
is hollow and trivial once the square is filled.

Our contributions are:

1. A synthetic definition of $\pi_1^{A}$ as a presented group (Section 3).
2. A **collapse criterion** (Theorem 4.1): triviality of all edge generators
   forces triviality of the whole group.
3. A **functoriality / monotonicity theorem** (Theorem 5.1): filling additional
   squares induces a canonical surjection preserving generators. Homotopy can be
   created but never destroyed by filling.
4. A complete analysis of the **single-square example** (Section 6): the hollow
   square realizes $\mathbb{Z}$ (Theorem 6.1) and the filled square is trivial
   (Theorem 6.2), with the filling map an explicit surjection onto the trivial
   group (Theorem 6.3).

## 2. Cubical sets and discrete loops

We recall only the structure we need.

**Definition 2.1 (1-truncated cubical set).** A *$1$-truncated cubical set*
consists of a set $V$ of **vertices**, a set $E$ of **edges** each equipped with
a source and target in $V$, and a set $S$ of **squares** (2-cubes), each
equipped with an ordered list of four boundary edges forming a closed walk. The
pair $(V, E)$ is the **$1$-skeleton**.

**Definition 2.2 (loop).** A *loop based at a vertex $v$* is a closed edge-walk
$v \to v$, i.e. a finite sequence of edges (traversed forwards or backwards) that
begins and ends at $v$. Concatenation of loops based at $v$ is associative up to
reparametrization and admits inverses (traverse the walk backwards), so loops
based at $v$ form a group under concatenation once we quotient by the appropriate
notion of deformation.

**Definition 2.3 (discrete homotopy).** Two loops are *discretely homotopic* if
one can be transformed into the other by a finite sequence of elementary moves:
(i) cancellation of an edge immediately followed by its reverse, and (ii)
replacement of the boundary walk of a *filled* square by the trivial walk (or
vice versa). Discrete homotopy is an equivalence relation compatible with
concatenation.

The content of the theory is that Definition 2.3 has a compact algebraic
description, to which we now turn.

## 3. The synthetic discrete fundamental group

Fix a $1$-truncated cubical set. Let $\mathrm{Free}(E)$ denote the free group on
the edge set $E$; a based loop, read as a word in the edges (an edge traversed
backwards contributing the inverse letter), determines an element of
$\mathrm{Free}(E)$. Choose a **spanning tree** $T \subseteq E$ of the connected
$1$-skeleton: a maximal acyclic set of edges. Traversing a tree edge cannot
enclose a hole, so we impose the relation $e = 1$ for each $e \in T$. Each filled
square $s \in S$ contributes its **boundary word** $w_s \in \mathrm{Free}(E)$,
the product of its four boundary edges in order, and filling imposes $w_s = 1$.

**Definition 3.1 (discrete fundamental group).** Let
$$R \;=\; \{\, e : e \in T \,\} \;\cup\; \{\, w_s : s \in S \,\} \;\subseteq\; \mathrm{Free}(E)$$
be the set of relators. The **discrete fundamental group** of the cubical set is
the presented group
$$\pi_1^{A}(R) \;:=\; \langle\, E \mid R \,\rangle \;=\; \mathrm{Free}(E) / \langle\!\langle R \rangle\!\rangle,$$
the quotient of the free group on the edges by the normal closure of $R$. We
write $\mathrm{of} : E \to \pi_1^{A}(R)$ for the map sending each edge to its
class (a generator), and $\mathrm{mk} : \mathrm{Free}(E) \to \pi_1^{A}(R)$ for
the induced quotient homomorphism on words.

Two loops are discretely homotopic (Definition 2.3) if and only if their words
have the same image under $\mathrm{mk}$. This is the standard spanning-tree /
van Kampen description of the fundamental group of a $2$-complex, transported to
the synthetic cubical setting; we take it as the definition of $\pi_1^{A}$.

The universal property of a presented group is the tool we use throughout: to
define a homomorphism out of $\pi_1^{A}(R)$ into a group $G$, it suffices to
specify the image of each generator $\mathrm{of}(e)$ by a function $f : E \to G$
such that every relator $r \in R$ maps to $1$ under the induced free-group
homomorphism $\mathrm{Free}(E) \to G$. We denote the resulting homomorphism
$\mathrm{toGroup}(f)$.

## 4. The collapse criterion

Our first structural result is elementary but foundational: a discrete
fundamental group in which every *generator* is trivial is itself trivial. This
is the algebraic form of "every loop is null-homotopic implies the space is
$\pi_1$-trivial."

**Theorem 4.1 (collapse criterion).** Let $R \subseteq \mathrm{Free}(E)$ and
suppose $\mathrm{of}(a) = 1$ in $\pi_1^{A}(R)$ for every edge $a \in E$. Then
$\pi_1^{A}(R)$ is trivial (a subsingleton).

*Proof sketch.* The generators generate: the subgroup generated by the set
$\{\mathrm{of}(a) : a \in E\}$ is the whole group $\pi_1^{A}(R)$ — indeed, the
closure of the range of $\mathrm{of}$ is $\top$. On the other hand, by
hypothesis that range is contained in $\{1\}$, so its generated subgroup is the
trivial subgroup $\bot$. Hence $\top = \bot$ as subgroups, which means every
element lies in the trivial subgroup; any two elements are therefore equal, and
the group is a subsingleton. $\square$

The two ingredients — "generators generate" ($\top$) and "everything trivial
gives $\bot$" — are exactly what one expects from a presentation, and the proof
is a direct comparison of the two subgroups. This lemma is the workhorse behind
the filled-square computation in Section 6.

## 5. Functoriality: filling only creates null-homotopies

We now come to the central structural theorem. Filling additional squares means
enlarging the relator set. We show that such an enlargement induces a canonical
surjection of discrete fundamental groups that preserves edge generators — so
that filling can merge loop classes together (create null-homotopies) but never
separate them (never create new nontrivial loops).

**Theorem 5.1 (functoriality under $2$-cube inclusion).** Let $R_1 \subseteq R_2
\subseteq \mathrm{Free}(E)$. Then there is a group homomorphism
$$\varphi : \pi_1^{A}(R_1) \longrightarrow \pi_1^{A}(R_2)$$
such that:
1. $\varphi(\mathrm{of}(a)) = \mathrm{of}(a)$ for every edge $a \in E$; and
2. $\varphi$ is surjective.

*Proof sketch.* To build $\varphi$ we use the universal property of the source
presentation. Consider the composite $E \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{of}\ }
\pi_1^{A}(R_2)$ and its induced free-group homomorphism $\mathrm{Free}(E) \to
\pi_1^{A}(R_2)$; this induced map is exactly the quotient map $\mathrm{mk}_{R_2}$
(they agree on generators, hence everywhere). We must check that every relator
$r \in R_1$ dies in the target: since $R_1 \subseteq R_2$, each such $r$ lies in
$R_2$ and therefore $\mathrm{mk}_{R_2}(r) = 1$. The universal property then
yields a homomorphism $\varphi = \mathrm{toGroup}$ with $\varphi(\mathrm{of}(a))
= \mathrm{of}(a)$, establishing (1).

For surjectivity (2), it suffices to show the image contains a generating set of
the target. The range of $\varphi$ is a subgroup containing every generator
$\mathrm{of}(a) = \varphi(\mathrm{of}(a))$; since the generators generate
$\pi_1^{A}(R_2)$ (the closure of the range of $\mathrm{of}$ is $\top$), the
image is all of $\pi_1^{A}(R_2)$. $\square$

**Remark 5.2 (asymmetry of filling).** Theorem 5.1 encodes a genuine directional
principle. A surjection has a possibly nontrivial kernel — it can identify
distinct loop classes — but it is never injective-failing in the other
direction: it cannot produce an element in the target that has no preimage.
Geometrically, *attaching cells can only add relations, hence only collapse
homotopy classes together; it can never resurrect a class that was already
trivial.* Homotopy is monotone-decreasing under filling.

## 6. The single square: hollow versus filled

We now exhibit the smallest example that separates "hollow" from "filled". Model
one square by its four boundary edges, indexed by $\{0,1,2,3\}$. Take the three
edges $0,1,2$ to be the spanning-tree edges (collapsed to $1$), leaving edge $3$
as the one honest generator that closes the loop.

Write $\mathrm{Free}(\{0,1,2,3\})$ for the free group on the four edges and
$g_i := \mathrm{of}(i)$ for the generators.

### 6.1 The hollow square

**Hollow relators.** With the square unfilled, the only relators are the three
tree edges:
$$R_{\text{hollow}} = \{\, g_0,\; g_1,\; g_2 \,\} \qquad\text{(as words } \mathrm{of}(0),\mathrm{of}(1),\mathrm{of}(2)\text{)}.$$
The presented group $\langle g_0,g_1,g_2,g_3 \mid g_0=g_1=g_2=1\rangle$ is free
on the single remaining generator $g_3$, i.e. isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Theorem 6.1 (the hollow square detects a hole).** $\pi_1^{A}(R_{\text{hollow}})$
is nontrivial. In fact it is infinite cyclic, generated by the boundary class
$g_3$.

*Proof sketch.* We construct an explicit "winding-number" homomorphism
$$w : \pi_1^{A}(R_{\text{hollow}}) \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}, \qquad
w(g_0) = w(g_1) = w(g_2) = 0, \quad w(g_3) = 1.$$
This is well-defined by the universal property: the induced free-group map sends
each relator $g_0, g_1, g_2$ to $0$, since each of those generators is sent to
$0$. Now $w(g_3) = 1 \neq 0 = w(1)$, so $g_3 \neq 1$ in the group; hence the
group is nontrivial. Combined with the observation that the tree relations kill
$g_0,g_1,g_2$ and leave $g_3$ free, $w$ is in fact an isomorphism onto
$\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

The homomorphism $w$ is literally a signed count of how many times a loop word
traverses edge $3$; it is the algebraic winding number around the missing
square. Its existence is what makes the nontriviality rigorous rather than
merely plausible.

### 6.2 The filled square

**Filled relators.** Filling the square adds its boundary word
$b := g_0\, g_1\, g_2\, g_3$ to the relators:
$$R_{\text{filled}} = R_{\text{hollow}} \cup \{\, b \,\} = \{\, g_0,\; g_1,\; g_2,\; g_0 g_1 g_2 g_3 \,\}.$$

**Theorem 6.2 (the filled square is contractible).**
$\pi_1^{A}(R_{\text{filled}})$ is trivial (a subsingleton).

*Proof sketch.* We verify the hypothesis of the collapse criterion
(Theorem 4.1): every generator is trivial. Directly, $g_0 = g_1 = g_2 = 1$ from
the three tree relators. For $g_3$, expand the boundary relator: in the quotient
$$1 = \mathrm{mk}(b) = \mathrm{mk}(g_0)\,\mathrm{mk}(g_1)\,\mathrm{mk}(g_2)\,\mathrm{mk}(g_3)
= 1 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot g_3 = g_3,$$
using $\mathrm{mk}(b) = 1$ (it is a relator), multiplicativity of $\mathrm{mk}$,
and the already-established triviality of $g_0,g_1,g_2$. Thus $g_3 = 1$ as well.
All four generators are trivial, so by Theorem 4.1 the group is trivial.
$\square$

### 6.3 Filling as a collapse map

The hollow and filled cases are linked by an instance of the functoriality
theorem, since $R_{\text{hollow}} \subseteq R_{\text{filled}}$.

**Theorem 6.3 (filling collapses).** There is a surjective homomorphism
$$\varphi : \pi_1^{A}(R_{\text{hollow}}) \twoheadrightarrow \pi_1^{A}(R_{\text{filled}})$$
preserving edge generators. Its target is trivial, and $\varphi$ sends the
boundary generator $g_3$ (a generator of $\mathbb{Z}$) to $1$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply Theorem 5.1 to the inclusion $R_{\text{hollow}} \subseteq
R_{\text{filled}}$ to obtain the surjection $\varphi$ with $\varphi(g_i) = g_i$.
By Theorem 6.2 the target is trivial, so in particular $\varphi(g_3) = 1$. Thus
$\varphi$ is the (necessarily surjective) quotient $\mathbb{Z} \to 1$ crushing
the winding-number generator. $\square$

**Interpretation.** Theorems 6.1–6.3 make the headline phenomenon precise: *the
same loop* — the boundary of the square — represents a nontrivial element
($g_3 \sim \mathbb{Z}$) when the square is hollow and the trivial element once
the square is filled. Two-dimensional cells are exactly the mechanism by which
one-dimensional loops become null-homotopic, and the transition is realized by a
canonical surjection, not by an accidental coincidence.

## 7. Algorithms

The synthetic definition is directly computable. We record the two core
algorithms; type-hinted implementations accompany this paper.

**Algorithm A (winding number of a loop word).** Given a reduced word $w$ in the
edge generators and a distinguished non-tree edge $e$, return the signed number
of occurrences of $e$ (occurrences of $e$ count $+1$, of $e^{-1}$ count $-1$).
This evaluates the homomorphism $w \mapsto \mathbb{Z}$ of Theorem 6.1 and decides
whether a loop is null-homotopic in the hollow square. Complexity: linear in the
length of the word.

**Algorithm B (normal form / discrete homotopy decision).** Given the relator
set $R$ (tree edges plus filled-square boundary words) and two loop words,
decide whether they represent the same element of $\pi_1^{A}(R)$. For the finite
presentations arising from finite cubical sets one may (i) set each tree edge to
the identity, (ii) use the square boundary relations as rewriting rules oriented
to eliminate the closing generator, and (iii) freely reduce and compare. For the
single-square presentations of Section 6 this terminates immediately: in the
hollow case two words are equal iff they have the same winding number; in the
filled case all words are equal. Complexity for the single-square cases: linear;
in general, the decision problem is that of the underlying group presentation.

## 8. Applications and discussion

**Digital topology.** Cubical sets are the native language of pixelated and
voxelized data. The discrete fundamental group provides a rigorous notion of
"an uncontractible loop in a digital image" whose triviality is decided by
filling in the two-cells corresponding to solid regions — exactly the
hollow-versus-filled dichotomy of Section 6.

**Cell-by-cell model building.** Theorem 5.1 guarantees that as one assembles a
complex by attaching cells, the fundamental group transforms by surjections. This
monotonicity is precisely the property that lets one reason incrementally: adding
detail (more filled cells) can only simplify $\pi_1$, never complicate it.

**A rigorous shadow of the continuous theory.** The presentation $\langle E \mid
R\rangle$ is the classical presentation of the fundamental group of the
geometric realization of a $2$-complex. Our results therefore align the
synthetic invariant with the expected classical answers on the examples treated,
supporting the view that $\pi_1^{A}$ is a faithful combinatorial model rather
than an ad hoc construction.

## 9. Future directions

The one-dimensional theory pinned down here fixes an algebraic template for
climbing in dimension. We highlight the program.

**Higher discrete homotopy from higher cubes.** The second discrete homotopy
group of a cubical set should be computed by its three-dimensional cubes acting
as relations among the boundary relations of its squares: the quotient of the
module of syzygies of the square relations by the boundaries of the cubes. The
boundary of a single solid cube should be a discrete two-sphere with second
homotopy group $\mathbb{Z}$, while a fully filled cube is contractible. The
passage from loops to spheres mirrors the passage from relations to
relations-among-relations, so each cube dimension plays the role that generators,
relations, and syzygies play in a group presentation.

**A dimension-shifting surjection principle.** Attaching an $n$-cube to a set
already connected through dimension $n-1$ should induce a surjection on the
$(n-1)$-st discrete homotopy group with kernel normally generated by the boundary
class of the attached cube, and leave all lower groups unchanged. A cube kills
homotopy only in the dimension of its boundary and never disturbs lower
dimensions; attachment is monotone. Theorem 5.1 is the base instance.

**Symmetry as an involution acting on homotopy.** For a quasisymmetric cubical
set the coordinate-swapping symmetries of its cubes should act on the discrete
fundamental group by automorphisms, and the fundamental group of the symmetric
quotient should be the fixed-point subgroup of this action — starting with the
diagonal flip of a single square.

**A gluing (van Kampen) law.** There should be a discrete van Kampen theorem
computing the fundamental group of a cubical set assembled from two overlapping
subcomplexes as an amalgamated product over the fundamental group of their
overlap.

## 10. Conclusion

We have given a compact, self-contained synthetic model of the discrete
fundamental group of a $1$-truncated cubical set, defined it as a presented group
via spanning-tree collapse and square boundary words, and proved the four
structural facts that make it a genuine invariant: a collapse criterion,
functoriality (surjectivity) under cell attachment, and the exact
hollow-versus-filled computation showing that the boundary of a square is
nontrivial ($\cong \mathbb{Z}$) precisely until the square is filled, whereupon
the group collapses to a point. Two-dimensional cells are, rigorously, the
mechanism by which loops become null-homotopic, and filling is a monotone,
never-destructive operation on homotopy. The framework is elementary enough to
compute by hand yet structured enough to fix the template for a full
higher-dimensional theory.

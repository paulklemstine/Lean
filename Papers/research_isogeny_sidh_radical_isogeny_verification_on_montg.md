## Abstract

We study an explicit radical degree-two quotient construction for Montgomery curves over an arbitrary field. For the affine curve

$$
E_A:\quad y^2=x^3+Ax^2+x,
$$

and an input with $x\ne0$, we consider the rational evaluation

$$
X=x+x^{-1},\qquad Y=y(1-x^{-2}).
$$

We prove by field identities that the image lies on

$$
E_A^{(2)}:\quad Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

The target cubic factors as $(X+A)(X-2)(X+2)$, exposing three affine points of ordinate zero. We characterize the fibers of the quotient coordinate exactly: for nonzero $x$ and $z$, equality $x+x^{-1}=z+z^{-1}$ holds precisely when $x=z$ or $xz=1$. The complete affine output is invariant under the deck transformation $(x,y)\mapsto(x^{-1},-yx^{-2})$. We also isolate the radical normalization used for quadratic twists: if $r^2=B$, then $(x,y)\mapsto(x,ry)$ sends $By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x$ to $E_A$, injectively when $r\ne0$. This yields an end-to-end normalize-then-quotient algorithm. Finally, in a finite free transitive action of a commutative group on curve classes, we prove composition, reversal, connector recovery, and uniqueness principles for labeled evaluation steps. The results provide a self-contained affine algebraic foundation for degree-two radical-isogeny computations while clearly delineating the projective and supersingular extensions that remain.

## 1. Introduction

Isogeny-based cryptography organizes elliptic curves into networks connected by algebraic maps. Efficient implementations require formulas that are not only fast but also mathematically transparent: a point-evaluation routine should land on the advertised target curve, identify the intended quotient fibers, behave predictably under normalization, and compose coherently with other steps.

Montgomery coordinates are particularly well suited to explicit arithmetic. We work over a field $K$ and use the normalized affine Montgomery equation

$$
y^2=x^3+Ax^2+x=x(x^2+Ax+1),
$$

where $A\in K$. The point $(0,0)$ is visibly a point of order two whenever the cubic defines a nonsingular elliptic curve. Quotienting by the subgroup generated by this point should produce a degree-two map. On the affine chart away from $x=0$, the relevant formulas are

$$
(x,y)\longmapsto \left(x+x^{-1},\;y(1-x^{-2})\right).
$$

The first coordinate is invariant under reciprocal inversion. The second coordinate supplies exactly the correction needed to map the source cubic to

$$
Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

Our scope is intentionally precise. We establish identities over an arbitrary field, subject only to nonvanishing assumptions required by inversion. We do not assume that $K$ is finite, that the source cubic is nonsingular, or that the characteristic is odd for these affine identities. When geometric terms such as two-torsion or isogeny are used interpretively, nonsingularity and the usual characteristic conditions should be imposed. The algebraic theorems themselves remain valid more generally.

The paper has four aims. First, it proves correctness of the rational evaluation and explains its target factorization. Second, it determines the nonzero fibers and the associated deck symmetry exactly. Third, it proves correctness and injectivity of radical normalization for quadratic twists, then combines normalization and quotient evaluation into one algorithm. Fourth, it formulates elementary but useful laws for labeled steps in free transitive commutative group actions, the standard algebraic abstraction underlying isogeny-class navigation.

## 2. Algebraic setting and definitions

Let $K$ be a field and $A\in K$.

**Definition 2.1 (Normalized Montgomery affine curve).** The source affine curve $E_A$ is the set of pairs $(x,y)\in K^2$ satisfying

$$
y^2=x^3+Ax^2+x.
$$

No nonsingularity assumption is needed for the polynomial identities below. If one wishes to regard $E_A$ as an elliptic curve in characteristic different from two, the cubic must have distinct roots; in this normalized model that excludes the relevant degenerate parameters.

**Definition 2.2 (Degree-two quotient equation).** The target affine curve $E_A^{(2)}$ is the set of pairs $(X,Y)\in K^2$ satisfying

$$
Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

**Definition 2.3 (Radical degree-two evaluation).** For $x\ne0$, define

$$
\Phi_A(x,y)=\left(x+x^{-1},\;y(1-x^{-2})\right).
$$

Although the formula itself does not display $A$, its source and target equations do. The map is rational and has a pole on the affine chart at $x=0$.

**Definition 2.4 (Deck transformation on the nonzero chart).** For $x\ne0$, define

$$
\iota(x,y)=\left(x^{-1},-yx^{-2}\right).
$$

The terminology anticipates that $oldsymbol{\iota}$ exchanges the two sheets of the degree-two quotient. The exact invariance and fiber theorems below justify this interpretation algebraically.

**Definition 2.5 (Radical normalization).** Given $r\in K$, define

$$
N_r(x,y)=(x,ry).
$$

When $r^2=B$, this scaling relates the twisted equation

$$
By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x
$$

to the normalized equation defining $E_A$.

## 3. The quotient polynomial and its visible roots

We begin with a factorization that organizes the target geometry.

**Theorem 3.1 (Target factorization).** For every $A,X\in K$,

$$
X^3+AX^2-4X-4A=(X+A)(X-2)(X+2).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Multiply the last two factors to obtain $X^2-4$, then multiply by $X+A$:

$$
(X+A)(X^2-4)=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

The identity holds in every commutative ring and therefore in every field. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2 (Visible ordinate-zero points).** For every $A\in K$, each of

$$
(-A,0),\qquad (2,0),\qquad (-2,0)
$$

satisfies the target equation $E_A^{(2)}$.

**Proof sketch.** At each listed abscissa, one factor in Theorem 3.1 vanishes, so the right side of the target equation is zero, equal to $Y^2$ for $Y=0$. $\square$

On a nonsingular elliptic curve in characteristic different from two, affine points with ordinate zero are two-torsion points. The factorization thus exposes the target’s rational two-torsion whenever these coordinates are distinct in the field.

## 4. Affine correctness of the quotient formula

The principal calculation is the following.

**Theorem 4.1 (Affine correctness).** Let $(x,y)\in K^2$ satisfy

$$
y^2=x^3+Ax^2+x
$$

and suppose $x\ne0$. Define

$$
X=x+x^{-1},\qquad Y=y(1-x^{-2}).
$$

Then

$$
Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

Equivalently, $
\Phi_A$ maps every source point in the nonzero affine chart to the target equation.

**Proof sketch.** Squaring the output ordinate and substituting the source equation gives

$$
Y^2=y^2(1-x^{-2})^2
=(x^3+Ax^2+x)(1-x^{-2})^2.
$$

Since $x\ne0$, denominators may be cleared. Expand the right side:

$$
(x^3+Ax^2+x)(1-2x^{-2}+x^{-4}).
$$

The terms independent of $A$ regroup as

$$
x^3+3x+3x^{-1}+x^{-3}-4x-4x^{-1}
=(x+x^{-1})^3-4(x+x^{-1}),
$$

while the $A$-terms regroup as

$$
A(x^2-2+x^{-2})=A(x+x^{-1})^2-4A.
$$

Substituting $X=x+x^{-1}$ yields

$$
Y^2=X^3-4X+AX^2-4A,
$$

which is the claimed equation. $\square$

The theorem is purely affine: it does not assert extension at $x=0$, separability, degree as a morphism of smooth curves, or an exact projective kernel. Those are natural geometric continuations, discussed later.

## 5. Deck invariance and exact fibers

The first quotient coordinate is visibly invariant under inversion, but the complete statement includes the correct transformation of $y$.

**Theorem 5.1 (Complete deck invariance).** If $x\ne0$, then

$$
\Phi_A\!\left(x^{-1},-yx^{-2}\right)=\Phi_A(x,y).
$$

**Proof sketch.** For the first coordinate,

$$
x^{-1}+(x^{-1})^{-1}=x^{-1}+x=x+x^{-1}.
$$

For the second coordinate,

$$
(-yx^{-2})\left(1-(x^{-1})^{-2}\right)
=-yx^{-2}(1-x^2)
=y(1-x^{-2}).
$$

Thus both coordinates coincide. $\square$

The next theorem shows that inversion is not merely one source of collisions: it is the only one for the quotient abscissa on $K^\times$.

**Theorem 5.2 (Exact classification of nonzero quotient-coordinate fibers).** Let $x,z\in K^\times$. Then

$$
x+x^{-1}=z+z^{-1}
$$

if and only if

$$
x=z\quad\text{or}\quad xz=1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** For the forward direction, multiply the equality by $xz$, which is nonzero. Rearranging gives

$$
(x-z)(xz-1)=0.
$$

A field has no zero divisors, so $x-z=0$ or $xz-1=0$. Conversely, if $x=z$, equality is immediate. If $xz=1$, then $z=x^{-1}$ and reciprocal symmetry gives equality. $\square$

**Corollary 5.3 (Fiber sizes).** Every fiber of the map

$$
K^\times\longrightarrow K,\qquad x\longmapsto x+x^{-1}
$$

contains at most two elements. It contains one element exactly when $x=x^{-1}$, equivalently $x^2=1$, and otherwise consists of the pair $\{x,x^{-1}\}$.

**Proof sketch.** Theorem 5.2 restricts any member of the fiber to $x$ or $x^{-1}$. These coincide precisely at fixed points of inversion. $\square$

This exact statement is stronger than a generic “two-to-one” description because it records ramified or fixed fibers without imposing a characteristic hypothesis.

## 6. Images of source two-torsion roots

The source cubic factors as

$$
x(x^2+Ax+1).
$$

Thus, besides $(0,0)$, any nonzero ordinate-zero source point has abscissa solving $x^2+Ax+1=0$.

**Theorem 6.1 (Nonzero source torsion roots map to $-A$).** Suppose $x\ne0$ and

$$
x^2+Ax+1=0.
$$

Then

$$
x+x^{-1}=-A.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Divide the quadratic equation by $x$ to obtain

$$
x+A+x^{-1}=0.
$$

Rearrangement gives the result. $\square$

**Corollary 6.2 (Collapse of reciprocal roots).** If the quadratic $t^2+At+1$ has two nonzero roots $x$ and $z$, then $xz=1$ and both roots have quotient coordinate $-A$. Their ordinate-zero source points therefore map, wherever evaluated, to the target point $(-A,0)$.

**Proof sketch.** The product of the roots is $1$. Theorem 6.1 gives the common quotient coordinate, while the output ordinate is zero because the source ordinate is zero. Corollary 3.2 confirms that $(-A,0)$ lies on the target. $\square$

This behavior is consistent with quotienting by the visible source two-torsion point $(0,0)$: the remaining nonzero two-torsion points are paired by reciprocal inversion.

## 7. Radical normalization of quadratic twists

Explicit isogeny pipelines often begin from a scaled equation

$$
By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x.
$$

A chosen square root of $B$ transfers it to normalized Montgomery form.

**Theorem 7.1 (Correctness of radical normalization).** Let $A,B,r,x,y\in K$. If

$$
r^2=B
$$

and

$$
By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x,
$$

then $N_r(x,y)=(x,ry)$ lies on $E_A$; explicitly,

$$
(ry)^2=x^3+Ax^2+x.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Compute

$$
(ry)^2=r^2y^2=By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x.
$$

No division is needed. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Injectivity away from a zero radical).** If $r\ne0$, the map

$$
N_r:K^2\longrightarrow K^2,\qquad (x,y)\longmapsto(x,ry)
$$

is injective.

**Proof sketch.** If $(x,ry)=(z,rw)$, equality of first coordinates gives $x=z$. Equality $ry=rw$ and cancellation of nonzero $r$ gives $y=w$. $\square$

The nonzero condition is exact: if $r=0$, all points sharing an $x$-coordinate are sent to the same ordinate $0$. In the intended twist setting, $B\ne0$ implies any $r$ with $r^2=B$ is nonzero.

**Theorem 7.3 (End-to-end normalize-then-quotient correctness).** Suppose

$$
r^2=B,\qquad By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x,\qquad x\ne0.
$$

Define

$$
X=x+x^{-1},\qquad Y=ry(1-x^{-2}).
$$

Then

$$
Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

**Proof sketch.** By Theorem 7.1, $(x,ry)$ lies on the normalized source $E_A$. Apply Theorem 4.1 to this point. $\square$

## 8. Algorithms and computational complexity

### 8.1 Direct degree-two evaluation

Given a field supporting exact arithmetic, a point $(x,y)$ with $x\ne0$, and parameter $A$, the quotient evaluation is:

1. Compute $u=x^{-1}$.
2. Compute $X=x+u$.
3. Compute $Y=y(1-u^2)$.
4. Optionally check $Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A$.

Ignoring validation, the algorithm uses one inversion, two multiplications if squaring is counted as a multiplication, and a constant number of additions or subtractions. Thus it requires $O(1)$ field operations per point. In finite-field implementations, inversion is typically more expensive than multiplication; batch inversion can amortize this cost across many points.

### 8.2 Radical normalize-then-quotient evaluation

For a twisted input satisfying $By^2=x^3+Ax^2+x$ and a supplied $r$ with $r^2=B$:

1. Validate or assume $r^2=B$.
2. Compute $y'=ry$.
3. Apply direct degree-two evaluation to $(x,y')$.

This adds one field multiplication to direct evaluation. Finding $r$ is a separate square-root problem and is not included in the constant-time evaluation count. Over a finite field, square-root extraction depends on the field representation and modulus.

### 8.3 Fiber classification

Given nonzero $x,z$, equality of quotient coordinates can be decided without computing two inverses:

1. If $x=z$, return “same input.”
2. Otherwise compute $xz$.
3. If $xz=1$, return “reciprocal pair.”
4. Otherwise return “distinct fibers.”

This uses one multiplication after the equality test and follows directly from Theorem 5.2.

### 8.4 Finite-field demonstration strategy

Over a prime field $\mathbb F_p$, numerical examples can enumerate $x\in\mathbb F_p^\times$, determine whether $x^3+Ax^2+x$ is a square, and evaluate every resulting affine point. For each output, the target equation and deck invariance can be checked modulo $p$. Enumeration costs $O(p)$ candidate abscissas with a square-root routine; using brute-force square-root tables also costs $O(p)$ storage and preprocessing. This is intended for illustration, not production cryptography.

## 9. Labeled steps in free transitive commutative actions

We now separate local rational evaluation from the global algebra of navigating curve classes.

Let $G$ be a finite commutative group acting on a finite set $S$. Write $a\cdot C$ for the action of $a\in G$ on $C\in S$. Assume the action is *transitive*: for every $C,D\in S$, some $a$ satisfies $a\cdot C=D$. Assume it is *free*: if $a\cdot C=b\cdot C$, then $a=b$. Together these properties make the action simply transitive.

**Definition 9.1 (Verified labeled step).** A label $a\in G$ verifies a step from $C$ to $D$ when

$$
a\cdot C=D.
$$

Transitivity guarantees that at least one label connects any ordered pair; freeness guarantees uniqueness.

**Theorem 9.2 (Composition of verified steps).** If $a$ verifies a step from $C$ to $D$ and $b$ verifies a step from $D$ to $E$, then $ba$ verifies a step from $C$ to $E$.

**Proof sketch.** The action law gives

$$
(ba)\cdot C=b\cdot(a\cdot C)=b\cdot D=E.
$$

Commutativity is compatible with the class-group setting, though this two-step calculation only needs the group action law with the chosen multiplication convention. $\square$

**Theorem 9.3 (Reversal).** If $a$ verifies a step from $C$ to $D$, then $a^{-1}$ verifies a step from $D$ to $C$.

**Proof sketch.** Since $D=a\cdot C$,

$$
a^{-1}\cdot D=a^{-1}\cdot(a\cdot C)=(a^{-1}a)\cdot C=C.
$$

$\square$

**Definition 9.4 (Connector).** For $C,D\in S$, the connector $\kappa(C,D)$ is the unique element of $G$ satisfying

$$
\kappa(C,D)\cdot C=D.
$$

Existence follows from transitivity and uniqueness from freeness.

**Theorem 9.5 (Recovery of a verified label).** If $a$ verifies the step from $C$ to $D$, then

$$
\kappa(C,D)=a.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Both $
\kappa(C,D)$ and $a$ act on $C$ to produce $D$. Freeness forces equality. $\square$

**Theorem 9.6 (Uniqueness of verified labels).** If both $a$ and $b$ verify a step from $C$ to $D$, then $a=b$.

**Proof sketch.** This is the freeness condition applied to $a\cdot C=b\cdot C=D$, or equivalently follows by identifying both labels with the connector. $\square$

**Corollary 9.7 (Finite-chain label law).** For a chain

$$
C_0\xrightarrow{a_1}C_1\xrightarrow{a_2}\cdots\xrightarrow{a_n}C_n
$$

of verified steps, the product $a_n\cdots a_2a_1$ verifies the step from $C_0$ to $C_n$. Consequently, any two verified chains with the same endpoints have equal total products.

**Proof sketch.** Induct on the chain length using Theorem 9.2. Two total products with the same endpoints are equal by Theorem 9.6. $\square$

The final corollary is an immediate mathematical consequence of the step laws. It explains why intermediate curves may vary while the aggregate class-group element remains determined by the endpoints.

## 10. Applications and interpretation

### 10.1 Local evaluation in isogeny pipelines

Theorem 4.1 supplies a direct postcondition for the local arithmetic routine: an accepted source point with nonzero abscissa produces a point on the advertised target cubic. This can be used in testing, symbolic derivation, or defensive implementations that retain curve-membership checks at trust boundaries.

### 10.2 Compression of reciprocal pairs

Theorem 5.2 makes the quotient coordinate a canonical invariant of the involution $x\mapsto x^{-1}$. In protocols or data structures that need to identify reciprocal pairs, $X=x+x^{-1}$ acts as a compact orbit descriptor. The fixed cases $x^2=1$ must be treated separately because their orbits have one element.

### 10.3 Visibility of target torsion

The factorization in Theorem 3.1 makes three target roots explicit. Such factorizations can simplify subsequent low-degree steps because ordinate-zero points are immediately available. In characteristics where $2=-2$, roots merge, so geometric claims require the usual characteristic and nonsingularity checks even though the polynomial identity remains true.

### 10.4 Twist normalization

Theorems 7.1–7.3 cleanly isolate reliance on a chosen radical. Once $r^2=B$ is available, the rest of the quotient computation is ordinary field arithmetic. The injectivity theorem ensures that normalization by nonzero $r$ itself introduces no collisions. Different choices of square root generally differ by sign in characteristic not two, suggesting a target sign isomorphism; establishing the full projective independence statement is a natural next step.

### 10.5 Chain accounting

The group-action theorems provide an abstract audit layer for multi-step evaluation. Local curve formulas determine individual transitions, while labels multiply to describe the total walk. Reversibility and uniqueness prevent two different labels from truthfully advertising the same fixed transition in a free action.

## 11. Limitations and future work

The affine calculation deliberately stops short of several geometric claims. First, the rational formula should be extended across $x=0$ and the point at infinity to a morphism of smooth projective curves. Under characteristic different from two and nonsingularity, one expects a separable degree-two isogeny with kernel exactly the identity and $(0,0)$.

Second, the quotient equation is not presented in normalized Montgomery form. Converting it appropriately and quotienting by the image two-torsion point should produce the dual isogeny; the two maps should compose to multiplication by two.

Third, for a nonsingular supersingular Montgomery curve over $\mathbb F_{p^2}$ with odd prime $p$, the projective quotient should remain supersingular and have the same number of $\mathbb F_{p^2}$-rational points. This requires genuine elliptic-curve and finite-field geometry beyond the affine identities.

Fourth, if nonzero $r$ and $s$ satisfy $r^2=s^2=B$, the two normalize-then-quotient evaluations are expected to differ only by the sign isomorphism $(X,Y)\mapsto(X,-Y)$ when the roots are opposite. A precise statement should account for characteristic two and projective isomorphism classes.

Finally, Corollary 9.7 suggests a chain-level uniqueness theorem stated directly for arbitrary finite sequences, including heterogeneous intermediate curves and labels. The simple-transitivity argument already supplies its algebraic core; a full protocol treatment would connect each concrete local evaluation to the corresponding action label.

## 12. Reproducible numerical checks

For educational experiments over a prime field $\mathbb F_p$, one may create a square table $q\mapsto q^2$ modulo $p$, enumerate every nonzero $x$, and recover all ordinates whose squares equal $x^3+Ax^2+x$. Each recovered point should pass four independent checks: source membership, target membership after evaluation, equality with the deck-transformed output, and the reciprocal fiber criterion. Separately, choose $r$ and set $B=r^2$; replacing a normalized ordinate $y'$ by $y=r^{-1}y'$ creates a twisted input whose normalization returns $y'$.

These checks illustrate the universal theorems but do not replace their algebraic proofs. Exhaustive enumeration over one finite field establishes only that finite instance. Its value is diagnostic: it reveals mishandled modular signs, accidental use of integer rather than field division, omitted pole checks, and characteristic-dependent collisions among $-A$, $2$, and $-2$. For reproducibility, a demonstration should print the modulus and parameter, assert every identity, and use deterministic enumeration rather than randomness.

## 13. Conclusion

The rational formulas

$$
X=x+x^{-1},\qquad Y=y(1-x^{-2})
$$

provide a compact affine quotient of the normalized Montgomery equation

$$
y^2=x^3+Ax^2+x
$$

onto

$$
Y^2=X^3+AX^2-4X-4A.
$$

Their behavior is completely controlled on the nonzero chart. The full output is invariant under $(x,y)\mapsto(x^{-1},-yx^{-2})$, and equality of quotient abscissas occurs exactly for equal or reciprocal inputs. The target polynomial factors into three visible roots, while the nonzero source torsion roots map to $X=-A$. Radical scaling correctly and injectively normalizes quadratic twists when the chosen radical is nonzero, and the combined algorithm inherits the quotient correctness theorem. At the global level, free transitive commutative actions give exact laws for composing, reversing, recovering, and uniquely identifying step labels.

These results form a coherent affine foundation: explicit field formulas govern points, reciprocal symmetry governs fibers, radicals govern twist normalization, and group actions govern chains. The remaining projective, duality, and supersingularity questions are sharply identified and can be addressed without altering this algebraic core.

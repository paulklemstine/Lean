## Abstract

For the quadratic family $f_c(z)=z^2+c$ on the complex plane, we develop a self-contained algebraic account of iteration, exact periodicity, multipliers, primitive point counts, rational mediation, and escape growth. The fundamental composition identity implies that if a point has exact period $q$, then its return times are precisely the multiples of $q$; before the first return, its $q$ orbit points are distinct, and every point on the cycle shares the same period. The algebraic multiplier

$$
M_n(c,z)=2^n\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}f_c^j(z)
$$

satisfies the expected chain-rule recurrence. Every positive-length multiplier based at the critical point $0$ vanishes, so a periodic critical orbit is superattracting. We characterize fixed and genuine period-two points and compute the period-two multiplier explicitly. Möbius inversion gives the generic exact-period count

$$
\Psi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)2^d,
$$

with $\Psi(1)=2$, $\Psi(2)=2$, $\Psi(3)=6$, and $\Psi(n)\ge0$ for $n>0$. At prime period $p$, the count specializes to $2^p-2$, divisible by $p$ by Fermat’s little theorem; for $p\ge3$, the quotient is at least two. We also isolate the Fibonacci identity generated by Farey mediation and prove a one-step escape-growth criterion. These results clarify exactly which number-theoretic conclusions follow from quadratic recurrence and which proposed geometric claims require additional analytic or topological structure.

## 1. Introduction

The Mandelbrot set is the parameter set of the quadratic family

$$
f_c(z)=z^2+c,
$$

for which the forward orbit of the critical point $0$ remains bounded. Its familiar cardioid-and-bulb geometry invites arithmetic interpretations: periodic components carry integer periods, combinatorial rotation data are rational, and recurring patterns evoke continued fractions and Fibonacci numbers. Such observations are valuable only when several logically distinct layers are kept separate.

The first layer is pure iteration. It concerns identities valid for every complex parameter $c$ and every initial point $z$. Here exact period is rigid: the primitive period divides every return time, and an exact period-$q$ cycle contains exactly $q$ distinct points.

The second layer concerns local stability. Multipliers are products of derivatives along orbits. Since the quadratic map has derivative $2z$, any positive-length product beginning at the critical point contains a zero factor. Consequently, every periodic critical orbit is superattracting. This observation decisively fixes the Lyapunov behavior at component centers: under the standard logarithmic convention, the exponent is $-\infty$, not a generally finite trigonometric expression.

The third layer is enumerative. The degree of $f_c^n(z)-z$ is $2^n$, but this equation includes all periods dividing $n$. Möbius inversion extracts the primitive contribution. Prime periods are especially simple because a prime has only two positive divisors. Fermat’s little theorem then supplies the divisibility needed to pass from a number of periodic points to a number of cycles.

The fourth layer is parameter-space geometry. Statements identifying a component’s period from an internal angle, classifying component symmetries, or decomposing component topology according to prime factorization require definitions and theorems beyond the recurrence alone. We therefore state the algebraic conclusions at their natural level and explicitly distinguish them from geometric conjectures.

The presentation is organized as follows. Section 2 defines iteration and proves the composition law. Section 3 establishes the complete classification of return times and the cardinality of primitive cycles. Section 4 develops multipliers and low-period formulas. Section 5 studies dynatomic counts and prime periods. Section 6 explains Farey–Fibonacci arithmetic. Section 7 gives an escape-growth theorem and algorithms. Sections 8 and 9 discuss applications, limitations, and future work.

## 2. Quadratic iteration

### 2.1. Definitions

Fix $c,z\in\mathbb C$. Define the iterates recursively by

$$
f_c^0(z)=z,
$$

and

$$
f_c^{n+1}(z)=\bigl(f_c^n(z)\bigr)^2+c.
$$

The **critical orbit** or **Mandelbrot orbit** is the sequence

$$
z_n=f_c^n(0).
$$

Thus $z_0=0$, $z_1=c$, and $z_{n+1}=z_n^2+c$.

A point $z$ is **periodic with return time $n$** if $f_c^n(z)=z$. A positive integer $q$ is the **exact period** of $z$ if

1. $f_c^q(z)=z$;
2. for every integer $d$ with $0<d<q$, one has $f_c^d(z)\ne z$.

The adjective “exact” is essential: a point of exact period $q$ also returns after $2q$, $3q$, and so forth, but these are not primitive periods.

### 2.2. Composition

**Lemma 2.1 (Composition law).** For all nonnegative integers $m,n$,

$$
f_c^{m+n}(z)=f_c^n\bigl(f_c^m(z)\bigr).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Induct on $n$. At $n=0$, both sides equal $f_c^m(z)$. If the identity holds for $n$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_c^{m+n+1}(z)
&=\bigl(f_c^{m+n}(z)\bigr)^2+c\\
&=\left(f_c^n(f_c^m(z))\right)^2+c\\
&=f_c^{n+1}(f_c^m(z)).
\end{aligned}
$$

This closes the induction. $\square$

**Corollary 2.2 (Multiples are return times).** If $f_c^q(z)=z$, then

$$
f_c^{qk}(z)=z
$$

for every nonnegative integer $k$.

**Proof sketch.** Induct on $k$ and use the composition law to append one further block of $q$ iterations. $\square$

**Corollary 2.3 (Period propagates around an orbit).** If $f_c^q(z)=z$, then for every nonnegative integer $k$,

$$
f_c^q\bigl(f_c^k(z)\bigr)=f_c^k(z).
$$

**Proof sketch.** By composition, the left side is $f_c^{k+q}(z)$. Commuting the addition and composing in the opposite order reduces this to $f_c^k(f_c^q(z))=f_c^k(z)$. $\square$

## 3. Exact period and arithmetic rigidity

### 3.1. Divisibility of every return time

**Theorem 3.1 (Exact-period divisibility).** Suppose $z$ has exact period $q$. If $f_c^n(z)=z$, then $q$ divides $n$.

**Proof sketch.** Exact period includes $q>0$. Apply Euclidean division to write

$$
n=mq+r,\qquad 0\le r<q.
$$

By Corollary 2.2, $mq$ is a return time. The composition identity gives

$$
f_c^n(z)=f_c^r\bigl(f_c^{mq}(z)\bigr)=f_c^r(z).
$$

Since the left side is $z$, the remainder $r$ is itself a return time. Exactness excludes $0<r<q$, so $r=0$. Hence $n=mq$. $\square$

Combining this theorem with Corollary 2.2 gives the complete classification.

**Theorem 3.2 (Return-time classification).** If $z$ has exact period $q$, then for every nonnegative integer $n$,

$$
f_c^n(z)=z\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\mid n.
$$

This equivalence is the central arithmetic statement. It says that the return-time set is the additive submonoid $q\mathbb N$ and that $q$ can be recovered as its least positive element.

### 3.2. No collision before the first return

**Theorem 3.3 (Injectivity before return).** If $z$ has exact period $q$, the map

$$
i\longmapsto f_c^i(z)
$$

is injective on the indices $0\le i<q$.

**Proof sketch.** Suppose $0\le i<j<q$ and $f_c^i(z)=f_c^j(z)$. Advance both equal points by $q-j$ steps. Using composition and the $q$-step return, one obtains

$$
f_c^{q-j+i}(z)=z.
$$

The integer $q-j+i$ is positive and strictly smaller than $q$, contradicting exactness. Thus no two pre-return positions coincide. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4 (Orbit cardinality).** An exact period-$q$ orbit contains exactly $q$ distinct points before returning:

$$
\left|\{f_c^i(z):0\le i<q\}\right|=q.
$$

Theorems 3.2–3.4 supply the rigorous algebra behind period labels. If a dynamical or geometric construction independently establishes that a marked orbit has exact period $q$, then all its return times and its finite orbit cardinality are determined. Conversely, these theorems alone do not identify a component labeled by a rational angle with a particular period; that requires a definition of the labeling map and a theorem relating the denominator to dynamics.

## 4. Orbit multipliers and low periods

### 4.1. Product and multiplier

Define the **orbit product** by

$$
P_0(c,z)=1,
$$

and

$$
P_{n+1}(c,z)=f_c^n(z)P_n(c,z).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
P_n(c,z)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}f_c^j(z).
$$

Define the algebraic **orbit multiplier**

$$
M_n(c,z)=2^nP_n(c,z)
=2^n\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}f_c^j(z).
$$

Since $f_c'(w)=2w$, this is exactly the expression obtained by applying the complex chain rule to the derivative of $f_c^n$ at $z$.

**Theorem 4.1 (Multiplier recurrence).** The multiplier satisfies

$$
M_0(c,z)=1
$$

and

$$
M_{n+1}(c,z)=2f_c^n(z)M_n(c,z).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Extract the newest factor from the product:

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{n+1}(c,z)
&=2^{n+1}f_c^n(z)P_n(c,z)\\
&=2f_c^n(z)M_n(c,z).
\end{aligned}
$$

$\square$

### 4.2. The critical point

**Theorem 4.2 (Critical-orbit multiplier).** For every $c\in\mathbb C$ and every positive integer $n$,

$$
M_n(c,0)=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The product defining $M_n(c,0)$ includes its $j=0$ factor, namely $f_c^0(0)=0$. Hence the whole product vanishes. $\square$

**Corollary 4.3 (Superattracting periodic critical orbit).** If $q>0$, $f_c^q(0)=0$, and there is no positive return before $q$, then the critical orbit has exact period $q$ and multiplier

$$
M_q(c,0)=0.
$$

Thus a center represented by a periodic critical orbit is superattracting. If the cycle Lyapunov exponent is defined by

$$
\lambda=\frac{1}{q}\log|M_q(c,0)|,
$$

with values allowed in the extended real line, then $\lambda=-\infty$. Therefore a proposed finite center formula such as $\log(2)\cos(\pi p/q)$ is incompatible with this standard multiplier convention except as a formula for some different observable.

### 4.3. Fixed points

**Theorem 4.4 (Fixed-point equation and multiplier).** A point $z$ is fixed by $f_c$ if and only if

$$
z^2-z+c=0.
$$

Its one-step multiplier is

$$
M_1(c,z)=2z.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The fixed-point equation $z^2+c=z$ rearranges to the displayed quadratic. The multiplier product for one step is $2f_c^0(z)=2z$. $\square$

At $c=0$, the critical point $z=0$ is fixed and has multiplier $0$, giving the superattracting center of the main cardioid.

### 4.4. Exact period two

**Theorem 4.5 (Period-two equation).** If $f_c^2(z)=z$ but $f_c(z)\ne z$, then

$$
z^2+z+c+1=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Direct expansion and factorization yield

$$
f_c^2(z)-z=\bigl(f_c(z)-z\bigr)\bigl(z^2+z+c+1\bigr).
$$

The left side is zero. The first factor is nonzero because $z$ is not fixed. Hence the second factor vanishes. $\square$

**Theorem 4.6 (Period-two multiplier).** If $z$ satisfies the period-two equation and $z_1=f_c(z)=z^2+c$, then

$$
M_2(c,z)=4zz_1=4z(z^2+c).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Substitute $n=2$ into the product formula:

$$
M_2(c,z)=2^2f_c^0(z)f_c^1(z)=4z(z^2+c).
$$

$\square$

The period-two equation has roots equal to the two points of the cycle. Vieta’s formulas give their sum as $-1$ and product as $c+1$, so the cycle multiplier can also be written $4(c+1)$. At $c=-1$, the critical orbit $0\mapsto-1\mapsto0$ has exact period two and multiplier zero.

## 5. Primitive point counts and prime periods

### 5.1. Möbius inversion

Let $\mu$ denote the Möbius function on positive integers:

- $\mu(n)=0$ if $n$ is divisible by the square of a prime;
- $\mu(n)=(-1)^k$ if $n$ is a product of $k$ distinct primes.

Define the **dynatomic point count**

$$
\Psi(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(n/d)2^d.
$$

The motivation is inclusion–exclusion over periods. Generically, the equation $f_c^n(z)=z$ has $2^n$ roots counted with multiplicity. Every point whose exact period divides $n$ appears among them. Möbius inversion extracts the contribution assigned to exact period $n$.

**Proposition 5.1 (Initial values).** The first three counts are

$$
\Psi(1)=2,\qquad \Psi(2)=2,\qquad \Psi(3)=6.
$$

**Proof sketch.** For $n=1$, the sole divisor gives $2$. For $n=2$, the divisors $1,2$ give $-2+4=2$. For $n=3$, they give $-2+8=6$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Nonnegativity).** For every positive integer $n$,

$$
\Psi(n)\ge0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Separate the divisor $d=n$. Since $\mu(1)=1$,

$$
\Psi(n)=2^n+\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\d<n}}\mu(n/d)2^d.
$$

As $|\mu(m)|\le1$, the absolute value of the proper-divisor contribution is bounded above by

$$
\sum_{\substack{d\mid n\\d<n}}2^d.
$$

The proper divisors form a subset of $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$ after harmlessly enlarging the index set, so this sum is at most

$$
\sum_{d=0}^{n-1}2^d=2^n-1.
$$

Therefore $\Psi(n)\ge2^n-(2^n-1)=1$ under this coarse enlargement whenever the indexing comparison is strict, and in particular $\Psi(n)\ge0$. The claimed nonnegativity follows. $\square$

The count is a point count rather than automatically a cycle count. Once exact-period points are known to partition into cycles of cardinality $n$, the corresponding number of cycles is $\Psi(n)/n$. Establishing genericity and multiplicity statements is part of dynatomic theory; the arithmetic formula itself provides the candidate count.

### 5.2. Prime specialization

**Theorem 5.3 (Prime-period divisibility).** If $p$ is prime, then

$$
p\mid 2^p-2.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Fermat’s little theorem gives $2^p\equiv2\pmod p$, including $p=2$. Subtracting $2$ yields the result. $\square$

For a prime $p$, the only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$, so Möbius inversion simplifies to

$$
\Psi(p)=2^p-2.
$$

The divisibility theorem therefore states exactly that the prime-period point count can be grouped into cycles of length $p$.

**Theorem 5.4 (Lower bound at prime period).** If $p$ is prime and $p\ge3$, then

$$
2\le\frac{2^p-2}{p}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** It is enough to show $2p\le2^p-2$. Equality holds at $p=3$. The elementary inequality persists and strengthens as $p$ increases because exponential growth of $2^p$ dominates linear growth of $2p$. An induction on integers $p\ge3$ proves the stronger bound, and restriction to primes gives the theorem. $\square$

Hence every prime period $p\ge3$ has a candidate count of at least two primitive cycles in the generic enumeration. What is special about primality here is the two-element divisor lattice and Fermat divisibility—not, without additional definitions, a unique planar dihedral symmetry.

## 6. Farey mediation and Fibonacci denominators

For nonnegative integers $p_1,q_1,p_2,q_2$ with positive denominators, define the **Farey mediant** by

$$
\frac{p_1}{q_1}\oplus\frac{p_2}{q_2}
=\frac{p_1+p_2}{q_1+q_2}.
$$

This operation is fundamental in the organization of rational rotation data. Its denominator rule is simply addition.

Let the Fibonacci sequence be defined by

$$
F_0=0,\qquad F_1=1,\qquad F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n.
$$

**Theorem 6.1 (Farey–Fibonacci denominator identity).** The mediant of

$$
\frac{F_n}{F_{n+1}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{F_{n+1}}{F_{n+2}}
$$

has denominator $F_{n+3}$.

**Proof sketch.** The mediant denominator is $F_{n+1}+F_{n+2}$, which equals $F_{n+3}$ by the Fibonacci recurrence. $\square$

This theorem isolates the exact arithmetic mechanism by which Fibonacci numbers arise under repeated mediation. A geometric claim that specific visible components follow this mediant hierarchy additionally requires a component-labeling theory; the identity itself is unconditional.

## 7. Escape growth and computational procedures

### 7.1. A one-step theorem

**Theorem 7.1 (Escape-norm growth).** Let $w=f_c^n(z)$. If

$$
|w|>2
$$

and

$$
|w|>|c|,
$$

then

$$
|f_c^{n+1}(z)|>|f_c^n(z)|.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The reverse triangle inequality gives

$$
|w^2+c|\ge |w|^2-|c|.
$$

Since $|c|<|w|$,

$$
|w|^2-|c|>|w|^2-|w|=|w|(|w|-1).
$$

The assumption $|w|>2$ implies $|w|-1>1$, so the final expression exceeds $|w|$. Thus $|w^2+c|>|w|$. $\square$

This theorem proves strict one-step growth under an explicit certificate. To derive a complete escape-time theorem, one strengthens the invariant so that the hypotheses persist and then iterates the lower bound.

### 7.2. Algorithms

**Algorithm A: Orbit and multiplier recurrence.** Starting from $z_0=z$ and $M_0=1$, update

$$
M_{k+1}=2z_kM_k,
$$

then

$$
z_{k+1}=z_k^2+c.
$$

After $n$ rounds, the algorithm returns $z_n=f_c^n(z)$ and $M_n(c,z)$. It uses constant auxiliary memory and $O(n)$ complex arithmetic operations. Updating the multiplier before the orbit is important because the factor is the current point $z_k$.

**Algorithm B: Exact period search with tolerance.** Given a numerical tolerance $\varepsilon$ and a maximum period $N$, compute successive iterates from $z$. Return the first $q\in\{1,\ldots,N\}$ for which $|f_c^q(z)-z|\le\varepsilon$. This costs $O(N)$ complex iterations and constant memory. It is a numerical diagnostic, not an exact proof: floating-point near-returns can be false positives, especially near neutral cycles.

**Algorithm C: Dynatomic count by divisor enumeration.** Enumerate all divisors $d$ of $n$, compute $\mu(n/d)$ by prime factorization, and sum $\mu(n/d)2^d$. Trial division gives a straightforward implementation with approximately $O(\sqrt n)$ divisibility checks plus integer-arithmetic costs. A sieve is preferable when counts are needed for a range of $n$.

**Algorithm D: Escape-time sampling.** Begin at $z_0=0$ and iterate $z_{k+1}=z_k^2+c$. Stop when a chosen certified radius is exceeded or when a maximum iteration count is reached. The cost per parameter is $O(N)$ in the nonescaping or late-escaping case and constant memory. Color by the stopping time to render parameter-space structure.

## 8. Applications and interpretation

The return-time classification has direct computational value. Once an exact period $q$ is established, checking later candidate return times is reduced to divisibility. The injectivity theorem guarantees that the first $q$ iterates provide a canonical list of the cycle’s distinct phases.

The multiplier recurrence computes stability without symbolic expansion of $f_c^n$. In numerical continuation of periodic cycles, this product indicates attraction or repulsion. At a critical-orbit center it also provides an immediate consistency test: any nonzero reported multiplier arises from numerical displacement, altered normalization, or a different base point.

Dynatomic counts connect complex dynamics to binary necklace enumeration. The number $2^n$ resembles the count of binary words of length $n$; Möbius inversion removes words with smaller primitive period. Dividing by $n$ then identifies words up to cyclic rotation, paralleling the passage from periodic points to cycles. Prime $n$ makes the subtraction especially transparent.

Farey mediation offers a combinatorial coordinate system for rational data. The Fibonacci identity shows why repeated “between” operations naturally produce Fibonacci denominators, independently of any visual claim about a particular spiral.

Finally, the escape-growth inequality supports reliable rendering. It explains why large iterates tend to accelerate outward and provides the local estimate behind bailout tests. In graphics, this translates an infinite boundedness question into a finite stopping rule once a persistent escape condition has been established.

## 9. Scope, limitations, and corrected conjectures

Several attractive statements are not consequences of the algebra developed here.

First, a denominator-period theorem for a bulb at internal angle $p/q$ needs precise definitions of hyperbolic components, their root or center, internal rays, and the reduced rotation number. The exact-period results then become the arithmetic endpoint of that geometric theory, not a substitute for it.

Second, the finite formula

$$
\log(2)\cos(\pi p/q)
$$

cannot represent the standard cycle Lyapunov exponent at a superattracting center. The multiplier there is zero, and the logarithmic exponent is $-\infty$. A viable trigonometric conjecture would need to identify another quantity, perhaps associated with boundary combinatorics or a normalized external model.

Third, the phrase “dihedral symmetry of a bulb” is ambiguous. It might refer to Euclidean symmetries of a planar component, automorphisms of an orbit portrait, or symmetries of an abstract combinatorial object. These notions differ and must be specified before a prime-versus-composite classification can be tested.

Fourth, a prime factorization

$$
n=p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}
$$

does not by itself define a topological product decomposition of a period-$n$ component. A product theorem requires explicit spaces and an explicit homeomorphism, together with invariants compatible with products. The arithmetic factorization of return times should not be conflated with topological factorization.

Accordingly, the supported number-theoretic picture is this: exact period controls divisibility and orbit cardinality; Möbius inversion counts primitive periodic points generically; primality simplifies this count and invokes Fermat divisibility; Farey addition creates Fibonacci denominators; and critical periodicity forces zero multiplier. These statements are substantial without overstating what the image alone can encode.

## 10. Future work

1. **Primitive dynatomic counts.** Establish the generic exact-period interpretation of $\Psi(n)$ with multiplicities, prove divisibility by $n$, and identify $\Psi(n)/n$ as the number of cycles.

2. **Prime-period specialization.** Derive $\Psi(p)=2^p-2$ uniformly from the divisor sum for every prime $p$.

3. **Complex derivatives.** Connect the algebraic multiplier recurrence explicitly to the Fréchet derivative of the iterated quadratic polynomial.

4. **Iterated escape criterion.** Strengthen Theorem 7.1 to a persistent lower bound proving divergence after crossing a standard escape radius.

5. **Hyperbolic components and internal angles.** Define components, centers, and internal-angle parametrizations, then formulate and prove the relation between reduced angle denominator and component period.

6. **Lyapunov conventions.** Develop finite-time and asymptotic exponents in the extended real line and derive exponent $-\infty$ for a superattracting cycle.

7. **Symmetry claims.** Separate planar, portrait, and combinatorial symmetry before testing any dihedral classification.

8. **Topology and factorization.** Specify candidate spaces and a homeomorphism before investigating whether any product structure can be indexed by prime factors.

## 11. Conclusion

Quadratic iteration converts repetition into arithmetic. The composition law forces exact period to divide every return time. Minimality forces the pre-return orbit to contain exactly as many distinct points as the period. The derivative product makes every periodic critical orbit superattracting. Möbius inversion separates primitive periods, while Fermat’s little theorem ensures that prime-period point counts split into complete cycles. Farey addition explains Fibonacci denominators, and norm estimates explain escape growth.

Together these results provide a disciplined interpretation of the Mandelbrot set’s number theory. The set is not literally a prime-factorization oracle, nor do period factors automatically dictate topology or symmetry. Its genuine arithmetic is more structural: divisibility organizes time, Möbius inversion organizes counting, and the critical point organizes stability. Those mechanisms form a precise bridge between the geometry seen in parameter space and the algebra generated by the recurrence $z\mapsto z^2+c$.

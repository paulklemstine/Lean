## Abstract

Let $f_+(x) = x^3 - x + 1$ and $f_-(x) = x^3 - x - 1$. These two "conjugate" cubics both have discriminant $-23$ and Galois group $S_3$. In numerical experiments their splitting-type channels were found to be identical: the mutual information between the residue $p \bmod 23$ and the number of roots of the cubic modulo $p$ came out as $1.000065$ bits for both, and the two semiprime channels agreed within sampling noise. We explain both observations exactly, and we prove a stronger result. The two cubics, together with the reciprocal cubic $f_r(x) = x^3 + x^2 - 1$, have the same number of roots in *every finite commutative ring*. Hence their type functions $T(n) = \#\{x \in \mathbb{Z}/n : f(x) = 0\}$ coincide for *every* positive integer $n$. As a consequence, every finite-sample information measure built from the type is the same real number for all three generators, for every sample and every labelling. The proofs rest on a transport principle: root counts are preserved by any pair of mutually inverse maps between zero sets. We apply it to affine substitutions $x \mapsto ux + c$ of arbitrary polynomials, and to a polynomial Möbius map $r \mapsto r^{-1} = r^2 - 1$ that needs no division. We also prove four further results. First, $T(pq) = T(p)T(q)$ for coprime $p, q$, together with the entropy inequality it implies. Second, the conjugates are irreducible together over any commutative ring and have the same factorisation type over any field. Third, both obey the same discriminant sign law with character $\left(\frac{-23}{p}\right)$. Fourth, the channel separates the field of discriminant $-23$ from that of discriminant $-31$ already at $p = 3$. Finally, we explain the one-bit value of the channel group-theoretically and attribute the measured excess to finite-sample estimator bias.

## 1. Introduction

A monic integer polynomial $f$ of degree $d$ determines, for each modulus $n$, a number
$$T_f(n) = \#\{x \in \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} : f(x) \equiv 0 \pmod n\},$$
which we call the **type** of $f$ at $n$. At a prime $p$ not dividing the discriminant, $T_f(p)$ is the number of fixed points of the Frobenius element acting on the roots of $f$. So the type is a coarse shadow of the Frobenius conjugacy class. A natural experimental programme studies *type channels*. One attaches to each prime $p$ a label $L(p)$, for example $p \bmod D$, and measures the empirical mutual information $I(L;T)$ on a sample of primes. Composite moduli, especially semiprimes $pq$, can be treated the same way.

In one such experiment the cubics $f_\pm(x) = x^3 - x \pm 1$ were compared. Both have discriminant $-23$. The measured values of $I(p \bmod 23;T)$ were equal, $1.000065$ bits, to every printed digit. The semiprime pair channels agreed "within Monte Carlo noise". The working interpretation was that the type channel depends on the *field* generated by a root, not on the chosen *polynomial*.

This paper makes that interpretation precise and proves it in a strong form for this field. Our main results are as follows.

1. **Transport principles** (Section 3). Root counts in a finite ring are preserved under any bijection between zero sets. As a consequence, for any polynomial $f$ over a finite commutative ring, any unit $u$ and any element $c$, the polynomials $f(x)$ and $f(ux+c)$ have the same number of roots.
2. **Invariance in every finite ring** (Section 4). In every finite commutative ring $R$, the three cubics $x^3 - x + 1$, $x^3 - x - 1$ and $x^3 + x^2 - 1$ have the same number of roots. Hence the three type functions agree at every $n \ge 1$.
3. **Exact channel identities** (Section 5). For every finite sample and every label, the type channel and both semiprime channels take identical values for the three generators. Any disagreement can only come from sampling.
4. **Chinese remainder law** (Section 6). $T(pq) = T(p)T(q)$ for coprime $p, q$. Consequently $H(T(pq)) \le H(T(p), T(q))$ on any sample of coprime pairs.
5. **Polynomial level** (Section 7). The conjugates are irreducible together over any commutative ring. Over any field they have the same multiset of irreducible-factor degrees.
6. **Sign law, ramification and separation** (Section 8). Both conjugates have discriminant $-23$. For $p \ne 2, 23$, each has exactly one root mod $p$ iff $-23$ is a non-square mod $p$. Modulo $23$ the cubic has a double root. The type distinguishes $x^3 - x - 1$ from $x^3 + x + 1$ (discriminant $-31$) at $p = 3$.

Section 9 explains why the limiting value of the channel is exactly one bit, and why a finite sample overshoots it. Section 10 describes the algorithms. Section 11 discusses the scope of the results and open problems.

## 2. Setting and definitions

Throughout, rings are commutative with $1$. For a finite set $A$ we write $\#A$ for its cardinality.

**Definition 2.1 (Root count).** Let $R$ be a finite ring and $c : R \to R$ a function. Its *root count* is $\#\{x \in R : c(x) = 0\}$. For a polynomial $f \in R[x]$ we use the evaluation function $x \mapsto f(x)$.

**Definition 2.2 (Type function).** For an integer polynomial $f$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, put
$$T_f(n) = \#\{\, k \in \{0,1,\dots,n-1\} : f(k) \equiv 0 \pmod n \,\}.$$
For $n \ge 1$ this is the root count of $f$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. By convention $T_f(0) = 0$, so $T_f$ is defined on all of $\mathbb{N}$.

We write $T_+$, $T_-$ and $T_r$ for the type functions of
$$f_+(x) = x^3 - x + 1,\qquad f_-(x) = x^3 - x - 1,\qquad f_r(x) = x^3 + x^2 - 1 .$$

**Definition 2.3 (Empirical entropy and mutual information).** Let $S$ be a nonempty finite sample, given equal weight, and $F : S \to B$ a function to a set with decidable equality. The *empirical entropy* of $F$ on $S$ is
$$H_S(F) = -\sum_{b \in F(S)} \frac{\#F^{-1}(b)}{\#S}\log_2 \frac{\#F^{-1}(b)}{\#S}.$$
For a label $L$ and a type $T$ on $S$, the *empirical mutual information* is
$$I_S(L;T) = H_S(L) + H_S(T) - H_S(L,T),$$
where $(L,T)$ denotes the paired function $s \mapsto (L(s), T(s))$.

The only properties of $H_S$ we use are these two.

- **Congruence:** if $F = F'$ on $S$ then $H_S(F) = H_S(F')$.
- **Data processing:** for any function $g$, $H_S(g \circ F) \le H_S(F)$.

The second holds because the fibres of $g\circ F$ are unions of fibres of $F$, and merging cells of a partition never increases Shannon entropy.

**Definition 2.4 (Factorisation type).** For a nonzero polynomial $f$ over a field $K$, its *factorisation type* is the multiset of degrees of its monic irreducible factors, counted with multiplicity. A cubic has one of the types $\{3\}$, $\{1,2\}$ or $\{1,1,1\}$.

**Definition 2.5 (Discriminant).** For the depressed cubic $x^3 + ax + b$ over a field, $\operatorname{disc}(a,b) = -4a^3 - 27b^2$.

## 3. Transport of root counts

The whole paper rests on one elementary principle.

**Lemma 3.1 (Global transport).** Let $R$ be a finite set with a distinguished element $0$, let $f, g : R \to R$, and let $e : R \to R$ be a bijection with $f(e(x)) = 0 \iff g(x) = 0$ for all $x$. Then $\#\{f = 0\} = \#\{g = 0\}$.

*Proof.* The map $e$ restricts to a bijection $\{g = 0\} \to \{f = 0\}$, with inverse given by the restriction of $e^{-1}$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.2 (Transport between zero sets).** Let $R$ be finite, let $f, g : R \to R$, and let $i, j : R \to R$ be maps such that

- $f(x) = 0 \Rightarrow g(i(x)) = 0$, and $g(y) = 0 \Rightarrow f(j(y)) = 0$;
- $f(x) = 0 \Rightarrow j(i(x)) = x$, and $g(y) = 0 \Rightarrow i(j(y)) = y$.

Then $\#\{f = 0\} = \#\{g = 0\}$.

*Proof.* The restrictions of $i$ and $j$ are mutually inverse maps between the two zero sets. $\square$

The point of Lemma 3.2 is that $i$ and $j$ need not be bijections of $R$. They only need to be bijections *on the roots*. This will matter in Section 4.

**Theorem 3.3 (Affine invariance).** Let $R$ be a finite ring, $f \in R[x]$ any polynomial, $u \in R^\times$ a unit and $c \in R$. Then
$$\#\{x \in R : f(ux + c) = 0\} = \#\{x \in R : f(x) = 0\}.$$

*Proof.* The map $e(x) = ux + c$ is a bijection of $R$ with inverse $e^{-1}(y) = u^{-1}(y - c)$. Apply Lemma 3.1 to $e^{-1}$, using $u\,e^{-1}(x) + c = x$, so that $f(u e^{-1}(x) + c) = f(x)$. $\square$

The case $u = -1$, $c = 0$ is the conjugation $x \mapsto -x$. The case $u = 1$ covers all translations, and in particular the passage from a general cubic to its depressed form whenever $3$ is invertible.

## 4. The three generators of the cubic field of discriminant $-23$

Let $\rho \approx 1.3247$ be the real root of $f_-$ (the *plastic number*), and let $K = \mathbb{Q}(\rho)$. Then $-\rho$ is a root of $f_+$ and $\rho^{-1} = \rho^2 - 1$ is a root of $f_r$. All three roots generate $K$. The polynomials $f_+$, $f_-$ and $f_r$ are therefore three generators of one field. The next lemma records the polynomial identities that drive everything. Each holds in an arbitrary commutative ring and can be checked by expanding.

**Lemma 4.1 (Generator identities).** In any commutative ring, for all $x, r, s$:

1. $f_+(-x) = -f_-(x)$.
2. $r(r^2 - 1) - 1 = f_-(r)$. Hence every root of $f_-$ is a unit, with inverse $r^2 - 1$.
3. $f_r(r^2 - 1) = (r^3 - r + 1)\,f_-(r)$.
4. $f_-(s^2 + s) = (s^3 + 2s^2 + s + 1)\,f_r(s)$.
5. $(r^2-1)^2 + (r^2 - 1) - r = r\,f_-(r)$.
6. $(s^2+s)^2 - 1 - s = (s+1)\,f_r(s)$.

So the polynomial maps $i(r) = r^2 - 1$ and $j(s) = s^2 + s$ send roots of $f_-$ to roots of $f_r$ and back (items 3 and 4), and they are mutually inverse on roots (items 5 and 6). They are the maps $r \mapsto r^{-1}$ and $s \mapsto s^{-1}$, written without division.

**Theorem 4.2 (Conjugate invariance).** In every finite commutative ring $R$,
$$\#\{x \in R : x^3 - x + 1 = 0\} = \#\{x \in R : x^3 - x - 1 = 0\}.$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 4.1(1), $f_+(-x) = 0 \iff f_-(x) = 0$. Apply Lemma 3.1 with the bijection $e = $ negation. Equivalently, apply Theorem 3.3 with $u = -1$, $c = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (Reciprocal invariance).** In every finite commutative ring $R$,
$$\#\{x \in R : x^3 + x^2 - 1 = 0\} = \#\{x \in R : x^3 - x - 1 = 0\}.$$

*Proof.* Apply Lemma 3.2 with $i(r) = r^2 - 1$ and $j(s) = s^2 + s$, using items 3–6 of Lemma 4.1. $\square$

**Remark 4.4.** The map $i(x) = x^2 - 1$ is far from a bijection in general. Modulo $6$ it sends both $1$ and $5$ to $0$. This is why Lemma 3.2, and not Lemma 3.1, is needed. The cubic forces each of its roots to be a unit, which turns "taking the reciprocal" into a polynomial operation that is valid in every ring.

**Corollary 4.5 (The field, not the polynomial).** $T_+ = T_- = T_r$ as functions $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$.

*Proof.* At $n = 0$ all three are $0$ by convention. For $n \ge 1$, the residues $0, \dots, n-1$ are in bijection with $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ by $k \mapsto k \bmod n$ (inverse: the canonical representative). So $T_f(n)$ is the root count on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 apply with $R = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

**Example 4.6.** Modulo $59$ the roots of $f_-$ are $4, 13, 42$. Their negatives $55, 46, 17$ are the roots of $f_+$. Their images under $r \mapsto r^2 - 1$ are $15, 50, 52$, the roots of $f_r$, and indeed $4\cdot 15 \equiv 13 \cdot 50 \equiv 42 \cdot 52 \equiv 1 \pmod{59}$. For a few sample moduli the common type is

| $n$ | 3 | 5 | 7 | 23 | 25 | 59 | 69 | 101 | 125 | 299 | 1357 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $T_\pm(n) = T_r(n)$ | 0 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 0 | 3 | 1 | 0 | 6 |

The theorem holds at prime powers ($25$, $125$), at semiprimes ($69$, $299$, $1357$), and at the ramified prime $23$.

## 5. Exact channel identities

**Theorem 5.1 (Bit-for-bit identity of the type channel).** Let $S \subset \mathbb{N}$ be any finite sample and $L : \mathbb{N} \to B$ any label, for instance $L(p) = p \bmod 23$. Then
$$I_S(L; T_+) = I_S(L; T_-) = I_S(L; T_r).$$

*Proof.* By Corollary 4.5 the type functions are equal, so the two sides are the same expression. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Semiprime channels).** Let $S \subset \mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}$ be any finite sample of pairs and $L$ any label on pairs. Then
$$I_S\big(L;\, (p,q) \mapsto T_+(pq)\big) = I_S\big(L;\, (p,q)\mapsto T_-(pq)\big),$$
$$I_S\big(L;\, (p,q) \mapsto (T_+(p),T_+(q))\big) = I_S\big(L;\, (p,q)\mapsto (T_-(p),T_-(q))\big).$$

*Proof.* Again immediate from $T_+ = T_-$. $\square$

**Interpretation.** These theorems turn an empirical coincidence into an identity. In the type channel, both polynomials were evaluated on the same primes, so their numbers had to agree to every digit, and they did. In the semiprime experiment, agreement held only "within noise". By Theorem 5.2 the noise came *entirely* from drawing independent random samples of semiprimes for the two polynomials. On a common sample the agreement is exact.

## 6. The Chinese remainder law for semiprime types

**Theorem 6.1 (Multiplicativity across products).** Let $R$, $A$, $B$ be finite rings and $e : R \to A \times B$ a ring isomorphism. Then
$$\#\{x \in R : f_-(x) = 0\} = \#\{a \in A: f_-(a) = 0\}\cdot \#\{b \in B: f_-(b) = 0\}.$$

*Proof.* Since $e$ is a ring homomorphism and $f_-$ has integer coefficients, $e(f_-(x)) = (f_-(e(x)_1), f_-(e(x)_2))$. Because $e$ is injective, $f_-(x) = 0$ iff both components vanish. Hence $e$ restricts to a bijection from the zero set in $R$ onto the product of the zero sets in $A$ and $B$. $\square$

The same argument applies to any integer polynomial. By Corollary 4.5 the conclusion transfers to $f_+$ and $f_r$.

**Corollary 6.2 ($T(pq) = T(p)T(q)$).** If $\gcd(p,q) = 1$ then $T(pq) = T(p)\,T(q)$, where $T$ is the common type function.

*Proof.* If $p = 0$ or $q = 0$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise the Chinese remainder theorem gives a ring isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/pq \cong \mathbb{Z}/p \times \mathbb{Z}/q$, and we apply Theorem 6.1. $\square$

For example, $T(1357) = T(23 \cdot 59) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6$.

**Corollary 6.3 (Entropy bound for semiprimes).** Let $S$ be a finite sample of coprime pairs. Then
$$H_S\big((p,q)\mapsto T(pq)\big) \;\le\; H_S\big((p,q)\mapsto (T(p),T(q))\big).$$

*Proof.* By Corollary 6.2, on $S$ the function $(p,q) \mapsto T(pq)$ agrees with $m \circ (T(p), T(q))$, where $m(a,b) = ab$. By congruence the entropies agree, and data processing gives the inequality. $\square$

On all $435$ pairs of distinct primes below $120$ the two sides are $1.566$ and $2.683$ bits. The gap measures how much information the product loses. For instance, $T(pq) = 3$ arises from both $(1,3)$ and $(3,1)$.

## 7. Polynomial level: irreducibility and factorisation type

Substituting $-X$ for $X$ defines a ring automorphism $\sigma$ of $R[X]$ over $R$, and $\sigma$ is its own inverse. In $R[X]$,
$$\sigma(X^3 - X + 1) = -X^3 + X + 1 = -(X^3 - X - 1).$$

**Theorem 7.1 (Joint irreducibility).** Over every commutative ring $R$, $X^3 - X + 1$ is irreducible in $R[X]$ if and only if $X^3 - X - 1$ is.

*Proof.* A multiplicative isomorphism preserves irreducibility, so $X^3 - X + 1$ is irreducible iff $\sigma(X^3 - X + 1) = -(X^3 - X - 1)$ is. Multiplying by the unit $-1$ preserves irreducibility as well. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Invariance of factorisation type).** Let $K$ be a field and $f \in K[X]$ nonzero. Then $f(-X)$ and $f(X)$ have the same factorisation type.

*Proof.* Let $f = u\prod_i g_i$ be the factorisation of $f$ into a unit and monic irreducibles. Applying $\sigma$ gives $f(-X) = \sigma(u)\prod_i \sigma(g_i)$. Each $\sigma(g_i)$ is irreducible, being the image of an irreducible under an automorphism, and is associated to a unique monic irreducible. By uniqueness of factorisation in $K[X]$, the multiset of normalised irreducible factors of $f(-X)$ is, up to association, $\{\sigma(g_i)\}$. Associated polynomials have equal degree, and $\deg \sigma(g) = \deg g$ because $\deg g(-X) = \deg g$. So the degree multisets coincide. $\square$

**Corollary 7.3.** Over every field, $X^3 - X + 1$ and $X^3 - X - 1$ have the same factorisation type.

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 7.2 to $f = X^3 - X + 1$. This polynomial is nonzero, since its value at $0$ is $1$. Then note that $f(-X) = (-1)\cdot (X^3 - X - 1)$ and that the unit $-1$ contributes no factors. $\square$

The factorisation type is a finer invariant than the root count at ramified primes, where the root count forgets multiplicities (see Theorem 8.4).

## 8. Discriminant, sign law, ramification and separation

**Lemma 8.1.** Over any field, $\operatorname{disc}(a,-b) = \operatorname{disc}(a,b)$. In particular $\operatorname{disc}(-1, 1) = \operatorname{disc}(-1,-1) = 4 - 27 = -23$.

*Proof.* $\operatorname{disc}(a,b) = -4a^3 - 27b^2$ depends on $b$ only through $b^2$. $\square$

We use the classical sign law for cubics over finite fields, which follows from Stickelberger's theorem on the parity of Frobenius.

**Proposition 8.2 (Sign law over $\mathbb{F}_p$).** Let $p$ be an odd prime and $a, b \in \mathbb{F}_p$ with $\operatorname{disc}(a,b) \ne 0$. Then $x^3 + ax + b$ has exactly one root in $\mathbb{F}_p$ iff $\operatorname{disc}(a,b)$ is a non-square in $\mathbb{F}_p$.

*Sketch.* Since the discriminant is nonzero, the cubic is separable. Frobenius $\phi : y \mapsto y^p$ permutes its three roots in a splitting field. The roots in $\mathbb{F}_p$ are exactly the fixed points of $\phi$. So the root count is $3$, $1$ or $0$ according as $\phi$ is the identity, a transposition or a $3$-cycle. Let $\delta = \prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i - \alpha_j)$, so that $\delta^2 = \operatorname{disc}$. Then $\phi(\delta) = \operatorname{sgn}(\phi)\,\delta$. Hence $\operatorname{disc}$ is a square in $\mathbb{F}_p$ iff $\delta \in \mathbb{F}_p$ iff $\phi$ is even. The only odd cycle type is the transposition, which is exactly the "one root" case. $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (Sign law for discriminant $-23$).** Let $p$ be a prime with $p \ne 2, 23$. Then $x^3 - x - 1$ has exactly one root modulo $p$ iff $-23$ is a non-square modulo $p$, and the same holds for $x^3 - x + 1$ with the *same* character.

*Proof.* For $p \ne 23$ we have $-23 \not\equiv 0 \pmod p$. Apply Proposition 8.2 with $(a,b) = (-1,-1)$ and use Lemma 8.1. For $f_+$, the bijection $r \mapsto -r$ carries the unique root of one cubic to the unique root of the other. $\square$

By quadratic reciprocity, $\left(\frac{-23}{p}\right) = \left(\frac{p}{23}\right)$ for odd $p \ne 23$. So the label $p \bmod 23$ determines whether $T(p) = 1$.

**Theorem 8.4 (Ramification at 23).** As polynomial functions on $\mathbb{Z}/23$,
$$x^3 - x - 1 \equiv (x - 3)(x - 10)^2 \pmod{23}.$$

*Proof.* $(x-3)(x-10)^2 = x^3 - x - 1 + 23(-x^2 + 7x - 13)$. $\square$

So $T(23) = 2$. This distinct-root count does not see the double root at $10$, while the factorisation type $\{1,1,1\}$ with a repeated factor does.

**Theorem 8.5 (The channel separates fields).** $x^3 - x - 1$ has no root modulo $3$, while $x^3 + x + 1$ (discriminant $-4 - 27 = -31$) has the root $1$ modulo $3$.

*Proof.* Modulo 3, $x^3 \equiv x$ for all $x$, so $x^3 - x - 1 \equiv -1 \not\equiv 0$, while $1 + 1 + 1 \equiv 0$. $\square$

Hence the type function is not blind. It forgets the generator but remembers the field.

## 9. Why one bit, and why slightly more

Assume, for primes $p \nmid 2\cdot 23$, that Frobenius classes in $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}) \cong S_3$ are equidistributed, where $L$ is the splitting field of $f_-$. This is the Chebotarev density theorem, which we do not reprove here. Then $T(p)$ takes the values $3, 1, 0$ with densities $\tfrac16, \tfrac12, \tfrac13$. The entropy is
$$H(T) = \tfrac16 \log_2 6 + \tfrac12 \log_2 2 + \tfrac13 \log_2 3 \approx 1.4591 \text{ bits}.$$
The label $p \bmod 23$ factors through the quadratic character $\left(\frac{p}{23}\right)$. This character is the sign of Frobenius, its image in $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-23})/\mathbb{Q})$. On the odd half, $T = 1$ deterministically. On the even half, $T \in \{3, 0\}$ with conditional probabilities $\tfrac13, \tfrac23$. Within either half the finer residue carries no further information about the Frobenius class in the limit. Therefore
$$I(p \bmod 23; T) = H(T) - \tfrac12 H\!\left(\tfrac13,\tfrac23\right) = 1.4591 - 0.4591 = 1 \text{ bit exactly}.$$
This group-theoretic identity is exact. The arithmetic limit statement depends on Chebotarev.

On a finite sample of $N$ primes, the plug-in estimator $I_S$ is biased upward. Its label alphabet has $22$ classes and its type alphabet has $3$, and the Miller–Madow heuristic predicts a positive excess of order $1/N$. The table below lists $I_S(p\bmod 23; T) - 1$ on the first $N$ odd primes other than $23$.

| $N$ | 250 | 500 | 1000 | 2000 | 2260 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $I_S - 1$ | $+0.01020$ | $+0.00253$ | $+0.00043$ | $+0.00029$ | $+0.00024$ |

With the two-class label $\left(\frac{-23}{p}\right)$ instead, the estimate at $N = 2260$ is $0.999995$. The experiment's value $1.000065$ fits this picture of an exact bit plus estimator bias. By Theorem 5.1, the value is the same for all three generators on any common sample.

## 10. Algorithms

**Algorithm A (Type function).** Input: integer coefficients of $f$ and a modulus $n \ge 1$. Evaluate $f(k) \bmod n$ by Horner's rule for $k = 0, \dots, n-1$ and count the zeros. The cost is $O(n \deg f)$ arithmetic operations on residues. For composite $n$ with known factorisation, Corollary 6.2 reduces this to prime-power moduli.

**Algorithm B (Root transport certificate).** Input: a modulus $n$. Compute the root sets $Z_-$, $Z_+$, $Z_r$ of $f_-$, $f_+$, $f_r$ modulo $n$, and check $\{-r : r \in Z_-\} = Z_+$ and $\{r^2 - 1 : r \in Z_-\} = Z_r$, together with $r(r^2-1) \equiv 1$ for all $r \in Z_-$. This exhibits the bijections of Theorems 4.2 and 4.3 explicitly. The cost is $O(n)$.

**Algorithm C (Empirical channel).** Input: a sample $S$, a label $L$ and a type $T$. Tabulate the counts of $L$, of $T$ and of $(L,T)$. Return $H(L) + H(T) - H(L,T)$ with $H(c) = -\sum (c_i/N)\log_2(c_i/N)$. The cost is $O(\#S)$ after computing the types. Because of Corollary 4.5 the output is independent of the choice among $f_+$, $f_-$, $f_r$.

Running these on all moduli $1 \le n \le 400$ found no disagreement among the three type functions. On all prime pairs below $120$, no failure of $T(pq) = T(p)T(q)$ was found. On primes up to $5000$, no violation of the sign law was found. On $300$ random (polynomial, modulus, unit, shift) quadruples, no failure of affine invariance was found. These checks are consistent with the theorems, which already cover them.

## 11. Discussion and future work

**Scope.** The slogan "the field, not the polynomial" is the classical principle of Dedekind and Kummer. For a monic generator $f$ of a number field $K$ and a prime $p$ not dividing the index $[\mathcal{O}_K : \mathbb{Z}[\theta]]$, the factorisation of $f \bmod p$ mirrors the splitting of $p$ in $\mathcal{O}_K$. Our results are sharper in two ways for the generators considered here. First, there are no exceptional moduli: the equality of root counts holds in every finite commutative ring, including at the ramified prime $23$ and at all prime powers. Second, the proofs are elementary, using one transport lemma and six polynomial identities. The affine case (Theorem 3.3) is completely general. The Möbius case was carried out for this particular cubic, through the explicit inverse $r^{-1} = r^2 - 1$.

**What is not claimed.** The limiting value $I(p \bmod 23; T) \to 1$ requires the Chebotarev density theorem. Only the group-theoretic identity in Section 9 is proved here. At ramified primes, the distinct-root count is a lossy invariant. The factorisation type of Section 7 is the right object there.

**Open directions.**

1. *Möbius–Tschirnhaus invariance for all generators.* Let $f, g$ be monic integer polynomials of degree $d$ generating the same number field. Is $T_f(n) = T_g(n)$ for all $n$ coprime to $\operatorname{ind}(f)\operatorname{ind}(g)$? A natural approach is to show that both root sets modulo $n$ are in bijection with the degree-one primes of the order above $n$ once the indices are inverted. A rational change of generator $\theta \mapsto h(\theta)$ would extend Lemma 3.2 beyond the affine and reciprocal cases treated here.
2. *Finite-sample bias law.* On the first $N$ primes, does $I_N(p \bmod D; T) - 1 = (c_D + o(1))/N$ hold, with an explicit $c_D$ depending on the label and type alphabet sizes? The exact value $1$ makes the entire excess an estimator bias on an $|L| \times |T|$ table.
3. *Class-field refinement.* The splitting field of $x^3 - x - 1$ is the Hilbert class field of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-23})$, whose class number is $3$. One can replace the label $p \bmod 23$ by the class of binary quadratic forms of discriminant $-23$ that represents $p$. Classically, $p$ splits completely iff it is represented by $x^2 + xy + 6y^2$. This label determines $T(p)$ completely and should raise the channel to the full $H(T) \approx 1.459$ bits.

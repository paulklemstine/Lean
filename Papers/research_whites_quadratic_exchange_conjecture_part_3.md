# The Element Census as the Exchange Invariant of White's Quadratic Conjecture

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-07-12

## Abstract

White's quadratic exchange conjecture — the weakest and still-open member of a
trio of conjectures posed by White in 1980 — asserts that for any matroid, two
multisets of bases with the same multiset union of ground-set elements can be
transformed into one another by iterated *quadratic exchange moves*, each move
replacing two bases with two others having the same combined multiset of
elements. Equivalently, the toric ideal of the matroid basis-exchange ring is
generated by quadratic binomials. We develop an abstract combinatorial theory of
these moves on multisets of finite sets and establish, unconditionally, the
necessary direction: the *total multiset union* (the element census) is an exact
invariant of the reachability relation, as are the number of bases and the
multiplicity of each individual element. We prove that the classical symmetric
exchange of matroid theory is always a legal quadratic move, identifying
quadratic moves as precisely the operations that redistribute element-ownership
among bases while fixing the global census. We package the conjecture as a
predicate over an arbitrary basis family, prove the invariant is genuinely
necessary, and verify the conjecture completely on the uniform matroid
$U_{2,4}$ — the smallest instance with nontrivial content — showing its three
perfect matchings are pairwise connected by basis-preserving quadratic moves.
Along the way we record a general single-swap lemma for uniform matroids and
discuss the boundary between the (false) symmetric-exchange variant and the
(open) quadratic variant.

**Keywords:** matroid, basis exchange, toric ideal, quadratic generation,
symmetric exchange, uniform matroid, multiset, White's conjecture.

## 1. Introduction

Matroids abstract the notion of linear independence, retaining only the
combinatorial data of which subsets of a finite ground set are independent.
Their maximal independent subsets — the **bases** — all share a common size, the
rank, and satisfy an exchange axiom that generalizes the Steinitz exchange lemma
of linear algebra. Beyond a single basis, one is often interested in *multisets*
of bases and in the combinatorial and algebraic relations among them. The
central object here is the **basis-exchange ring** (equivalently, the basis
monomial ring) and its defining relations.

In 1980, White formulated three nested conjectures about these relations,
increasingly weak:

1. a set-theoretic (strongest) statement about compatible sequences of bases;
2. a **symmetric-exchange** statement, that any two configurations sharing their
   multiset union are connected using only symmetric exchanges;
3. a **quadratic** statement, that they are connected by arbitrary quadratic
   exchange moves.

The second (symmetric) conjecture is now known to be **false** in general. The
third — the weakest — remains **open** after more than four decades and is the
subject of this paper. It admits a clean algebraic reformulation: the toric
ideal of the matroid is generated by quadratic binomials.

### Contributions

We isolate the exact combinatorial engine of the conjecture and prove the
following, in full generality unless stated otherwise.

- **(Invariance.)** The total multiset union (element census) of a configuration
  is invariant under quadratic moves and hence under reachability; the number of
  bases and the multiplicity of every element are likewise invariant
  (Theorems 4.1–4.4).
- **(Symmetric exchange is quadratic.)** Every symmetric exchange of matroid
  theory is a quadratic exchange move, via an explicit multiset identity
  (Theorem 5.2). This characterizes quadratic moves as the census-preserving
  redistributions.
- **(Packaging and necessity.)** We express White's Part 3 as a predicate over
  an arbitrary basis family and prove the census invariant is a genuine
  precondition (Theorem 6.2).
- **(Verified instance.)** In every uniform matroid, symmetric exchanges are
  basis-preserving quadratic moves (Theorem 7.3); and the conjecture holds
  completely on $U_{2,4}$, whose three perfect matchings are pairwise connected
  (Theorem 7.5).

We emphasize the boundary of what is proved: the converse of the invariance
theorem — that equal census *implies* reachability — is exactly White's open
conjecture and is not asserted.

## 2. Preliminaries and notation

Let $\alpha$ be a type of ground-set elements (a fixed finite set in
applications, but no finiteness is needed for the invariance results). We write
$\mathrm{Finset}\,\alpha$ for finite subsets and use $\uplus$ for multiset
addition (union with multiplicities). For a finite set $B$, its underlying
multiset of elements is denoted $B^\flat$; for genuine sets this simply lists
each element once.

**Matroid.** A matroid $M$ on $\alpha$ is a nonempty family $\mathcal{B}$ of
finite subsets (the bases), all of the same cardinality $r$ (the rank),
satisfying the exchange axiom: for all $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and
$x \in B_1$ there is $y \in B_2$ with $B_1 - x + y \in \mathcal{B}$. We use
Brualdi's symmetric strengthening where needed: $y$ may be chosen so that both
$B_1 - x + y$ and $B_2 - y + x$ are bases.

**Uniform matroid.** $U_{r,n}$ has ground set of size $n$ and basis family equal
to *all* $r$-element subsets; its basis predicate is simply $|B| = r$.

## 3. Configurations, moves, and reachability

**Definition 3.1 (Configuration).** A *configuration* is a multiset
$\mathcal{C}$ of bases (finite subsets). We do not assume distinctness: bases may
repeat.

**Definition 3.2 (Total multiset union / census).** The *total multiset union*
of a configuration $\mathcal{C} = \{B_1, \dots, B_m\}$ is
$$
\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} B_i^\flat
\;=\; B_1^\flat \uplus \cdots \uplus B_m^\flat .
$$
Equivalently, for each element $e$ the census records
$\#\{ i : e \in B_i\}$ counted with multiplicity. It satisfies
$\mathrm{union}(\varnothing) = 0$ and the recursion
$\mathrm{union}(B \cup \mathcal{C}) = B^\flat \uplus \mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C})$.

**Definition 3.3 (Quadratic exchange move).** A single *quadratic exchange move*
$\mathcal{C} \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{D}$ replaces two bases by two others with
the same pooled elements: there exist a remainder configuration $R$ and bases
$B_1, B_2, C_1, C_2$ with
$$
\mathcal{C} = \{B_1, B_2\} \cup R, \quad
\mathcal{D} = \{C_1, C_2\} \cup R, \quad
B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat = C_1^\flat \uplus C_2^\flat .
$$

**Definition 3.4 (Reachability).** *Reachability* is the equivalence closure of
the quadratic-move relation: the smallest reflexive, symmetric, transitive
relation containing $\rightsquigarrow$.

**Definition 3.5 (Basis-restricted move).** For a basis predicate $\mathcal{B}$,
a *basis-preserving* quadratic move additionally requires the produced bases
$C_1, C_2$ to satisfy $\mathcal{B}(C_1)$ and $\mathcal{B}(C_2)$. Its equivalence
closure is *basis-restricted reachability*. A configuration is *supported on*
$\mathcal{B}$ if all its bases satisfy $\mathcal{B}$.

## 4. The census is an exact invariant

**Theorem 4.1 (Symmetry of moves).** If $\mathcal{C} \rightsquigarrow
\mathcal{D}$ then $\mathcal{D} \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{C}$.

*Proof.* The defining data are symmetric in $(B_1, B_2)$ and $(C_1, C_2)$; the
multiset equality $B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat = C_1^\flat \uplus C_2^\flat$ is an
equation, so it reverses. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (One-step invariance).** A quadratic move preserves the total
multiset union: $\mathcal{C} \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{D}$ implies
$\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) = \mathrm{union}(\mathcal{D})$.

*Proof.* Write $\mathcal{C} = \{B_1, B_2\} \cup R$ and $\mathcal{D} = \{C_1,
C_2\} \cup R$. By the recursion for $\mathrm{union}$,
$\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) = B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat \uplus
\mathrm{union}(R)$ and similarly for $\mathcal{D}$. Reassociating and
substituting the move's hypothesis $B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat = C_1^\flat \uplus
C_2^\flat$ gives equality. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (Cardinality invariance).** A quadratic move preserves the number
of bases; hence reachable configurations have equally many bases.

*Proof.* Each move replaces two bases with two bases and leaves $R$ untouched,
so the cardinality is unchanged. Induct along the equivalence closure. $\square$

**Theorem 4.4 (Reachability invariants).** If $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ are
reachable, then $\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) = \mathrm{union}(\mathcal{D})$; in
particular every element $e$ has the same total multiplicity in both, and the two
configurations have the same number of bases. Moreover, reachability is an
equivalence relation.

*Proof.* Reachability is generated by moves under reflexivity, symmetry, and
transitivity. The base case is Theorem 4.2; reflexivity is trivial; symmetry and
transitivity of the invariant equality are immediate. Multiplicity preservation
follows by reading off the count of $e$ from the equal multiset unions.
Cardinality is Theorem 4.3. That reachability is an equivalence relation holds by
construction as the equivalence closure. $\square$

Theorem 4.4 is the **necessary direction** of White's conjecture, proved
unconditionally. It shows the census is a well-defined invariant of the
reachability classes, and the *only* candidate obstruction the conjecture must
overcome.

## 5. The symmetric exchange as a quadratic move

The atomic operation of matroid theory is the symmetric exchange. We show it is
always a quadratic move; the content is a single multiset identity.

**Lemma 5.1 (Head-swap identity).** For multisets $a, b$ and elements $x, y$,
$$
(x \uplus a) \uplus (y \uplus b) \;=\; (y \uplus a) \uplus (x \uplus b),
$$
where $x, y$ denote the corresponding singletons. Consequently, for bases
$B_1 \ni x$ and $B_2 \ni y$ with $x \notin B_2$ and $y \notin B_1$,
$$
B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat
= (B_1 - x + y)^\flat \uplus (B_2 - y + x)^\flat . \tag{$\ast$}
$$

*Proof.* The first identity is commutativity/associativity of multiset addition.
For $(\ast)$, write $B_1^\flat = x \uplus (B_1 - x)^\flat$ and $B_2^\flat = y
\uplus (B_2 - y)^\flat$ using $x \in B_1$, $y \in B_2$; because $y \notin B_1$
and $x \notin B_2$, the inserted elements are fresh, so $(B_1 - x + y)^\flat = y
\uplus (B_1 - x)^\flat$ and $(B_2 - y + x)^\flat = x \uplus (B_2 - y)^\flat$. Now
apply the head-swap identity with $a = (B_1 - x)^\flat$, $b = (B_2 - y)^\flat$.
$\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Symmetric exchange is quadratic).** Let $B_1, B_2$ be bases,
$x \in B_1 \setminus B_2$, $y \in B_2 \setminus B_1$, and $R$ any remainder.
Then
$$
\{B_1, B_2\} \cup R \;\rightsquigarrow\; \{\,B_1 - x + y,\; B_2 - y + x\,\} \cup R
$$
is a quadratic exchange move.

*Proof.* Immediate from Definition 3.3 with $C_1 = B_1 - x + y$ and $C_2 = B_2 -
y + x$, the required multiset equality being $(\ast)$ of Lemma 5.1. $\square$

**Interpretation.** Theorem 5.2 pinpoints the meaning of a quadratic move:
quadratic moves are exactly the operations that permute which basis owns each
element without altering the global census. Every symmetric exchange is such an
operation. The converse fails: not every quadratic move factors as a sequence of
symmetric exchanges — this is precisely why White's stronger (symmetric)
conjecture is false while the quadratic conjecture may still hold. The quadratic
game has strictly more freedom.

## 6. Packaging White's Part 3

**Definition 6.1 (White's Part 3, as a predicate).** For a basis family
$\mathcal{B}$, say *White's Part 3 holds for $\mathcal{B}$* if: for all
configurations $\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}$ supported on $\mathcal{B}$ with
$\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) = \mathrm{union}(\mathcal{D})$, the configurations
are connected by basis-preserving quadratic moves.

**Theorem 6.2 (Necessity of the invariant).** If $\mathcal{C}$ and $\mathcal{D}$
are connected by basis-preserving quadratic moves, then
$\mathrm{union}(\mathcal{C}) = \mathrm{union}(\mathcal{D})$.

*Proof.* A basis-preserving move is in particular a quadratic move; hence
basis-restricted reachability implies reachability, and Theorem 4.4 applies.
$\square$

Theorem 6.2 confirms the definition is non-vacuous: the census hypothesis in
Definition 6.1 is a genuine necessary condition, not a triviality. White's
conjecture is the assertion that it is also sufficient — the direction we do not
prove in general.

### Algebraic reformulation

Assign an indeterminate $y_B$ to each basis $B$ and $x_e$ to each ground element
$e$, and consider the ring homomorphism $y_B \mapsto \prod_{e \in B} x_e$. Its
kernel, the **toric ideal** of the matroid, is spanned by binomials $y_{B_1}
y_{B_2} - y_{C_1} y_{C_2}$ indexed by quadratic moves $B_1^\flat \uplus B_2^\flat
= C_1^\flat \uplus C_2^\flat$. The multi-degree in the $x_e$ is exactly the
census. In this language, White's Part 3 reads:

> **The toric ideal of any matroid is generated by quadratic binomials.**

Reachability of two census-matched configurations corresponds to expressing the
binomial relating their basis-monomials as an algebraic consequence of the
quadratic generators. The combinatorial and algebraic statements are
equivalent.

## 7. Uniform matroids: a verified instance

Uniform matroids are the natural first test theatre: since every $r$-subset is a
basis, every conceivable swap is admissible.

**Lemma 7.1 (Uniform membership).** In $U_{r,n}$, a subset $B$ is a basis iff
$|B| = r$.

**Lemma 7.2 (Single-swap cardinality).** If $x \in B$ and $y \notin B$ then
$|B - x + y| = |B|$.

*Proof.* Inserting a fresh element adds one and erasing a present element
subtracts one; since $|B| > 0$ (it contains $x$), the two cancel. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Uniform symmetric exchange is basis-preserving).** In $U_{r,n}$,
for bases $B_1, B_2$ (each of size $r$), $x \in B_1 \setminus B_2$, $y \in B_2
\setminus B_1$, and any remainder $R$,
$$
\{B_1, B_2\} \cup R \;\rightsquigarrow\; \{\,B_1 - x + y,\; B_2 - y + x\,\} \cup R
$$
is a **basis-preserving** quadratic move.

*Proof.* The multiset identity $(\ast)$ (Lemma 5.1) supplies the quadratic-move
condition; Lemma 7.2 shows each produced set again has size $r$, hence is a basis
by Lemma 7.1. $\square$

**Lemma 7.4.** $U_{2,4}$ has exactly $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ bases.

**Theorem 7.5 (White's Part 3 verified on $U_{2,4}$).** The three perfect
matchings
$$
\mathcal{M}_1 = \{\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\}, \quad
\mathcal{M}_2 = \{\{1,3\},\{2,4\}\}, \quad
\mathcal{M}_3 = \{\{1,4\},\{2,3\}\}
$$
all share the census "one copy of each of $1,2,3,4$," and are pairwise connected
by basis-preserving quadratic moves.

*Proof.* Each matching's census is $\{1,2,3,4\}$ with unit multiplicities;
equality is a direct computation. For connectivity, $\mathcal{M}_1
\rightsquigarrow \mathcal{M}_2$ pools $\{1,2\} \uplus \{3,4\} = \{1,2,3,4\}$ and
repartitions into $\{1,3\}, \{2,4\}$, both of size $2$ hence bases; likewise
$\mathcal{M}_2 \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{M}_3$. Transitivity of reachability
yields $\mathcal{M}_1 \rightsquigarrow \mathcal{M}_3$. $\square$

$U_{2,4}$ is the smallest matroid where the conjecture has genuine content: rank
one is degenerate (bases are singletons, so moves only relabel ownership), and
$U_{2,4}$ is the first case where two *distinct* bases must actually be
exchanged.

## 8. Algorithms

We describe the two computational primitives used to verify instances and to
explore reachability classes.

**Algorithm A (Census computation).** Given a configuration as a list of bases,
accumulate the multiplicity of each element across all bases. Complexity
$O(m \cdot r)$ for $m$ bases of size $r$. This computes the sole invariant of
Theorem 4.4 and is used to bucket configurations into candidate classes.

**Algorithm B (Reachability search).** Given a matroid basis family and a
starting configuration, perform a breadth-first search over configurations,
where the neighbors of a configuration are all results of a legal
basis-preserving quadratic move on some pair of its bases. Because the census is
invariant (Theorem 4.4), the entire search stays within one census bucket, which
bounds the search space. The connected component recovered is exactly the
reachability class; comparing it against the census bucket tests White's
conjecture on that instance.

## 9. Applications and connections

- **Commutative algebra of matroids.** White's Part 3 is the quadratic
  generation of matroid toric ideals; verified instances feed Gröbner-basis and
  normality studies of basis monomial rings.
- **Discrete polymatroids and integer points.** The invariance results use no
  matroid structure and hold for arbitrary families of finite sets; they extend
  to discrete polymatroids, where the census becomes the toric degree map.
- **Sampling and mixing.** Reachability by local quadratic moves underlies
  Markov-chain samplers over bases with fixed marginals; the census is the
  conserved quantity of the chain, and connectivity (the conjecture) is exactly
  the irreducibility question for such chains.

## 10. Discussion and boundary phenomena

The theory cleanly separates the settled from the open. Necessity (Theorem 4.4)
is unconditional; the classical swap is subsumed (Theorem 5.2); the smallest
nontrivial matroid is fully resolved (Theorem 7.5). The open content is a single
converse.

A key boundary is the failure of the *symmetric* variant (White's Part 2) versus
the openness of the *quadratic* variant (Part 3). Theorem 5.2 shows symmetric
exchanges are quadratic moves, but the reverse decomposition can fail: quadratic
moves may reroute a redistribution around a circuit that no symmetric exchange
can. The extra latitude of quadratic moves is exactly what keeps Part 3 alive
after Part 2 collapses.

Another boundary is rank. Rank one is degenerate; uniform matroids of any rank
are tractable because they are *base-orderable*. Dropping base-orderability is
where the difficulty concentrates.

## 11. Future directions

This cycle isolated the *element-census invariant* as the exact obstruction that
quadratic exchange moves preserve, verified that every symmetric exchange of
matroid theory is a legal quadratic move, and confirmed White's third conjecture
on the smallest nontrivial uniform matroid $U_{2,4}$. From these findings we
distill the following bold, testable conjectures.

**1. Census-completeness for base-orderable matroids.** For every strongly
base-orderable matroid, two multisets of bases are connected by quadratic
exchange moves if and only if they have the same multiset union of ground-set
elements. Base-orderability supplies a global bijection between any two bases
that simultaneously realizes all pairwise swaps, so the local element-census
invariant can be upgraded to a global connectivity certificate one transposition
at a time. The single-swap building block (Theorem 7.3) is exactly the atomic
move a base-ordering composes, so the remaining work is a bookkeeping induction
on the number of disagreeing elements — a finite descent rather than an
open-ended search.

**2. Bounded exchange distance.** If two configurations of $m$ bases of rank $r$
share their multiset union, they can be connected using at most $m \cdot r$
quadratic moves. Each quadratic move can be made to strictly decrease a potential
function counting element-ownership mismatches against a fixed target
configuration, and that potential is bounded by the total number of
element-slots $m \cdot r$. The invariants on preserved cardinality and preserved
element count already pin down $m$ and the element census, giving a ready-made,
monotone potential for the descent.

**3. Symmetric-exchange deficiency is a rank-3 phenomenon.** The failure of the
symmetric-exchange (stronger) variant while the quadratic variant survives first
occurs at rank 3; all rank-$\le 2$ matroids satisfy the symmetric-exchange
property outright. In rank 2 every basis is a pair, so a single swap always
returns two pairs, forcing symmetric exchanges to close every move; only from
rank 3 onward can a required swap be blocked by a circuit that a non-symmetric
quadratic move can still route around. The $U_{2,4}$ instance shows the
quadratic and symmetric pictures coincide in rank 2, sharpening the search for
the exact rank where they diverge into a finite, enumerable question.

**4. Toric grading detects the exchange components.** The connected components of
the quadratic-exchange graph on configurations of a matroid are in bijection with
the fibers of the multiset-union (degree) map; equivalently, the degree map is a
*complete* invariant exactly when White's conjecture holds. The necessity
direction already proves the degree map is constant on components; the conjecture
is the statement that it separates them.

---

## 12. Conclusion

We have distilled White's quadratic exchange conjecture to its combinatorial
core. The total multiset union — the element census — is the exact invariant of
quadratic reachability, along with the number of bases and each element's
multiplicity. The symmetric exchange of matroid theory is always a legal
quadratic move, characterizing quadratic moves as the census-preserving
redistributions of element ownership. The conjecture holds completely on
$U_{2,4}$, the smallest matroid where it has teeth. What remains — and what has
resisted proof for over four decades — is the converse: that equal census is not
merely necessary but sufficient for connectivity. The map is drawn; the summit
awaits.

## Abstract

A *duality eigensystem* of degree $d$ and weight $w$ is a finite multiset of nonzero complex numbers $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_d$ that is stable under the involution $\alpha \mapsto Q/\alpha$, where $Q = q^w$. Such systems arise as eigenvalues of Frobenius on pure pieces of a Weil cohomology that satisfies Poincaré duality. Their characteristic polynomial satisfies a functional equation whose sign is $\varepsilon = \prod_i \alpha_i / s^d$, where $s^2 = Q$. We first prove the *sign formula* $\varepsilon = (-1)^{n_-}$, where $n_-$ is the multiplicity of the fixed point $-s$. We then study a proposed description of the kernel of the sign map. The conjecture was that two systems of the same degree have the same sign if and only if they are related by permutations, insertion and deletion of pairs $(-s,-s)$ ("double-minus moves"), and sign-preserving quadratic twists. We prove that these moves preserve the sign, and we give a *complete classification* of the equivalence relation they generate. Its invariants are the *core* (the non-fixed eigenvalues), the number of $+s$ fixed points, and the parity of the number of $-s$ fixed points; in even degree they are taken up to the twist, which negates the core and exchanges the two parities. As a consequence the conjecture holds in degree $d$ **if and only if $d \le 1$**. For every $d \ge 2$ a single sign class contains infinitely many move classes, so the core is a strictly finer invariant than the sign. Finally we repair the conjecture. If insertion and deletion of an arbitrary dual pair $\{\beta, Q/\beta\}$ is allowed, then equal degree and equal sign are equivalent to move-equivalence in **every** degree, and there are exactly four normal forms: $[\,]$, $[s,-s]$, $[s]$ and $[-s]$. We describe the resulting decision algorithms, give numerical illustrations, and state several conjectures for future work.

## 1. Introduction

Let $X$ be a smooth projective variety over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$, and let $H$ be a pure weight-$w$ piece of its cohomology with $d = \dim H$. The zeta function of $X$ factors through the characteristic polynomials

$$P(T) = \det(1 - F T \mid H) = \prod_{i=1}^{d} (1 - \alpha_i T)$$

of the Frobenius $F$. When $H$ is self-dual under Poincaré duality, the multiset of *inverse roots* $\{\alpha_i\}$ is stable under $\alpha \mapsto Q/\alpha$ with $Q = q^w$. This symmetry is equivalent to a functional equation. Writing $s$ for a fixed square root of $Q$, a direct computation gives

$$P\!\left(\frac{1}{QT}\right) = (-1)^d\, \varepsilon\, Q^{-d/2}\, T^{-d}\, P(T), \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_d}{s^d}.$$

The number $\varepsilon$ is the *sign* of the functional equation. Signs of this kind, and their global relatives the root numbers, carry arithmetic meaning: the parity conjecture relates the root number of an elliptic curve to the parity of its rank. It is therefore natural to ask how much of the eigenvalue data the sign detects. Equivalently: what is the *kernel* of the sign map, viewed as a function on eigensystems of fixed degree?

**Main questions.** (i) Find a closed formula for $\varepsilon$. (ii) Decide whether the kernel of the sign is generated by a short list of elementary moves, in particular by insertion and deletion of pairs of the eigenvalue $-\sqrt{Q}$. (iii) If it is not, find the correct generators and a complete normal form.

**Summary of results.**

1. *Sign formula* (Theorem 3.3): $\varepsilon = (-1)^{n_-}$, where $n_-$ counts the eigenvalue $-s$. Two eigensystems therefore have the same sign if and only if $n_-$ has the same parity (Corollary 3.4).
2. *Sign invariance* (Theorem 4.2): permutations, double-minus moves and even-degree twists preserve the sign, the eigensystem property and the parity of the degree.
3. *Complete classification of the original moves* (Theorem 5.4), in terms of the core and the fixed-point counts.
4. *Verdict* (Theorem 6.3): completeness of the original moves holds in degree $d$ if and only if $d \le 1$. An explicit family (Theorem 6.2) exhibits infinitely many move classes in a single sign class. For core-free systems completeness holds in even degree and fails in every odd degree $\ge 3$ (Propositions 6.4 and 6.5).
5. *Repair* (Theorem 7.4): with dual-pair moves, equal sign is equivalent to move-equivalence for systems of the same degree, in every degree. There are four normal forms (Theorem 7.3), and the original moves are strictly weaker (Proposition 7.5).

All results are purely algebraic. Beyond $s \neq 0$ we use no hypothesis about the eigenvalues: no integrality and no absolute-value condition. The theorems therefore apply verbatim to Weil numbers, and also to any formal system of "eigenvalues" equipped with a duality of the form $\alpha \mapsto s^2/\alpha$.

## 2. Setting and notation

Throughout, $s \in \mathbb{C}$ is a fixed nonzero complex number and $Q = s^2$. For an honest Weil cohomology one takes $s = q^{w/2} > 0$, but nothing below depends on that choice.

**Definition 2.1 (Duality involution).** The *duality* is the map $D(\alpha) = s^2/\alpha$ on $\mathbb{C}^\times$.

**Lemma 2.2.** $D$ is injective on $\mathbb{C}^\times$. It is an involution, $D(D(\alpha)) = \alpha$, and it commutes with negation, $D(-\alpha) = -D(\alpha)$. Its fixed points are exactly $\alpha = s$ and $\alpha = -s$.

*Proof.* The first three statements are immediate. For the last, $s^2/\alpha = \alpha$ means $\alpha^2 - s^2 = (\alpha - s)(\alpha + s) = 0$. $\square$

**Definition 2.3 (Duality eigensystem).** A *duality eigensystem* of degree $d$ is a finite list $L = (\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_d)$ of nonzero complex numbers such that the list $(D(\alpha_1),\dots,D(\alpha_d))$ is a permutation of $L$. Equivalently, the multiset of entries is $D$-stable.

**Definition 2.4 (Sign, fixed-point counts, core).** For any finite list $L$ of complex numbers of length $d$, set

- the *sign* $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \left(\prod_i \alpha_i\right)/s^d$;
- $n_+(L)$, the number of entries equal to $+s$ (the *$+Q$ fixed points*);
- $n_-(L)$, the number of entries equal to $-s$ (the *$-Q$ fixed points*);
- the *core* $\operatorname{core}(L)$, the multiset of entries different from both $s$ and $-s$.

Every list is determined up to permutation by the triple $(n_+, n_-, \operatorname{core})$. Its degree is $d = n_+ + n_- + |\operatorname{core}|$.

**Normalisation.** Dividing every eigenvalue by $s$ replaces $D$ by $x \mapsto 1/x$, the fixed points by $\pm 1$, and the sign by the plain product $\prod x_i$. We use these "unitary units" in all numerical examples. For a Weil eigensystem the normalised eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and $D$ becomes complex conjugation.

## 3. The sign formula

The key observation is that dual pairs are invisible to the sign: if $\beta \neq D(\beta)$, then $\beta\cdot D(\beta) = s^2$.

**Lemma 3.1 (Erasing an element).** Let $M$ be a $D$-stable multiset in $\mathbb{C}^\times$ and let $a \in M$. Then the image of $M \setminus \{a\}$ under $D$ is $M \setminus \{D(a)\}$.

*Proof.* Because $D$ is injective, applying $D$ commutes with erasing one copy of an element. Hence $D(M \setminus\{a\}) = D(M)\setminus\{D(a)\} = M \setminus \{D(a)\}$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.2 (Product formula).** For every $D$-stable multiset $M$ of nonzero complex numbers,

$$\prod_{\alpha \in M} \alpha = (-1)^{n_-(M)}\, s^{|M|}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Use strong induction on $|M|$. The empty case is trivial. Otherwise pick $a \in M$.

*Case 1: $D(a) = a$.* By Lemma 2.2, $a = \pm s$. By Lemma 3.1, $M' = M\setminus\{a\}$ is again $D$-stable, and the induction hypothesis applies to it. Multiplying by $a$ gives the claim, since a factor $s$ leaves $n_-$ unchanged and a factor $-s$ increases $n_-$ by one.

*Case 2: $b = D(a) \neq a$.* Then $b \in M$, and $b$ still occurs in $M\setminus\{a\}$. Erasing $a$ and then $b$, Lemma 3.1 and the involution property show that $M'' = M \setminus \{a, b\}$ is $D$-stable. Neither $a$ nor $b$ equals $-s$: if $a = -s$ then $a$ would be fixed, and if $b = -s$ then $a = D(b) = -s$. Since $ab = s^2$, we get $\prod M = s^2 \prod M'' = s^2 (-1)^{n_-(M'')} s^{|M''|}$, and $n_-(M) = n_-(M'')$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.3 (Sign formula).** For every duality eigensystem $L$,

$$\operatorname{sgn}(L) = (-1)^{n_-(L)}.$$

*Proof.* Divide Theorem 3.2 by $s^{d} \neq 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4 (Sign parity criterion).** Two duality eigensystems $L$ and $L'$ satisfy $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L')$ if and only if $n_-(L) \equiv n_-(L') \pmod 2$.

In particular the sign takes only the values $\pm 1$, and the eigenvalue $+s$ never affects it.

*Example 3.5.* For an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb{F}_p$ with trace $a_p$, the system $H^1$ is $\{\alpha, \bar\alpha\}$ with $\alpha\bar\alpha = p$, so $\operatorname{sgn} = +1$. If $E$ is supersingular with $a_p = 0$, then $\alpha = i\sqrt{p}$. The degree-4, weight-2 system $H^1\otimes H^1 = \{\alpha^2, \alpha\bar\alpha, \bar\alpha\alpha, \bar\alpha^2\} = \{-p, p, p, -p\}$ has $s = p$ and $n_- = 2$, so its sign is $+1$. For an ordinary $E$ the same construction gives core $\{\alpha^2, \bar\alpha^2\}$ and $n_+ = 2$, $n_- = 0$.

## 4. The original moves

**Definition 4.1 (Moves).** The *move relation* $\sim$ on finite lists of complex numbers is the smallest equivalence relation containing:

- **(P) Permutation.** $L \sim L'$ whenever $L'$ is a permutation of $L$.
- **(N) Double-minus insertion.** $L \sim (-s, -s, L)$ for every list $L$. By symmetry this also covers the deletion of such a pair.
- **(T) Even twist.** $L \sim -L := (-\alpha_1,\dots,-\alpha_d)$ whenever $d$ is even.

The twist is the quadratic twist by the character of order two. Twisting by a scalar $c$ preserves $D$-stability only if $c^2 = 1$. Since $\operatorname{sgn}(-L) = (-1)^d \operatorname{sgn}(L)$, the twist preserves the sign exactly in even degree, which is why it is allowed only there.

**Theorem 4.2 (Sign invariance and preservation).** If $L \sim L'$ then:

1. $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L')$, for arbitrary lists, not only eigensystems;
2. $L$ is a duality eigensystem if and only if $L'$ is;
3. $|L| \equiv |L'| \pmod 2$.

*Proof sketch.* It suffices to check the generators.
- (P) preserves product, length and multiset.
- (N) multiplies the product by $(-s)^2 = s^2$ and the length by adding $2$, so the sign is unchanged. Since $D(-s) = -s$, the system $(-s,-s,L)$ is $D$-stable exactly when $L$ is.
- (T) multiplies the product by $(-1)^d = 1$ for even $d$. Since $D(-\alpha) = -D(\alpha)$, negation maps $D$-stable lists to $D$-stable lists.

Symmetry and transitivity preserve all three properties. $\square$

The "if" half of the conjecture therefore holds in every degree. The question is completeness.

## 5. Complete classification of the original moves

### 5.1 Invariants

For a list $L$ write $C(L) = \operatorname{core}(L)$ and $-C$ for the negated multiset. Negation maps entries outside $\{\pm s\}$ to entries outside $\{\pm s\}$, so $C(-L) = -C(L)$, $n_+(-L) = n_-(L)$ and $n_-(-L) = n_+(L)$.

**Definition 5.1.** For lists $L, L'$ define the following relations.

- $R_{\mathrm{odd}}(L,L')$ holds if $C(L') = C(L)$, $n_+(L') = n_+(L)$, and $n_-(L') \equiv n_-(L) \pmod 2$.
- $R_{\mathrm{even}}(L,L')$ holds if either
  - $C(L') = C(L)$, $n_+(L') \equiv n_+(L)$, and $n_-(L') \equiv n_-(L) \pmod 2$; or
  - $C(L') = -C(L)$, $n_+(L') \equiv n_-(L)$, and $n_-(L') \equiv n_+(L) \pmod 2$.

**Lemma 5.2.** $R_{\mathrm{odd}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{even}}$ are equivalence relations.

*Proof.* Reflexivity and symmetry are clear; for symmetry of the twisted branch, substitute $-x$ for $x$. For transitivity of $R_{\mathrm{even}}$, compose branches: same∘same = same, same∘twisted = twisted, and twisted∘twisted = same, because negating twice is the identity. $\square$

**Proposition 5.3 (Invariance).** If $L \sim L'$ then $R_{\mathrm{even}}(L,L')$. If moreover $|L|$ is odd, then $R_{\mathrm{odd}}(L, L')$.

*Proof sketch.* Check the generators. (P) preserves all counts. (N) adds $2$ to $n_-$ and changes nothing else. (T) lands in the twisted branch of $R_{\mathrm{even}}$. In odd degree no twist can occur along a chain of moves, because by Theorem 4.2 the degree parity is constant along the chain. Hence every step preserves $C$ and $n_+$ exactly and $n_-$ modulo $2$. $\square$

### 5.2 Normal forms and completeness

For $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and a list $C$ with no entry $\pm s$, define the *normal form*

$$\mathrm{nf}(a, b, C) = (\underbrace{s,\dots,s}_{a},\ \underbrace{-s,\dots,-s}_{b},\ C).$$

Every list $L$ is a permutation of $\mathrm{nf}(n_+(L), n_-(L), C(L))$.

**Lemma 5.4.1 (Double-minus reduction).** Every list satisfies $L \sim \mathrm{nf}\big(n_+(L),\ n_-(L) \bmod 2,\ C(L)\big)$.

*Proof.* Permute $L$ into normal form and delete $\lfloor n_-/2 \rfloor$ pairs $(-s,-s)$. $\square$

**Lemma 5.4.2 (Double-plus insertion in even degree).** If $|L|$ is even, then $L \sim (s, s, L)$.

*Proof.* Use a twist–insert–twist chain:
$$L \ \overset{T}{\sim}\ -L \ \overset{N}{\sim}\ (-s,-s,-L) \ \overset{T}{\sim}\ (s, s, L).$$
Both twists are legal, because $|L|$ and $|L|+2$ are even. $\square$

**Lemma 5.4.3 (Even reduction).** If $|L|$ is even, then $L \sim \mathrm{nf}\big(n_+(L) \bmod 2,\ n_-(L)\bmod 2,\ C(L)\big)$.

*Proof.* Apply Lemma 5.4.1, then delete $\lfloor n_+/2\rfloor$ pairs $(s,s)$ using the inverse of Lemma 5.4.2. The degree stays even throughout. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Complete classification).** For all finite lists $L, L'$ of complex numbers,

$$L \sim L' \iff \big(|L| \text{ odd and } R_{\mathrm{odd}}(L,L')\big)\ \text{ or }\ \big(|L| \text{ even and } R_{\mathrm{even}}(L,L')\big).$$

Explicitly, in terms of the core and the fixed-point counts:

- **odd degree:** $L \sim L'$ if and only if $C(L') = C(L)$, $n_+(L') = n_+(L)$ and $n_-(L') \equiv n_-(L) \pmod 2$;
- **even degree:** $L \sim L'$ if and only if either $C(L') = C(L)$ and both fixed-point parities agree, or $C(L') = -C(L)$ and the parities are exchanged, $n_+(L') \equiv n_-(L)$ and $n_-(L') \equiv n_+(L) \pmod 2$.

*Proof sketch.* The forward direction is Proposition 5.3. For the converse in odd degree, Lemma 5.4.1 reduces $L$ and $L'$ to normal forms with the same $a = n_+$, the same $b \in \{0,1\}$, and cores that are permutations of each other. Hence $L \sim \mathrm{nf} \sim L'$.

In even degree, the untwisted branch works the same way using Lemma 5.4.3. The degree of $L'$ is even as well, because $|L'| = n_+(L') + n_-(L') + |C(L')|$ and the parities and core sizes match. In the twisted branch, first replace $L'$ by $-L'$; this is a legal twist because $|L'|$ is even. Now $L$ and $-L'$ satisfy the untwisted branch, so $L \sim -L' \sim L'$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.5 (Odd degree: only permutations).** If $L$ and $L'$ have the same odd degree, then $L \sim L'$ if and only if $L'$ is a permutation of $L$.

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.4 the two lists have equal cores and equal $n_+$. Since the degrees are equal, $n_-$ is equal too, so the triples $(n_+, n_-, C)$ coincide. $\square$

Thus, at a fixed odd degree, the original moves identify nothing beyond reordering. At a fixed even degree a move class consists of the systems whose core agrees up to a global sign, with the parity data transformed accordingly.

## 6. The verdict on the double-minus conjecture

**Definition 6.1.** The original moves are *complete in degree $d$* if, for any two duality eigensystems $L$, $L'$ of degree $d$, the equality $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L')$ implies $L \sim L'$.

For integers $n \ge 2$ and $k \ge 0$ define the *test family*

$$F_{n,k} = \big(ns,\ s/n,\ \underbrace{s,\dots,s}_{k}\big).$$

**Theorem 6.2 (An infinite family in one sign class).** Let $n, m \ge 2$ and $k \ge 0$. Then:

1. $F_{n,k}$ is a duality eigensystem of degree $k+2$;
2. $\operatorname{sgn}(F_{n,k}) = +1$;
3. $F_{n,k} \sim F_{m,k}$ if and only if $n = m$.

*Proof sketch.* (1) We have $D(ns) = s/n$, $D(s/n) = ns$ and $D(s) = s$, and all entries are nonzero.

(2) First, $ns \neq \pm s$ because $n \ge 2$, and $s/n \neq \pm s$ for the same reason. So $n_-(F_{n,k}) = 0$, and Theorem 3.3 gives sign $+1$. Directly: $ns \cdot s/n \cdot s^k = s^{k+2}$.

(3) Suppose $F_{n,k} \sim F_{m,k}$. By Proposition 5.3, either $C(F_{m,k}) = C(F_{n,k})$ or $C(F_{m,k}) = -C(F_{n,k})$. Here $C(F_{n,k}) = \{ns, s/n\}$.

In the first case, $ms \in \{ns, s/n\}$. But $ms = s/n$ would force $mn = 1$, so $ms = ns$ and $m = n$.

In the second case, $-ms \in \{ns, s/n\}$. This would require $-m = n$ or $-mn = 1$, both impossible for positive integers.

The converse is trivial. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Exact boundary).** The original moves are complete in degree $d$ if and only if $d \le 1$.

*Proof sketch.* In degree $0$ there is only the empty list. In degree $1$, a singleton $(a)$ is $D$-stable only if $D(a) = a$, so $a = \pm s$. The systems $(s)$ and $(-s)$ have signs $+1$ and $-1$, so equal sign forces equal systems.

For $d \ge 2$, Theorem 6.2 with $k = d-2$ gives the eigensystems $F_{2,k}$ and $F_{3,k}$. They have the same degree and the same sign, but $F_{2,k} \not\sim F_{3,k}$. $\square$

Hence every sign class in degree $\ge 2$ that contains a system with a nonempty core (for example $F_{n,k}$, or $-F_{n,k}$ in even degree) splits into infinitely many move classes. The **core**, taken up to negation in even degree, is the finer invariant hidden behind the sign. By Theorem 5.4, the core together with the fixed-point data is the *complete* invariant of the original moves.

Systems without a core behave differently, and the answer depends on the parity of the degree.

**Proposition 6.4 (Core-free completeness in even degree).** Let $L, L'$ be lists of the same even length, with all entries in $\{s, -s\}$. If $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L')$, then $L \sim L'$.

*Proof.* Both lists are eigensystems with empty cores. Corollary 3.4 gives $n_-(L) \equiv n_-(L')$. Since $n_+ + n_- = d$ is the same for both, also $n_+(L) \equiv n_+(L')$. The untwisted branch of Theorem 5.4 applies. $\square$

**Proposition 6.5 (Core-free counterexample in odd degree).** The lists $(s,s,s)$ and $(s,-s,-s)$ are duality eigensystems of degree $3$, both of sign $+1$, but $(s,s,s) \not\sim (s,-s,-s)$.

*Proof.* In odd degree $n_+$ is a move invariant (Theorem 5.4), and here $3 \neq 1$. $\square$

Appending pairs $(s,s)$ to both lists gives a core-free counterexample in every odd degree $\ge 3$.

## 7. The repair: dual-pair moves

The double-minus move inserts the dual pair $\{-s, D(-s)\} = \{-s,-s\}$. The obstruction found in Section 6 disappears as soon as *every* dual pair may be inserted.

**Definition 7.1 (Dual-pair moves).** Let $\approx$ be the smallest equivalence relation on finite lists containing:

- **(P)** $L \approx L'$ whenever $L'$ is a permutation of $L$;
- **(DP) Dual-pair insertion.** $L \approx (\beta, D(\beta), L)$ for every $\beta \neq 0$, and hence, by symmetry, deletion of such a pair.

No twist is included, and none is needed. Taking $\beta = -s$ in (DP) recovers the double-minus move.

**Proposition 7.2 (Invariance).** If $L \approx L'$, then $\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L')$ and $|L| \equiv |L'| \pmod 2$.

*Proof.* The move (DP) multiplies the product by $\beta \cdot s^2/\beta = s^2$ and adds $2$ to the length. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Dual normal form).** Every duality eigensystem $L$ satisfies

$$L \approx \mathrm{nf}\big(n_+(L) \bmod 2,\ n_-(L) \bmod 2,\ \varnothing\big),$$

and this is one of the four lists $[\,]$, $[s,-s]$, $[s]$ and $[-s]$.

*Proof sketch.* Use strong induction on the length, with one *pair-removal step*. If $a \in L$ and another copy of $D(a)$ occurs in $L \setminus \{a\}$, then $L$ is a permutation of $(a, D(a), L'')$ with $L'' = L \setminus \{a, D(a)\}$. By Lemma 3.1, $L''$ is again an eigensystem, and $L \approx L''$ by a (DP) deletion. Three cases cover everything:

1. *Some $a \in L$ lies outside $\{\pm s\}$.* Then $D(a) \neq a$ and $D(a) \in L$, so the step applies. It leaves $n_\pm$ unchanged, so the parities are preserved.
2. *Otherwise, if $n_+ \geq 2$,* remove the pair $(s, s)$; this lowers $n_+$ by $2$.
3. *Otherwise, if $n_- \geq 2$,* remove the pair $(-s,-s)$.

If none of the cases applies, $L$ consists of at most one $s$ and at most one $-s$, so it is a permutation of the stated normal form. $\square$

**Theorem 7.4 (Dual-pair completeness, all degrees).** Let $L, L'$ be duality eigensystems of the same degree. Then

$$\operatorname{sgn}(L) = \operatorname{sgn}(L') \iff L \approx L'.$$

*Proof.* The direction "$\Leftarrow$" is Proposition 7.2. For "$\Rightarrow$", Corollary 3.4 gives $n_-(L) \equiv n_-(L')$. Since $n_+ \equiv d - n_- - |C|$ and cores of eigensystems have even size (they split into pairs $\{\beta, D(\beta)\}$ with $\beta \ne D(\beta)$), we also get $n_+(L) \equiv n_+(L')$. Theorem 7.3 then sends both lists to the same normal form. $\square$

In words: **the functional equation sees exactly the degree and the sign.** The invisible part of the eigenvalue data, namely the entire core and the even part of the fixed-point counts, is generated by inserting and deleting dual pairs. The four normal forms correspond to (degree parity, sign) $\in \{0,1\}\times\{\pm1\}$:

| normal form | degree parity | sign |
|---|---|---|
| $[\,]$ | even | $+1$ |
| $[s, -s]$ | even | $-1$ |
| $[s]$ | odd | $+1$ |
| $[-s]$ | odd | $-1$ |

**Proposition 7.5 (Strictness).** We have $(2s, s/2) \approx (3s, s/3)$, but $(2s, s/2) \not\sim (3s, s/3)$.

*Proof.* The first relation follows from Theorem 7.4, since both systems have degree $2$ and sign $+1$. Explicitly, delete $\{2s, s/2\}$ and insert $\{3s, s/3\}$. The second is Theorem 6.2 with $k = 0$. $\square$

## 8. Algorithms

The proofs are constructive and lead directly to linear-time procedures after hashing.

**Algorithm A (Sign via fixed-point parity).** Input: an eigensystem $L$ of length $d$. Count the occurrences of $-s$ and return $(-1)^{n_-}$. This takes $O(d)$ time. It avoids the $d$-fold product, which in floating point accumulates rounding error, and it gives an exact answer when the eigenvalues are given symbolically or through a hash key. A certificate of correctness is the matching of every remaining element with its dual, which exists by the proof of Theorem 3.2.

**Algorithm B (Decision procedure for the original moves).** Input: two lists $L$ and $L'$. Compute $n_\pm$ and the core multisets (as hash maps) of both lists. If $|L|$ is odd, test $R_{\mathrm{odd}}$. If $|L|$ is even, test the two branches of $R_{\mathrm{even}}$, comparing $C(L')$ with $C(L)$ and with $-C(L)$. This takes $O(d)$ expected time, or $O(d\log d)$ by sorting. When the answer is yes, a witnessing chain of moves is obtained by concatenating the reduction of Lemma 5.4.1 or 5.4.3 for $L$, a permutation, and the reversed reduction for $L'$ (twisted first, if necessary). The chain has $O(d)$ moves.

**Algorithm C (Dual-pair reduction with certificate).** Input: an eigensystem $L$. Bucket the entries by value. While some bucket for a value $a$ and the bucket for $D(a)$ can supply a pair, which requires two copies if $a = \pm s$, delete the pair and log the move "delete $\{a, D(a)\}$". Output the residual list, which is one of the four normal forms, together with the log. Each deletion removes two entries, so there are at most $d/2$ deletions, and the total cost is $O(d)$ expected. To connect $L$ with $L'$ of the same degree and sign, run C on both and concatenate the log of $L$ with the reversed log of $L'$, turning deletions into insertions.

**Numerical illustration.** We ran an exhaustive breadth-first search over all lists of length at most $6$ in the normalised alphabet $\{\pm1, \pm2, \pm\tfrac12\}$ (that is, eigenvalues $\pm s, \pm 2s, \pm s/2$): $924$ lists and about $1.5\times10^5$ same-degree pairs. Connectivity under the original moves agreed with the criterion of Theorem 5.4 in every case. The same search, extended to length $8$, gave the following class counts inside each sign class:

| degree | sign | eigensystems | original-move classes | dual-pair classes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $+1$ | 4 | 2 | 1 |
| 3 | $+1$ | 4 | 4 | 1 |
| 4 | $+1$ | 10 | 4 | 1 |
| 5 | $+1$ | 10 | 10 | 1 |
| 6 | $+1$ | 20 | 6 | 1 |
| 7 | $+1$ | 20 | 20 | 1 |
| 8 | $+1$ | 35 | 9 | 1 |

The odd-degree rows show Corollary 5.5: every eigensystem is its own class. The last column shows Theorem 7.4.

## 9. Discussion

**What the functional equation can and cannot see.** Theorem 7.4 gives a complete normal form for duality eigensystems modulo the sign: given the degree, the sign is the only information the functional equation retains, and every finer feature can be created or destroyed by dual-pair moves. The original proposal located the invisible part in the $-\sqrt{Q}$ fixed points, because those are what the sign formula counts. This was half right. The fixed points determine the sign, but the invisible part also contains all genuine dual pairs, and these cannot be generated by fixed-point moves.

**Role of the twist.** The twist is the only scalar operation compatible with duality. It plays a double role. In even degree it identifies a core with its negative, and through the twist–insert–twist chain of Lemma 5.4.2 it makes $+\sqrt{Q}$ pairs disposable. In odd degree it is unavailable, and this rigidity explains Corollary 5.5 and the core-free counterexample of Proposition 6.5.

**Structure of the moduli of original-move classes.** At a fixed even degree $2k$, the original-move classes are indexed by the core modulo negation together with a pair of parities. A dual pair $\{\beta, Q/\beta\}$ with $\beta \neq \pm s$ is determined by its trace $\tau = \beta + Q/\beta \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2s\}$, and negation acts by $\tau \mapsto -\tau$. This suggests the parametrisation stated as Conjecture 10.2 below.

**Relation to arithmetic.** For a Weil $q$-polynomial (integer coefficients, all $|\alpha_i| = \sqrt{Q}$) the normalised eigenvalues lie on the unit circle, and $-s$ occurs only through specific factors $1 + sT$ (when $s \in \mathbb{Z}$) or $1 - QT^2$ (when $s \notin \mathbb{Z}$). The sign formula is then a statement about the multiplicity of these factors. Example 3.5 shows how the supersingular case produces $-\sqrt{Q}$ fixed points in tensor constructions.

## 10. Future directions

**Conjecture 10.1 (Integral dual-pair completeness).** For eigensystems with integral characteristic polynomial whose eigenvalues are Weil numbers, Theorem 7.4 continues to hold when every intermediate system in the chain of dual-pair moves is required to have an integral characteristic polynomial and Weil-number eigenvalues. A dual pair $\{\beta, q^w/\beta\}$ of Weil numbers is cut out by the integral quadratic $T^2 - aT + q^w$ with $|a| \le 2q^{w/2}$, so the deletions of Theorem 7.3 should be grouped into Galois orbits. Theorem 7.3 deletes one pair at a time, so only this Galois-orbit bookkeeping is missing.

**Conjecture 10.2 (Moduli of the core).** In degree $2k$, with the fixed-point data held fixed, the classes of the original moves are in bijection with $\operatorname{Sym}^k(\mathbb{C}\setminus\{\pm 2s\})/(z\mapsto -z)$ via the trace map $\alpha \mapsto \alpha + Q/\alpha$. The trace is a complete invariant of a dual pair, and the twist acts on traces by negation. Theorem 5.4 already reduces the question to the core multiset up to negation, so only the trace parametrisation remains.

**Conjecture 10.3 (Orthogonal versus symplectic generators).** For self-dual systems carrying a pairing of sign $\pm1$, the kernel of the root number is generated by dual-pair moves, together with moves inserting pairs of $(-1)$-eigenvalues in the orthogonal case only. The sign formula computes a determinant of the fixed part, and the determinant of a symplectic transformation is always $+1$. The $\pm\sqrt{Q}$ analysis of Section 3 is the rank-one shadow of this determinant computation.

**Direction 10.4 (Counting over finite alphabets).** For a finite $D$-stable alphabet $A$, count the original-move classes inside each sign class of degree $d$ as a function of $d$ and $|A|$. The data in Section 8 suggest that in odd degree the count equals the number of eigensystems, as Corollary 5.5 predicts, while in even degree it grows like the number of cores modulo negation.

## 11. Conclusion

We determined the sign of the functional equation of a duality eigensystem exactly: it is $(-1)^{n_-}$, a single parity bit. We classified completely the moves originally proposed to generate its kernel, and identified the core as the finer invariant they preserve. This proves that the double-minus conjecture holds exactly in degrees $0$ and $1$. Allowing arbitrary dual pairs to be inserted and deleted repairs it in every degree, with four normal forms. The resulting picture is a complete classification of what the functional equation can and cannot see: it sees the degree and the sign, and nothing else.

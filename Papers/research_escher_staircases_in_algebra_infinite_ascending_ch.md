## Abstract

We study an **Escher staircase**, defined to be a strictly increasing sequence of ideals in a commutative ring. The central result identifies existence of such a staircase exactly with failure of Noetherianity. We give an explicit construction in the product ring of integer sequences: the ideals of sequences supported in an initial finite segment form a strict ascending chain whose intersection is the zero ideal, equal to its initial rung. We also discuss the analogous chain generated by successively many variables in a countably generated polynomial ring. In contrast, finite-variable polynomial rings over fields and the ring of $p$-adic integers admit no such staircase because they are Noetherian. A proposed construction based on pointwise divisibility by $2^n$ is shown to have the reverse orientation: its ideals form a strictly descending chain, separated by constant functions. These results clarify that staircase existence is not a new invariant but a reformulation of the ascending chain condition. We explain why the intersection condition is automatic, why Krull dimension cannot serve as the proposed staircase height, and what additional structure would be required for a meaningful refined invariant. We conclude with algorithms and finite numerical models that expose the relevant containments and witnesses.

## 1. Introduction

Ascending chains of ideals are a basic language for algebraic growth. Starting from an ideal $I_0$, one may adjoin new generators, close under ring operations, and obtain a sequence

$$
I_0\subseteq I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots.
$$

The Noetherian condition asserts that this process cannot create genuinely new ideals forever. It is one of the principal finiteness hypotheses in commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, and symbolic computation.

The term **Escher staircase** suggests an infinite strict ascent with a “loop-back” feature. The natural formal content is an infinite chain

$$
I_0\subsetneq I_1\subsetneq I_2\subsetneq\cdots.
$$

If the chain begins with a least rung, its intersection is automatically that least rung. Thus the loop-back image is legitimate as a metaphor: every higher rung retains the common foundation. It is not, however, an extra algebraic condition. Moreover, the intersection contains ring elements, whereas an ideal is itself a set-like substructure; an ideal cannot literally be an element of that intersection without changing the ambient type.

The first objective of this paper is to isolate the precise theorem behind the metaphor: a commutative ring admits an Escher staircase if and only if it is not Noetherian. The second is to exhibit explicit positive and negative examples. The ring of all integer sequences has a staircase obtained by allowing one new coordinate at each stage. A polynomial ring in countably many variables has another, obtained by adjoining one new variable at each stage. Finite-variable polynomial rings over fields and $p$-adic integer rings provide negative instances.

The third objective is diagnostic. The ideals defined by requiring every function value to be divisible by $2^n$ become smaller, not larger, as $n$ increases. This orientation check is elementary but decisive. It illustrates a general computational distinction between adding generators, which enlarges ideals, and strengthening divisibility constraints, which shrinks them.

Finally, we consider the proposed notion of an “Escher height.” Under the definition above, mere staircase existence is binary and coincides with non-Noetherianity. Krull dimension does not repair this issue: finite-variable polynomial rings have positive finite Krull dimension but are Noetherian. Any numerical or ordinal refinement must therefore introduce genuinely new data.

Throughout, rings are commutative with identity, and ideals are two-sided automatically.

## 2. Definitions and order-theoretic background

### Definition 2.1 (Strict ideal chain)

Let $R$ be a commutative ring. A sequence $(I_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$ of ideals is **strictly ascending** if

$$
m<n\implies I_m\subsetneq I_n.
$$

It is enough to verify the adjacent containments $I_n\subsetneq I_{n+1}$ for all $n$.

### Definition 2.2 (Escher staircase)

An **Escher staircase** in $R$ is a strictly ascending sequence $(I_n)_{n\in\mathbb N}$ of ideals of $R$.

The definition imposes no separate intersection condition. If one did require $I_0=\bigcap_n I_n$, the requirement would be redundant, as shown below.

### Definition 2.3 (Ascending chain condition and Noetherianity)

A ring $R$ satisfies the **ascending chain condition on ideals** if every chain

$$
J_0\subseteq J_1\subseteq J_2\subseteq\cdots
$$

is eventually constant: there exists $N$ such that $J_n=J_N$ for every $n\ge N$. A commutative ring satisfying this condition is called **Noetherian**.

Equivalent formulations say that every ideal is finitely generated, or that every nonempty collection of ideals has a maximal member. The chain formulation is the one directly relevant here.

### Lemma 2.4 (Intersection of an ascending chain)

If $I_0\subseteq I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots$, then

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge 0}I_n=I_0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Every member of the intersection lies in $I_0$ because $I_0$ is one of the intersected sets. Conversely, every element of $I_0$ lies in every $I_n$ by monotonicity. The two containments give equality. $\square$

This elementary lemma explains the “loop-back” feature without mystification. If $I_0$ is the zero ideal, the common core is $\{0\}$. In addition, the zero element belongs to every ideal in every ring, so merely requiring $0\in I_0\cap\bigcap_n I_n$ carries no information.

## 3. The characterization theorem

### Theorem 3.1 (Staircase characterization)

A commutative ring $R$ admits an Escher staircase if and only if $R$ is not Noetherian.

**Proof sketch.** Suppose first that $(I_n)$ is an Escher staircase. It is an ascending chain that is never constant, since $I_n\subsetneq I_{n+1}$ for every $n$. Hence the ascending chain condition fails, so $R$ is not Noetherian.

Conversely, suppose $R$ is not Noetherian. Then there exists an ascending chain $(J_n)$ that is not eventually constant. We recursively extract strict growth. Choose $n_0=0$. Once $n_k$ is chosen, non-stabilization ensures that some $n_{k+1}>n_k$ satisfies $J_{n_k}\subsetneq J_{n_{k+1}}$; otherwise every later ideal would equal $J_{n_k}$. Set $I_k=J_{n_k}$. Then $I_k\subsetneq I_{k+1}$ for every $k$, and transitivity gives $I_m\subsetneq I_n$ whenever $m<n$. Thus $(I_k)$ is an Escher staircase. $\square$

### Corollary 3.2

Staircase existence is not independent of established finiteness theory: it is precisely the negation of Noetherianity.

### Corollary 3.3

Every Noetherian ring has no Escher staircase, and every non-Noetherian commutative ring has at least one.

The theorem is useful in both directions. An explicit staircase proves non-Noetherianity. Conversely, a structural proof of Noetherianity rules out every possible staircase without examining candidate chains one at a time.

## 4. An explicit staircase in the ring of integer sequences

Let

$$
R=\mathbb Z^{\mathbb N}=\prod_{k\in\mathbb N}\mathbb Z
$$

with coordinatewise operations. An element $a\in R$ is a sequence $a=(a_0,a_1,a_2,\ldots)$.

### Definition 4.1 (Initial-support ideals)

For each $n\in\mathbb N$, define

$$
S_n=\{a\in R: \text{for every }k\ge n,\ a_k=0\}.
$$

Equivalently, $S_n$ consists of sequences whose support is contained in $\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}$.

### Lemma 4.2

For every $n$, the set $S_n$ is an ideal of $R$.

**Proof sketch.** The zero sequence belongs to $S_n$. If $a,b\in S_n$, then for $k\ge n$ one has $(a+b)_k=a_k+b_k=0$. If $c\in R$ and $a\in S_n$, then for $k\ge n$ one has $(ca)_k=c_ka_k=0$. Thus $S_n$ is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by arbitrary ring elements. $\square$

For $n=0$, the condition requires every coordinate to vanish, so $S_0=\{0\}$. At the next stages,

$$
S_1=\{(a_0,0,0,\ldots):a_0\in\mathbb Z\},
$$

and

$$
S_2=\{(a_0,a_1,0,0,\ldots):a_0,a_1\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

### Lemma 4.3 (Strict adjacent growth)

For every $n\in\mathbb N$,

$$
S_n\subsetneq S_{n+1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** If a sequence vanishes for all $k\ge n$, then it vanishes for all $k\ge n+1$, proving $S_n\subseteq S_{n+1}$. Let $e_n$ be the coordinate indicator defined by $(e_n)_n=1$ and $(e_n)_k=0$ for $k\ne n$. It vanishes at every coordinate $k\ge n+1$, so $e_n\in S_{n+1}$. But it does not vanish at coordinate $n$, so $e_n\notin S_n$. $\square$

### Theorem 4.4 (Product-ring staircase)

The sequence

$$
S_0\subsetneq S_1\subsetneq S_2\subsetneq\cdots
$$

is an Escher staircase in $\mathbb Z^{\mathbb N}$. Its intersection is the zero ideal:

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge 0}S_n=S_0=\{0\}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Strictness follows from Lemma 4.3. The intersection equality follows either directly or from Lemma 2.4 and the identity $S_0=\{0\}$. $\square$

### Corollary 4.5

The ring $\mathbb Z^{\mathbb N}$ is not Noetherian.

The construction is algorithmically transparent. At stage $n$, the first $n$ coordinates are available and the tail is forced to zero. The next stage frees exactly one new coordinate. The indicator $e_n$ is a compact certificate of strictness.

## 5. Polynomial rings: countably many versus finitely many variables

Let $k$ be a field and consider the polynomial ring in countably many indeterminates,

$$
P=k[x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots].
$$

Every polynomial uses only finitely many variables, but there is no global finite list containing all available variables.

### Definition 5.1 (Variable-prefix ideals)

For $n\in\mathbb N$, define

$$
J_n=(x_0,x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}),
$$

with $J_0=(0)$.

### Lemma 5.2

For every $n$,

$$
J_n\subsetneq J_{n+1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The inclusion follows because every generator of $J_n$ is also a generator of $J_{n+1}$. The variable $x_n$ belongs to $J_{n+1}$. To show $x_n\notin J_n$, apply the homomorphism that sends $x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}$ to $0$ and leaves $x_n$ as an indeterminate. Every element of $J_n$ maps to $0$, while $x_n$ maps to itself and is nonzero. $\square$

### Theorem 5.3 (Countable-variable staircase)

The ideals $(J_n)_{n\ge 0}$ form an Escher staircase in $k[x_0,x_1,x_2,\ldots]$. Their intersection is $(0)$.

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 5.2 supplies adjacent strictness. Since $J_0=(0)$ and the chain is ascending, Lemma 2.4 gives the intersection. $\square$

This is the generator analogue of the coordinate construction: each stage introduces one independent variable.

The finite-variable case behaves differently.

### Theorem 5.4 (Finite-variable obstruction)

For every field $k$ and every finite $d\ge 0$, the polynomial ring

$$
k[x_1,\ldots,x_d]
$$

has no Escher staircase.

**Proof sketch.** A field is Noetherian. Hilbert's basis theorem states that if $A$ is Noetherian, then $A[x]$ is Noetherian. Iterating the theorem $d$ times proves that $k[x_1,\ldots,x_d]$ is Noetherian. The Staircase Characterization Theorem then rules out an Escher staircase. $\square$

### Remark 5.5 (Krull dimension is different)

The Krull dimension of $k[x_1,\ldots,x_d]$ is $d$, witnessed by finite chains of prime ideals such as

$$
(0)\subsetneq(x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq(x_1,\ldots,x_d).
$$

This does not contradict Theorem 5.4. Krull dimension measures lengths of chains of prime ideals, while Noetherianity prohibits infinite ascending chains of arbitrary ideals. A finite chain of length $d$ is compatible with the ascending chain condition. Consequently, the assertion that finite-variable polynomial rings have “Escher height $d$” conflates two distinct notions unless height is redefined.

## 6. Divisibility ideals and the orientation reversal

Let $F=\mathbb Z^{\mathbb Z}$ be the ring of all functions $f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, again with pointwise operations.

### Definition 6.1 (Power-divisibility ideals)

For $n\in\mathbb N$, define

$$
D_n=\{f\in F: 2^n\mid f(z)\text{ for every }z\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

### Lemma 6.2

Each $D_n$ is an ideal of $F$.

**Proof sketch.** The zero function is divisible pointwise by $2^n$. Sums of multiples of $2^n$ remain multiples of $2^n$. If $f(z)$ is divisible by $2^n$, then so is $c(z)f(z)$ for every function $c$. These statements hold at each integer input $z$. $\square$

### Theorem 6.3 (Strict descent of divisibility rungs)

For every $n$,

$$
D_{n+1}\subsetneq D_n.
$$

Consequently, $(D_n)$ is strictly descending and is not an Escher staircase.

**Proof sketch.** Since $2^n$ divides $2^{n+1}$, every multiple of $2^{n+1}$ is a multiple of $2^n$, giving $D_{n+1}\subseteq D_n$. For strictness, take the constant function $c_n(z)=2^n$. It belongs to $D_n$, but $2^{n+1}$ does not divide its value $2^n$, so $c_n\notin D_{n+1}$. $\square$

### Corollary 6.4

The same proposed rungs remain descending in any function subring containing the constant functions $2^n$ and in which the pointwise divisibility condition defines ideals. In particular, restricting attention to integer-valued polynomials does not reverse the containment.

The theorem highlights an important design rule. An index that raises a divisibility exponent tightens membership and therefore usually produces an antitone family. To build an ascending chain, one normally relaxes conditions or adds generators.

### Remark 6.5 (The intersection of the descending chain)

For integer-valued functions, if $f\in\bigcap_nD_n$, then every value $f(z)$ is divisible by every power of $2$. The only such integer is $0$, so $f$ is the zero function. Hence

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge 0}D_n=\{0\}.
$$

This zero intersection does not make the chain ascending. Direction and intersection are independent features.

## 7. The $p$-adic integers: an infinite object with no staircase

Fix a prime $p$. The ring of $p$-adic integers, denoted $\mathbb Z_p$, is a discrete valuation ring with uniformizer $p$. Every nonzero ideal has the form $(p^m)$ for some $m\ge 0$.

### Theorem 7.1 ($p$-adic obstruction)

The ring $\mathbb Z_p$ admits no Escher staircase.

**Proof sketch.** Every discrete valuation ring is a principal ideal domain and hence Noetherian. The Staircase Characterization Theorem therefore excludes every infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals. More concretely, the nonzero ideals are ordered as

$$
\mathbb Z_p\supsetneq(p)\supsetneq(p^2)\supsetneq\cdots.
$$

Moving upward by inclusion decreases the valuation exponent, which can happen only finitely many times from any given principal ideal. $\square$

The infinite digit expansion of a $p$-adic integer does not imply non-Noetherian ideal behavior. Topological infinitude and algebraic failure of the ascending chain condition are distinct.

## 8. Algorithms and computational demonstrations

The results above are structural, but finite truncations provide useful diagnostics.

### Algorithm 8.1 (Initial-support membership)

Given a finite vector $a=(a_0,\ldots,a_{L-1})$ and a rung $n\le L$, declare that $a$ belongs to the truncated model of $S_n$ exactly when

$$
a_k=0\quad\text{for every }n\le k<L.
$$

The test inspects at most $L-n$ entries, so its time complexity is $O(L-n)$ and its auxiliary-space complexity is $O(1)$. Strictness is witnessed by the unit vector $e_n$, which passes membership at rung $n+1$ and fails at rung $n$.

### Algorithm 8.2 (Pointwise power-divisibility membership)

Given sampled values $(f(z_1),\ldots,f(z_L))$ and an exponent $n$, test whether every value is congruent to $0$ modulo $2^n$. This costs $O(L)$ arithmetic divisibility tests and $O(1)$ auxiliary space. The constant sample $2^n$ certifies $D_{n+1}\subsetneq D_n$.

### Algorithm 8.3 (Finite chain orientation audit)

For finite approximations to sets $A_0,\ldots,A_m$, compare adjacent membership sets. Report “ascending” if every $A_i\subseteq A_{i+1}$, “descending” if every $A_{i+1}\subseteq A_i$, and “mixed” otherwise; separately record strict witnesses. With bit-set representations over a universe of size $U$, this costs $O(mU)$ time and $O(U)$ working space.

Finite computations do not prove the infinite theorems by themselves, but they expose orientation errors and produce the same witnesses used in the general proofs.

## 9. Applications and interpretation

### 9.1 Symbolic computation

Noetherianity is closely tied to finite generation. In polynomial computation over finitely many variables, ideals admit finite generating sets, enabling terminating methods such as Gröbner basis algorithms under suitable monomial orders. The countably generated polynomial staircase identifies what fails when infinitely many independent variables are available: no finite stage can absorb the next variable.

### 9.2 Product systems and local coordinates

The sequence-ring example models systems with countably many independent coordinates. Each initial-support ideal represents perturbations confined to a finite prefix. The strict chain records the fact that a new independent coordinate always creates a new ideal element. Similar constructions occur in infinite products of nonzero rings.

### 9.3 Constraint orientation

Divisibility, congruence, and vanishing-order conditions are frequently indexed by a nonnegative integer. Increasing the index usually strengthens the constraint and shrinks the admissible set. Theorem 6.3 gives a reusable audit pattern: prove the divisibility implication and find a boundary element satisfying level $n$ but not level $n+1$.

### 9.4 Choosing invariants carefully

A proposed invariant must distinguish examples in a way not already determined by its definition. Since staircase existence is equivalent to non-Noetherianity, assigning a binary existence value merely renames an established property. Assigning finite-variable polynomial rings a positive staircase height conflicts with the definition because those rings have no staircase. This does not prevent a new invariant, but it requires a new definition.

## 10. Toward a meaningful notion of height

Several possible refinements deserve investigation.

First, one could consider **ordinal chain height**: the supremum of order types of strictly ascending chains of ideals. Such a quantity requires care because it may be a proper-class issue without cardinal bounds, and its relationship to existing ordinal ranks must be clarified.

Second, one could restrict the class of ideals, for example to prime, radical, principal, or finitely generated ideals. Each restriction changes the phenomenon. Chains of prime ideals connect to Krull dimension; chains of principal ideals connect to divisibility and factorization.

Third, a **generator-growth profile** could ask for the least number of generators needed at successive stages, or the rate at which new independent generators must appear. This could distinguish the coordinate and variable-prefix examples while retaining computational meaning.

Fourth, one might study a **finite observation function**: for a chosen representation and resource bound, how many strict ideal enlargements can an algorithm certify? Such a quantity would depend on presentation unless invariance under changes of generators or ring isomorphisms were proved.

Any successful definition should satisfy at least four tests: invariance under ring isomorphism, agreement with the intended examples, separation from ordinary Krull dimension and Noetherianity, and computability or estimability on a substantial class of rings.

## 11. Algebraic integers

The phrase “the ring of all algebraic integers” should be made precise. A standard choice is the integral closure $\overline{\mathbb Z}$ of $\mathbb Z$ in a fixed algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ of $\mathbb Q$. Elements are algebraic numbers satisfying monic polynomials with integer coefficients.

A plausible route to an explicit ascending chain is to choose compatible roots of a fixed prime $p$. If elements $\alpha_n$ satisfy appropriate relations such as $\alpha_{n+1}^{q}=\alpha_n$ for a fixed prime $q$, then the associated principal ideals may be comparable. Their orientation must be checked from the equation: from $\alpha_n=\alpha_{n+1}^{q}$ one obtains $(\alpha_n)\subseteq(\alpha_{n+1})$. Strictness requires an additional argument, often using valuations or algebraic norms in finite subextensions.

Once non-Noetherianity of the chosen ring is established, Theorem 3.1 yields an Escher staircase immediately. It does not yield a canonical numerical height. A height computation must wait for a coherent definition and a proof that it is independent of auxiliary choices such as the algebraic closure, roots, or valuation extensions.

## 12. Discussion

The main theorem resolves the existence question completely. The positive examples are not anomalies: they are concrete witnesses to non-Noetherianity. The negative examples are not failures of imagination: Noetherianity rules out every candidate.

The intersection language requires special care. For an ascending chain beginning at $I_0$, equality $I_0=\bigcap_nI_n$ follows from order alone. If the intended “loop” was meant to impose something stronger, it must be formulated differently—perhaps through a limit, completion, recurrence, or self-similar operation. Likewise, the statement that an ideal is an element of an intersection of ideals confuses levels of structure.

The orientation correction is equally central. For the divisibility ideals, larger exponents mean fewer functions. The chain's zero intersection is compatible with strict descent and does not turn it into an ascending chain. This is the algebraic analogue of distinguishing a filtration from a cofiltration.

Finally, dimension should not be used as a universal synonym for chain length. Krull dimension concerns prime ideals and permits finite chains inside Noetherian rings. Escher staircases concern infinite ascending chains of arbitrary ideals and occur exactly outside the Noetherian class.

## 13. Conclusion

An Escher staircase, understood as an infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals, exists precisely in non-Noetherian commutative rings. The ring of integer sequences supplies an explicit staircase of finite-support prefixes, beginning and intersecting at the zero ideal. The countably generated polynomial ring supplies another by adjoining variables one at a time. Finite-variable polynomial rings over fields and $p$-adic integer rings admit none.

The pointwise $2^n$-divisibility construction does not furnish an ascending staircase: it forms a strict descending chain, with the constant function $2^n$ separating successive levels. This correction illustrates the importance of auditing inclusion direction before drawing conclusions from indexed constraints.

The architectural metaphor remains useful when interpreted correctly. The endless ascent records failure of the ascending chain condition; the return to the first rung records the elementary fact that the intersection of an ascending chain is its least member. A genuinely new height theory will require more than existence, more than an automatic intersection, and more than Krull dimension. It must specify what is measured and demonstrate that the measurement captures new algebraic information.

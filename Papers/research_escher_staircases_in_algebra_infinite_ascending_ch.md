# Divisibility Filtrations and the Ascending-Chain Obstruction

## Abstract

A proposed “Escher staircase” of ideals arises by requiring integer-valued functions or polynomials to take values divisible by successively higher powers of two. Careful analysis reverses the advertised containment: stronger divisibility defines smaller ideals. This paper develops the corrected structure in two settings. For the ring of all functions $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, and for the ring

$$
\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)=\{p\in\mathbb Q[X]:p(\mathbb Z)\subseteq\mathbb Z\},
$$

the powers-of-two ideals form an infinite strictly descending filtration with zero intersection. Strictness is witnessed by constant functions or polynomials $2^n$; separation follows because a nonzero integer cannot be divisible by every power of two, together with the fact that a nonzero rational polynomial has only finitely many roots. In the opposite direction, every ascending chain of ideals in a Noetherian ring stabilizes, so no Noetherian ring—including a finite-variable polynomial ring over a field or a discrete valuation ring—admits an infinite strict ascending chain. These facts separate three notions that should not be conflated: descending divisibility filtrations, arbitrary ascending ideal chains, and prime-ideal chains defining Krull dimension. We also give finite algorithms for visualizing divisibility depth and strictness witnesses, explain their limitations, and identify natural generalizations.

## 1. Introduction

Nested ideals encode scale. Powers of an ideal describe progressively stronger vanishing, prime powers record arithmetic precision, and filtrations organize approximation. Because the index usually increases as the condition becomes stronger, it is easy to mistake an indexed family for an ascending chain. The family studied here offers a clean example of why containment must be checked rather than inferred from notation.

Let $D_n$ consist of integer-valued functions whose values are divisible by $2^n$. Increasing $n$ makes membership harder, so $D_{n+1}\subseteq D_n$. The constant function $2^n$ proves that containment is strict. Moreover, a function lying in every $D_n$ must vanish pointwise. The same argument transfers to integer-valued rational polynomials, with polynomial rigidity converting vanishing on all integers into equality with the zero polynomial.

This corrected interpretation also clarifies an alleged “loop.” Zero belongs to every ideal in every ring, so its presence in an intersection carries no special information. An intersection of ideals contains ring elements, not the ideals themselves. Thus common membership of zero cannot make a chain return to its beginning.

The complementary issue is ascent. A Noetherian ring is characterized by stabilization of every ascending ideal chain. Consequently, an infinite sequence of strict inclusions is impossible in such a ring. In particular, finite-variable polynomial rings over fields cannot have an “ascending height” equal to their Krull dimension: Krull dimension concerns chains of prime ideals, and finite Krull dimension does not count the length of a nonexistent infinite strict ascending chain of arbitrary ideals.

The main results are:

1. The function ideals $D_n$ form a strict descending chain and satisfy $\bigcap_nD_n=\{0\}$.
2. The corresponding ideals $J_n$ in $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$ form a strict descending chain, never stabilize, and satisfy $\bigcap_nJ_n=\{0\}$.
3. No Noetherian commutative ring admits an infinite strictly ascending natural-number-indexed chain of ideals.
4. Hence finite-variable polynomial rings over fields, and likewise discrete valuation rings, have no such ascending chain.

## 2. Algebraic preliminaries

### 2.1 Rings and ideals

Throughout, rings are commutative and have an identity. An **ideal** $I$ of a ring $R$ is an additive subgroup such that $ri\in I$ whenever $r\in R$ and $i\in I$. Ideals are ordered by inclusion. The zero ideal is $\{0\}$, and every ideal contains it.

A family $I_0,I_1,\ldots$ is **ascending** if $I_n\subseteq I_{n+1}$ for all $n$, and **strictly ascending** if every such inclusion is proper. It is **descending** if $I_{n+1}\subseteq I_n$, and **strictly descending** if every inclusion is proper. A descending family is **separated** if

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge0}I_n=\{0\}.
$$

It **stabilizes** if there is an $N$ such that $I_n=I_N$ for all $n\ge N$.

### 2.2 Noetherianity and Krull dimension

A ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if every ideal is finitely generated. Equivalently, every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes. The equivalence is fundamental: finite generation prevents genuinely new generators from appearing forever, while stabilization implies that the ideal generated by an arbitrary set is already generated by finitely many of its elements.

The **Krull dimension** of $R$ is the supremum of the lengths of chains of prime ideals. Thus a chain relevant to dimension has the form

$$
\mathfrak p_0\subsetneq\mathfrak p_1\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq\mathfrak p_r,
$$

where each $\mathfrak p_i$ is prime. Krull dimension is not the supremum of lengths of arbitrary ascending chains of ideals. A Noetherian ring may have positive or large finite Krull dimension while still forbidding every infinite strict ascending ideal chain.

### 2.3 Integer-valued polynomials

The ring of integer-valued polynomials is

$$
\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)
=
\{p\in\mathbb Q[X]:p(z)\in\mathbb Z\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

It is a subring of $\mathbb Q[X]$: the constant polynomials $0$ and $1$ are included, and sums, products, and negatives preserve integer values. It is larger than $\mathbb Z[X]$. For example, $X(X-1)/2$ is integer-valued because the product of two consecutive integers is even.

For every $z\in\mathbb Z$, evaluation defines a ring homomorphism

$$
\operatorname{ev}_z:\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)\longrightarrow\mathbb Z,
\qquad
p\longmapsto p(z).
$$

This map allows pointwise divisibility conditions to be expressed as intersections of inverse images of principal ideals.

## 3. The function-ring filtration

Let $F=\mathbb Z^{\mathbb Z}$ be the ring of all functions $f:\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z$, with pointwise operations. For each $n\ge0$, define

$$
D_n=\{f\in F:2^n\mid f(z)\text{ for every }z\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

### Lemma 3.1 (Ideal property)

For each $n$, the subset $D_n$ is an ideal of $F$.

**Proof sketch.** The zero function belongs to $D_n$. If $f,g\in D_n$, then $2^n$ divides both $f(z)$ and $g(z)$ for every $z$, hence divides $(f+g)(z)$. If $a\in F$ and $f\in D_n$, then $2^n$ divides $a(z)f(z)$ for every $z$. Therefore $af\in D_n$. $\square$

### Lemma 3.2 (Antitonicity)

If $m\le n$, then $D_n\subseteq D_m$.

**Proof sketch.** Since $2^m$ divides $2^n$, divisibility by $2^n$ implies divisibility by $2^m$ at every input. $\square$

### Lemma 3.3 (Consecutive witness)

For every $n\ge0$, the constant function $c_n(z)=2^n$ belongs to $D_n$ but not to $D_{n+1}$.

**Proof sketch.** The first assertion is immediate. The second follows because $2^{n+1}$ does not divide $2^n$. Equivalently, a nonzero multiple of $2^{n+1}$ has absolute value at least $2^{n+1}>2^n$. $\square$

### Theorem 3.4 (Strict descent for integer-valued functions)

For every $n\ge0$,

$$
D_{n+1}\subsetneq D_n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 3.2 gives containment, and Lemma 3.3 supplies an element of $D_n\setminus D_{n+1}$. $\square$

### Lemma 3.5 (Infinite binary divisibility)

If $x\in\mathbb Z$ and $2^n\mid x$ for every $n\ge0$, then $x=0$.

**Proof sketch.** If $x\ne0$, choose $n$ with $2^n>|x|$. Divisibility gives $x=2^nq$ for a nonzero integer $q$, so $|x|=2^n|q|\ge2^n$, a contradiction. $\square$

### Theorem 3.6 (Separation of the function filtration)

The common intersection is the zero ideal:

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge0}D_n=\{0\}.
$$

Equivalently, a function belongs to every $D_n$ if and only if it is the zero function.

**Proof sketch.** If $f$ lies in every $D_n$, fix $z$. Then $2^n$ divides $f(z)$ for every $n$, so Lemma 3.5 yields $f(z)=0$. Since this holds for every $z$, $f=0$. Conversely, zero belongs to every ideal. $\square$

## 4. The filtration in $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$

For each $n\ge0$, define

$$
J_n=
\{p\in\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z):2^n\mid p(z)
\text{ for every }z\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
J_n=\bigcap_{z\in\mathbb Z}\operatorname{ev}_z^{-1}((2^n)),
$$

where $(2^n)$ denotes the principal ideal in $\mathbb Z$ generated by $2^n$.

### Lemma 4.1 (Ideal property and membership criterion)

For every $n$, $J_n$ is an ideal of $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$, and membership is exactly the pointwise divisibility condition in its definition.

**Proof sketch.** The inverse image of an ideal under a ring homomorphism is an ideal, and arbitrary intersections of ideals are ideals. Since $a\in(2^n)$ precisely when $2^n\mid a$, the displayed intersection has the asserted membership criterion. $\square$

### Lemma 4.2 (Antitonicity in the polynomial ring)

If $m\le n$, then $J_n\subseteq J_m$.

**Proof sketch.** For every integer $z$, divisibility of $p(z)$ by $2^n$ implies divisibility by $2^m$. $\square$

### Lemma 4.3 (Constant-polynomial witness)

For every $n\ge0$, the constant polynomial $2^n$ lies in $J_n$ but not in $J_{n+1}$.

**Proof sketch.** Evaluation at any integer returns $2^n$. It is divisible by $2^n$ but not by $2^{n+1}$. $\square$

### Theorem 4.4 (Strict divisibility filtration)

For every $n\ge0$,

$$
J_{n+1}\subsetneq J_n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Combine Lemma 4.2 with the witness in Lemma 4.3. $\square$

### Corollary 4.5 (Nonstabilization)

There is no $N$ such that $J_n=J_N$ for all $n\ge N$.

**Proof sketch.** Stabilization at $N$ would imply $J_{N+1}=J_N$, contradicting Theorem 4.4. $\square$

### Lemma 4.6 (Rigidity from integer evaluations)

If $p\in\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$ and $p(z)=0$ for every $z\in\mathbb Z$, then $p=0$.

**Proof sketch.** The polynomial is an element of $\mathbb Q[X]$. A nonzero polynomial over a field has at most as many roots as its degree. Since all integers are roots and $\mathbb Z$ is infinite, $p$ must be zero. $\square$

### Theorem 4.7 (Zero intersection in the integer-valued polynomial ring)

The filtration is separated:

$$
\bigcap_{n\ge0}J_n=\{0\}.
$$

Equivalently, an integer-valued polynomial belongs to every $J_n$ if and only if it is the zero polynomial.

**Proof sketch.** Let $p$ lie in every $J_n$. Fix $z\in\mathbb Z$. Then $2^n\mid p(z)$ for every $n$, so Lemma 3.5 gives $p(z)=0$. Thus $p$ vanishes at all integers, and Lemma 4.6 gives $p=0$. The reverse implication follows because zero lies in every ideal. $\square$

Theorem 4.7 is stronger than the statement that zero merely belongs to the intersection. It identifies the entire intersection. Since every ideal contains zero, common membership of zero alone imposes no condition.

## 5. The obstruction to infinite ascent

### Theorem 5.1 (Noetherian ascending-chain obstruction)

Let $R$ be a Noetherian commutative ring, and let $I_0,I_1,\ldots$ be ideals satisfying $I_n\subseteq I_{n+1}$ for every $n$. Then it is impossible that $I_n\subsetneq I_{n+1}$ for every $n$.

**Proof sketch.** By the ascending-chain condition, there is an $N$ such that $I_n=I_N$ for every $n\ge N$. Taking $n=N+1$ yields $I_{N+1}=I_N$, contradicting strictness at $N$. $\square$

### Corollary 5.2 (Finite-variable polynomial rings)

Let $k$ be a field and $m$ a nonnegative integer. The polynomial ring $k[x_1,\ldots,x_m]$ admits no infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals indexed by the natural numbers.

**Proof sketch.** A field is Noetherian, and the Hilbert basis theorem implies that adjoining finitely many polynomial variables preserves Noetherianity. Apply Theorem 5.1. $\square$

### Corollary 5.3 (Discrete valuation rings)

A discrete valuation ring admits no infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals.

**Proof sketch.** A discrete valuation ring is a principal ideal domain and hence Noetherian. Apply Theorem 5.1. Its familiar powers of the maximal ideal form a descending, not ascending, filtration. $\square$

### Remark 5.4 (Dimension is a different measurement)

For a field $k$, the Krull dimension of $k[x_1,\ldots,x_m]$ is $m$. One chain witnessing the lower bound is

$$
(0)\subsetneq(x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq(x_1,\ldots,x_m),
$$

with suitable interpretation at $m=0$. This is a finite chain of prime ideals. It neither produces nor measures an infinite ascending chain of arbitrary ideals. Therefore the number $m$ cannot be interpreted as the length of an infinite “Escher staircase.”

## 6. Algorithms and numerical illustrations

The theorems are exact and infinite, but finite computations can expose their mechanisms.

### 6.1 Binary valuation

For a nonzero integer $x$, define its binary valuation by

$$
\nu_2(x)=\max\{r\ge0:2^r\mid x\}.
$$

Set $\nu_2(0)=\infty$. For a finite list of integer values $v_1,\ldots,v_s$, every value is divisible by $2^n$ exactly when

$$
\min_i\nu_2(v_i)\ge n.
$$

An algorithm repeatedly divides $|x|$ by $2$ while it is even. Its running time is $O(\log |x|)$ arithmetic iterations for nonzero $x$. For a list, the total is $O(\sum_i\log(1+|v_i|))$.

### 6.2 Testing a sampled filtration level

Given an integer-valued expression and a finite sample $S\subset\mathbb Z$, compute all values and check their remainders modulo $2^n$. This can disprove membership in $J_n$: one failed sample is a genuine certificate. It cannot generally prove global membership because the definition quantifies over every integer. For special explicitly represented polynomials, stronger finite criteria may be available, but no such criterion is required for the present theorems.

### 6.3 Generating strictness certificates

At any requested level $n$, output the constant polynomial $p_n(X)=2^n$. Direct evaluation shows

$$
p_n(z)\equiv0\pmod{2^n}
\quad\text{and}\quad
p_n(z)\not\equiv0\pmod{2^{n+1}}
$$

for every integer $z$. Computing $2^n$ by exponentiation by squaring uses $O(\log n)$ multiplications, while the output integer has $n+1$ binary digits. This algorithm does not search for a witness; it constructs the canonical one.

### 6.4 Example: an integer-valued polynomial

Consider

$$
b(X)=\frac{X(X-1)}2.
$$

Its values at $0,1,2,3,4,5$ are $0,0,1,3,6,10$. Since the value at $2$ is odd, $b\notin J_1$. Multiplying by $8$ gives values all divisible by $8$, so $8b\in J_3$. Because $b(2)=1$, the value $(8b)(2)=8$ is not divisible by $16$, hence $8b\notin J_4$. Thus nonconstant polynomials can also occupy an exact filtration layer.

## 7. Interpretation and applications

The descending ideals $J_n$ measure uniform divisibility of polynomial values. One may define the finite divisibility depth of a nonzero $p$ by

$$
\delta_2(p)=\sup\{n:p\in J_n\}.
$$

The Zero-Intersection Theorem ensures that $\delta_2(p)$ is finite for every nonzero $p$, while $\delta_2(0)=\infty$. This is a valuation-like statistic on values, although it should not automatically be identified with a valuation on the whole ring without checking the relevant axioms.

The construction also illustrates a general method. Evaluation maps translate arithmetic conditions on values into ideals by inverse image. Intersecting over all evaluation points enforces a uniform condition. Constant elements often provide sharp witnesses between adjacent levels. Finally, a rigidity theorem—here, the finite-root property of polynomials—turns pointwise separation into algebraic separation.

Applications of this pattern include fixed divisors of polynomials, congruence-preserving maps, approximation by powers of an ideal, and local or adic topologies. A separated filtration can define a Hausdorff topology when its levels form a neighborhood basis at zero. The present results establish the essential separation property, though a full topological development would require additional definitions and continuity statements.

The comparison with Noetherianity is equally instructive. Descending chains need not stabilize in a Noetherian ring: the ideals $(2^n)$ in $\mathbb Z$ already form an infinite strict descending chain. Noetherianity controls ascent, not descent. Artinianity is the corresponding finiteness condition for descending chains. Thus the nonstabilization of $J_n$ does not by itself prove that $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$ is non-Noetherian.

## 8. Discussion of the corrected “staircase” metaphor

An actual ascending loop would require incompatible order behavior. If $I_0\subsetneq I_1\subsetneq\cdots$, then each ideal is a subset of the next. The intersection $\bigcap_n I_n$ is simply $I_0$ when $I_0$ is included among the indices, because $I_0$ is already contained in every later ideal. But the elements of this intersection are elements of the ring, not ideals. Saying that zero lies there says only what is true for all ideals.

For a descending chain $J_0\supsetneq J_1\supsetneq\cdots$, the intersection is a meaningful limiting core. Theorem 4.7 says that the core is exactly zero. This is not a loop; it is convergence in the order of ideals.

The distinction also blocks premature invariant-building. Any proposed height must specify what chains are allowed, whether they are prime, whether direction is ascending or descending, whether ordinal lengths are permitted, and how stabilization is treated. Krull dimension, valuative dimension, and chain conditions answer different questions. A new invariant should be compared with these only after a coherent definition and basic invariance properties are established.

## 9. Boundary cases and logical scope

At level $n=0$, the condition is divisibility by $1$, so $D_0$ is the whole function ring and $J_0$ is all of $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$. The first proper restriction appears at $n=1$. This confirms that the chain begins at the largest possible ideal and then narrows. Negative inputs cause no exceptional behavior: integer divisibility and polynomial evaluation are compatible with signs, and the absolute-value argument in Lemma 3.5 treats positive and negative integers uniformly.

The proofs use only two special properties of the base $2$: its powers form a divisibility chain, and their absolute values are unbounded. The same integer argument works for any integer $a$ with $|a|>1$. For $a=1$ or $a=-1$, every power is a unit and the filtration is constant, while $a=0$ requires a separate convention because $0^0$ and later zero powers create a degenerate family. These cases show why hypotheses matter in a general principal-filtration theorem.

It is also important to delimit what has not been inferred. The strict descending filtration does not imply failure of Noetherianity, because Noetherian rings may possess infinite descending chains. The zero-intersection theorem does not compute Krull dimension. The Noetherian obstruction says nothing against finite strict ascending chains, which occur abundantly. Finally, finite numerical sampling illustrates but does not replace the universal quantifier over all integer inputs. Each conclusion rests on its own mechanism: divisibility for containment, explicit constants for strictness, unbounded powers for pointwise vanishing, root finiteness for polynomial identity, and the ascending-chain condition for the obstruction.

## 10. Future work

Several directions arise naturally.

First, replace $2\in\mathbb Z$ by an element $a$ of an integral domain and determine hypotheses under which $\bigcap_n(a^n)=0$. This leads toward separated adic filtrations and intersection theorems.

Second, study genuine ascent in non-Noetherian rings. In the polynomial ring with countably many variables, the chain

$$
(x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq\cdots
$$

is strict because $x_{n+1}$ belongs to the next ideal but not the preceding one. It witnesses failure of the ascending-chain condition without suggesting any loop.

Third, sharpen the standard equivalence between Noetherianity and stabilization of monotone natural-number-indexed ideal chains. From a nonstabilizing ascending chain one can select a strictly increasing subsequence, making the obstruction exact.

Fourth, investigate the ring of all algebraic integers. It is non-Noetherian and therefore supports genuine failures of finite generation, but assigning it an “Escher height” requires a precise invariant. Comparisons with Krull dimension, valuative dimension, and prime-chain lengths should precede numerical claims.

Fifth, develop the structure of $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$ through its binomial basis and fixed divisors. This may yield effective membership tests for the ideals $J_n$ and connect uniform divisibility to the arithmetic of polynomial coefficients.

## 11. Conclusion

The powers-of-two construction does not produce an infinite ascending staircase. It produces something precise and robust: in both the integer-valued function ring and $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$, the ideals defined by uniform divisibility by $2^n$ strictly descend, never stabilize, and intersect only in zero. Constant powers of two witness strictness, infinite divisibility forces integer values to vanish, and polynomial rigidity completes the argument in $\operatorname{Int}(\mathbb Z)$.

In the opposite direction, Noetherianity prohibits infinite strict ascent. Finite-variable polynomial rings over fields and discrete valuation rings therefore cannot support the proposed ascending phenomenon. Krull dimension remains a theory of prime-ideal chains and should not be conflated with arbitrary ideal-chain length.

The corrected architecture is not paradoxical. It is a separated filtration: an infinite descent whose narrowing levels measure divisibility ever more finely, until only zero survives them all.

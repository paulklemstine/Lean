## Abstract

The Berggren tree is the free ternary tree on the primitive Pythagorean triples, rooted at $(3,4,5)$ and generated by three unimodular linear maps. We study a walk on this tree in which a target integer $N$ defines an arithmetic *energy* $E_N(a,b,c) = c \bmod N$, and call a node **resonant** when the energy vanishes, i.e. $N \mid c$. We prove four groups of results.

**(1) Structure and completeness.** The three branch maps preserve primitivity; every branch increases the hypotenuse by at least $8$ and by at most a factor of $7$; the walk is a bijection between finite words in a three-letter alphabet and the primitive Pythagorean triples with odd first leg, so the depth-$n$ layer consists of exactly $3^n$ distinct triples.

**(2) Exact resonance dichotomy and collapse.** For odd $N > 1$, a resonant node exists if and only if $-1$ is a quadratic residue modulo $N$; in particular no node at all is resonant when some prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides $N$. Any two resonant nodes satisfy the congruence of squares $(a_1a_2)^2 \equiv (b_1b_2)^2 \pmod N$, so a non-degenerate resonant pair yields the proper nontrivial divisor $\gcd(a_1a_2-b_1b_2, N)$. We prove that this is not conditional: **every** odd $N$ with all prime factors $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ that is not a prime power carries two distinct nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$ whose interference gcd is an exact nontrivial proper divisor of $N$.

**(3) Multiplicity as an arithmetic invariant.** The number $r(N)$ of nodes with hypotenuse exactly $N$ equals $1$ for every prime power $p^k$ with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and exactly $2$ for a semiprime $pq$ with distinct $p,q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Consequently unique resonance characterises prime powers exactly, and resonance multiplicity is a primality certificate.

**(4) A kinematic barrier.** Since branch growth is bounded by the factor $7$ while layer width grows as $3^n$, and $9>7$, any resonance for $N$ at depth $n$ forces $3^n \ge \sqrt{N/5}$: the first resonant layer already has dimension $\Omega(\sqrt N)$, exponential in $\log N$. The barrier is *coin-independent*: for an arbitrary complex amplitude assignment $\psi$ on depth-$n$ histories, the coherent resonance amplitude vanishes identically below the critical depth, and always obeys $|\mathcal A(\psi)|^2 \le |R|\,\|\psi\|^2$ with equality **if and only if** $\psi$ is a scalar multiple of the indicator of the resonance set. Hence the optimal state encodes the answer, and no engineering of the dynamics can remove the obstruction.

The net picture is sharp: on its admissible domain the mechanism is an exact factoring oracle with no heuristic component, and the sole obstruction to efficiency is the geometry of the state space.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, congruence of squares, sums of two squares, integer factorisation, quantum walk, interference bound, rigidity.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

Every factoring algorithm of the modern era is, at its core, a machine for producing congruences of squares: numbers $x, y$ with
$$x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod N, \qquad x \not\equiv \pm y \pmod N,$$
from which $\gcd(x-y,N)$ is a proper divisor. The differences between Fermat's method, Dixon's method, the quadratic sieve and the number field sieve lie entirely in *how* such congruences are manufactured.

Pythagorean triples manufacture them for free. If $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ and $N \mid c$, then $a^2 \equiv -b^2 \pmod N$. Two such triples multiply into a congruence of squares. The question is where to find the triples, and the answer is one of the prettiest structures in elementary number theory: the Berggren ternary tree, in which every primitive Pythagorean triple occurs exactly once.

This suggests a physical picture. Regard the tree as the configuration space of a three-outcome walk. Attach to each node the arithmetic energy $E_N(t) = c \bmod N$. The minima of the energy spectrum are the resonant nodes, and a coherent walker whose amplitude concentrated on those minima would, upon measurement, deliver a pair of resonant nodes and therefore a factor of $N$. The programme of this paper is to determine, exactly and without heuristics, how much of this picture is true.

### 1.2 Results and organisation

Section 2 sets up the tree and proves the structural results: primitivity preservation, two-sided growth, and the completeness bijection. Section 3 introduces resonance and proves the collapse theorem and the exact dichotomy governing which targets resonate. Section 4 proves the universal collapse theorem — the statement that on the admissible domain, every non-prime-power target splits — via the theory of primitive representations as sums of two squares. Section 5 computes resonance multiplicities and derives the primality certificate. Section 6 proves the search barrier, its coin-independent form, and the rigidity of the interference bound. Section 7 gives algorithms and complexity. Section 8 discusses what the results mean, and Section 9 lists open problems.

A running numerical example is the target $N = 65$, which the mechanism factors as $13 \times 5$ using the two nodes $(33,56,65)$ and $(63,16,65)$.

## 2. The Berggren tree as a state space

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Node).** A *node* is an ordered triple $t = (a,b,c)$ of integers. We write $t.a$, $t.b$, $t.c$ for its coordinates.

**Definition 2.2 (Primitive Pythagorean triple).** A node $t$ is a *primitive Pythagorean triple* (PPT) if $a^2+b^2=c^2$, all three coordinates are positive, and $\gcd(a,b)=1$.

**Definition 2.3 (Branch operators).** The three Berggren maps are the linear maps given by the unimodular matrices
$$A = \begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\2&-1&2\\2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B = \begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&2\\2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
C = \begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\-2&1&2\\-2&2&3\end{pmatrix},$$
acting on column vectors $(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}$. We index them by $i \in \{0,1,2\}$ and write $\mathrm{br}_i$.

**Definition 2.4 (Walk).** A *word* is a finite list $w = (i_1,\dots,i_n)$ with $i_j \in \{0,1,2\}$. The walk map is
$$\mathrm{walk}(\,) = (3,4,5), \qquad \mathrm{walk}(i :: w) = \mathrm{br}_i(\mathrm{walk}(w)),$$
i.e. the word is read right to left from the root $(3,4,5)$. The *depth* of $w$ is its length $|w|$.

### 2.2 Invariance and growth

**Proposition 2.5 (Primitivity is preserved).** If $t$ is a PPT then $\mathrm{br}_i(t)$ is a PPT for each $i \in \{0,1,2\}$. Consequently $\mathrm{walk}(w)$ is a PPT for every word $w$.

*Proof sketch.* The Pythagorean relation is preserved by a direct algebraic identity for each matrix: e.g. for $A$,
$$(a-2b+2c)^2 + (2a-b+2c)^2 - (2a-2b+3c)^2 = -(a^2+b^2-c^2).$$
Positivity follows from the elementary PPT estimates below. Coprimality is preserved because each matrix is unimodular: writing the source coordinates as integral linear combinations of the image coordinates, a common prime factor $p$ of the image legs also divides the image hypotenuse (since $p^2 \mid a'^2 + b'^2 = c'^2$), hence divides both source legs, contradicting primitivity of $t$. $\square$

Two elementary estimates for a PPT $t$ carry the growth analysis. Both legs satisfy $a,b \ge 3$ and $c \ge 5$; and the *skewness estimates*
$$c + a - b \ \ge\ 4, \qquad c + b - a \ \ge\ 4$$
hold, proved by checking the boundary cases $a=3$ (forcing $t=(3,4,5)$) and $b=3$ (forcing $t = (4,3,5)$, excluded when the first leg is odd) and otherwise squaring.

**Theorem 2.6 (Two-sided growth law).** For every PPT $t$ and every $i$,
$$t.c + 8 \ \le\ \mathrm{br}_i(t).c \ \le\ 7\, t.c .$$

*Proof sketch.* The three hypotenuse rows are $2a-2b+3c$, $2a+2b+3c$, $-2a+2b+3c$. The lower bounds reduce, via the skewness estimates, to $2(c+a-b) + c \ge c+8$ and its mirror, and to $2a+2b+3c \ge c + 8$ which is immediate. The upper bounds follow from $a<c$ and $b<c$: e.g. $2a-2b+3c \le 2c + 3c = 5c \le 7c$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.7 (Depth window).** For every word $w$,
$$8\,|w| + 5 \ \le\ \mathrm{walk}(w).c \ \le\ 5 \cdot 7^{|w|} .$$
Equivalently, a node of hypotenuse $c$ occurs only at depths $n$ with $\log_7(c/5) \le n \le (c-5)/8$.

**Proposition 2.8 (The slow branch is quadratic).** Iterating the single map $A$ from the root,
$$A^n(3,4,5) = (2n+3,\ 2n^2+6n+4,\ 2n^2+6n+5).$$
So hypotenuse $c$ is reachable at depth $\Theta(\sqrt c)$: the tree is shallow along at least one path.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$, with the three coordinate identities verified by expansion. $\square$

### 2.3 Completeness: the tree is the whole family

**Theorem 2.9 (Berggren bijection).** The map $w \mapsto \mathrm{walk}(w)$ is injective, and its image is exactly the set of PPTs with odd first leg. Consequently every primitive Pythagorean triple, written with the odd leg first, has a unique address in $\{0,1,2\}^{*}$.

*Proof sketch.* Parity: each branch preserves oddness of the first leg, and the root has $a=3$. Surjectivity is a descent. Define candidate parent maps $\mathrm{par}_A, \mathrm{par}_B, \mathrm{par}_C$ by the inverse matrices; each satisfies $\mathrm{br}_X(\mathrm{par}_X(t)) = t$ identically. For a PPT $t \ne (3,4,5)$ with odd first leg, put
$$u = a + 2b - 2c, \qquad v = 2a + b - 2c .$$
One checks (i) $u \ne 0$ always, by parity; (ii) $v = 0$ forces $t = (3,4,5)$; (iii) $u$ and $v$ cannot both be negative for a PPT; and (iv) the sign pattern of $(u,v)$ selects exactly one of $\mathrm{par}_A, \mathrm{par}_B, \mathrm{par}_C$ that returns a PPT with strictly smaller hypotenuse (indeed $\mathrm{par}_X(t).c = -2a-2b+3c < c$). Descent on $c$ terminates at the root, producing a word. Injectivity follows because each $\mathrm{par}_X$ inverts the corresponding $\mathrm{br}_X$ on the nose and the three images are disjoint, so a word can be recovered from its endpoint. $\square$

**Corollary 2.10 (Layer size).** The depth-$n$ layer $\{\mathrm{walk}(w) : |w| = n\}$ consists of exactly $3^n$ distinct nodes.

## 3. Resonance and collapse

### 3.1 The energy spectrum

**Definition 3.1.** For a target $N$ and node $t$, the *energy* is $E_N(t) = t.c \bmod N$. A node is *resonant* for $N$ when $N \mid t.c$; equivalently $E_N(t) = 0$. For $n \in \mathbb N$, the *resonance set* is
$$R_N^{(n)} = \{\, r \in \{0,1,2\}^n : N \mid \mathrm{walk}(r).c \,\},$$
and the *multiplicity* is $r(N) = \#\{ w : \mathrm{walk}(w).c = N \}$, the number of nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$.

Note the distinction: $R_N^{(n)}$ collects depth-$n$ histories whose hypotenuse is a *multiple* of $N$; $r(N)$ counts nodes whose hypotenuse *equals* $N$.

### 3.2 The collapse theorem

**Lemma 3.2 (Congruence of squares).** Let $N > 1$ and $x,y \in \mathbb Z$ with $N \mid x^2-y^2$, $N \nmid x-y$ and $N \nmid x+y$. Then $g = \gcd(x-y,N)$ satisfies $1 < g < N$ and $g \mid N$.

*Proof.* $g \mid N$ by definition, and $g > 0$ since $N \ne 0$. If $g=1$ then $x-y$ is invertible modulo $N$, and $N \mid (x-y)(x+y)$ gives $N \mid x+y$, a contradiction. If $g = N$ then $N \mid x-y$, again a contradiction; and $g > N$ is impossible for a positive divisor of $N>0$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.3 (Resonant pair identity).** If $a_i^2+b_i^2 = c_i^2$ for $i=1,2$ and $N \mid c_1$, $N \mid c_2$, then
$$(a_1a_2)^2 - (b_1b_2)^2 = a_2^2c_1^2 - b_1^2c_2^2,$$
and therefore $N \mid (a_1a_2)^2 - (b_1b_2)^2$; in fact $N^2$ divides the right-hand side.

*Proof.* Substitute $a_1^2 = c_1^2 - b_1^2$ and $b_2^2 = c_2^2 - a_2^2$ into the left-hand side and expand. Divisibility follows since $N \mid c_1, c_2$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Resonance collapse).** Let $N>1$ and let $t_1, t_2$ be PPTs with $N \mid t_1.c$ and $N \mid t_2.c$. If
$$N \nmid a_1a_2 - b_1b_2 \quad\text{and}\quad N \nmid a_1a_2 + b_1b_2,$$
then $\gcd(a_1a_2-b_1b_2, N)$ is a proper nontrivial divisor of $N$. In particular $N$ is composite.

*Proof.* Combine Lemmas 3.2 and 3.3 with $x = a_1a_2$, $y = b_1b_2$. Compositeness: a prime $N$ has no divisor strictly between $1$ and $N$. $\square$

We call a pair satisfying the two non-divisibility hypotheses *non-degenerate*, and the quantity $\gcd(a_1a_2-b_1b_2,N)$ the *interference gcd* of the pair.

**Example 3.5 ($N = 65$).** The words $w_1 = (0,2)$ and $w_2 = (2,2,2)$ give the nodes $(33,56,65)$ and $(63,16,65)$. Then $a_1a_2 = 2079$, $b_1b_2 = 896$, and $2079^2 - 896^2 = 1183 \cdot 2975 = 3\,519\,425 = 65 \cdot 54\,145$, so $2079^2 \equiv 896^2 \pmod{65}$. Finally
$$\gcd(2079-896,\;65) = \gcd(1183, 65) = 13 .$$
Hence $65 = 13 \cdot 5$.

### 3.3 Which targets resonate

**Theorem 3.6 (Resonance dichotomy).** Let $N > 1$ be odd. Then there exists a word $w$ with $N \mid \mathrm{walk}(w).c$ **iff** $-1$ is a square in $\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$. Moreover, when a resonance exists it can be found with hypotenuse at most $(N-1)^2+1$.

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) Let $t$ be a resonant PPT. Then $a^2 + b^2 \equiv 0 \pmod N$. Since $\gcd(a,b)=1$ and $N \mid a^2+b^2$, any prime factor of $N$ dividing $b$ would divide $a$ too; so $b$ is invertible modulo $N$ and $(ab^{-1})^2 \equiv -1$.

($\Leftarrow$) Let $s^2 \equiv -1 \pmod N$. As $N$ is odd we may replace $s$ by $s+N$ if necessary and assume $s$ even, hence $s$ and $s^2 \pm 1$ have opposite parities and $\gcd(s^2-1, 2s) = 1$. The triple
$$\bigl(s^2-1,\; 2s,\; s^2+1\bigr)$$
is a PPT with odd first leg and $N \mid s^2+1$. By Theorem 2.9 it is a node of the tree. Choosing $0 \le s \le N-1$ gives $s^2+1 \le (N-1)^2+1$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.7 (Invisible targets).** If a prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ divides $N$, no node of the tree is resonant for $N$: the resonance set is empty at every depth.

*Proof.* $-1$ is a non-residue modulo such $p$, hence a non-residue modulo $N$. Directly: $N \mid c$ implies $p \mid a^2+b^2$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$, forcing $-1$ to be a square modulo $p$. $\square$

So the *admissible domain* of the mechanism is
$$\mathcal D = \{\,N > 1 : N \text{ odd, every prime factor of } N \text{ is} \equiv 1 \bmod 4 \,\}$$
(together with the trivially handled even part; we restrict to odd $N$ throughout).

### 3.4 Euler's two-representation mechanism, in walk language

If $N = u_1^2+v_1^2 = u_2^2+v_2^2$ are two essentially different primitive representations, then the nodes $\rho(u_i,v_i) = (u_i^2-v_i^2,\ 2u_iv_i,\ u_i^2+v_i^2)$ are two distinct nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$, and Theorem 3.4 applies to them. This is Euler's classical factorisation method, recovered as the depth-graded interference of two tree addresses. Our contribution in the next section is to show that *the required pair always exists* on $\mathcal D$ outside the prime powers, including in non-squarefree cases.

## 4. Universal collapse

### 4.1 Primitive representations

**Definition 4.1.** A *primitive representation* of $M$ is a pair $(A,B)$ with $A,B > 0$, $\gcd(A,B)=1$ and $A^2+B^2 = M$.

**Lemma 4.2 (Brahmagupta composition).** If $x^2+y^2 = m$ and $u^2+v^2 = n$, then
$$(xu - yv)^2 + (xv + yu)^2 = mn, \qquad (xu + yv)^2 + (xv - yu)^2 = mn .$$

**Lemma 4.3 (Composition preserves primitivity across coprime parts).** If $(x,y)$ is a primitive representation of an odd $m$, $(u,v)$ a primitive representation of an odd $n$, and $\gcd(m,n)=1$, then both compositions of Lemma 4.2 are primitive representations of $mn$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose a prime $r$ divides both coordinates of a composition. Then $r^2 \mid mn$, and $r$ divides the "quartic" combinations obtained from the composed pair; the standard identities show $r$ then divides both $x,y$ or both $u,v$ (using $\gcd(m,n)=1$ to localise $r$ in one factor), contradicting primitivity. Oddness excludes $r=2$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.4 (Prime powers are primitively represented).** Let $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ be prime and $k \ge 1$. Then $p^k$ admits a primitive representation.

*Proof sketch.* Induct on $k$. Fermat's two-square theorem gives $p = x^2+y^2$ with $\gcd(x,y)=1$, the case $k=1$. Given a primitive representation $(A,B)$ of $p^{k}$, the two Brahmagupta compositions with $(x,y)$ give two representations of $p^{k+1}$, namely
$$(xA - yB,\ xB + yA) \quad\text{and}\quad (xA + yB,\ xB - yA).$$
Both cannot fail to be primitive: if a prime $r$ divides both coordinates of both compositions, then $r$ divides their sums and differences $2xA$, $2yB$, $2xB$, $2yA$, and elementary case analysis with $r \ne 2$ and $\gcd(A,B) = \gcd(x,y)=1$ forces $r \mid p$ and then $p$ divides both $A$ and $B$ — impossible. So exactly one composition remains primitive (the other is the one divisible by $p$). Adjusting signs makes both coordinates positive. $\square$

**Theorem 4.5 (Every admissible modulus is primitively represented).** If $N > 1$ is odd and every prime factor of $N$ is $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, then $N$ admits a primitive representation.

*Proof.* Write $N = \prod_j p_j^{k_j}$. By Theorem 4.4 each $p_j^{k_j}$ has a primitive representation; the factors are pairwise coprime and odd, so Lemma 4.3 composes them into a primitive representation of $N$ by induction on the number of prime factors. $\square$

### 4.2 Splitting a coprime factorisation

**Theorem 4.6 (Coprime-split collapse).** Let $N = mn$ with $m,n > 1$ odd and coprime, and let $(x,y)$, $(u,v)$ be primitive representations of $m$ and $n$ respectively. Let $t_1$, $t_2$ be the two nodes obtained from the two Brahmagupta compositions
$$P = (xu-yv,\; xv+yu), \qquad Q = (xu+yv,\; xv-yu)$$
via $\rho(A,B) = (A^2-B^2,\ 2AB,\ A^2+B^2)$ (adjusting signs and order so that the first leg is odd and positive). Then $t_1 \ne t_2$, both have hypotenuse exactly $N$, and their interference gcd is an exact nontrivial proper divisor of $N$; specifically it equals one of $m$, $n$ up to the exchange of roles.

*Proof sketch.* Both compositions are primitive representations of $N$ by Lemma 4.3, so both give PPTs of hypotenuse $N$; the corresponding nodes are distinct because the coordinate pairs $P$ and $Q$ are distinct up to sign and order (they differ by $2yv$ and $2yu$ in the two slots, and $m,n>1$ prevents degeneracy). For the gcd, write the interference term $a_1a_2 - b_1b_2$ in the coordinates of $P$ and $Q$; the classical *sign-interference identity* expresses it as a product in which the $m$-part and the $n$-part separate:
$$a_1a_2 - b_1b_2 = \pm\, (\text{a multiple of } m) \cdot (\text{a unit mod } n) ,$$
and two gcd-evaluation lemmas (using $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and the coprimality of the quartic combinations with $m$ and with $n$) identify $\gcd(a_1a_2-b_1b_2, N)$ *exactly* as $m$ (resp. $n$). Both are $>1$ and $<N$. $\square$

The point of the exact evaluation is that the collapse never returns the useless answers $1$ or $N$: the pair produced by the two sign choices is automatically non-degenerate.

### 4.3 The headline theorem

**Theorem 4.7 (Universal resonance collapse).** Let $N$ be odd with every prime factor $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$, and suppose $N$ has two distinct prime divisors $p \ne q$. Then there exist distinct words $w_1 \ne w_2$ with
$$\mathrm{walk}(w_1).c = \mathrm{walk}(w_2).c = N$$
such that, writing $t_i = \mathrm{walk}(w_i)$,
$$1 \;<\; \gcd(a_1a_2 - b_1b_2,\ N) \;<\; N, \qquad \gcd(a_1a_2 - b_1b_2,\ N) \ \big|\ N .$$

*Proof.* Let $k = v_p(N)$ and split $N = m \cdot n$ with $m = p^k$ and $n = N/p^k$. Then $m,n > 1$ (the latter because $q \mid n$), they are coprime and odd, and each has all prime factors $\equiv 1 \bmod 4$. By Theorems 4.4 and 4.5 both have primitive representations. Apply Theorem 4.6 to obtain the two nodes, and Theorem 2.9 to convert them into words. $\square$

**Corollary 4.8 (Explicit factorisation).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.7 there are $d,e$ with $1 < d,e$, $de = N$, and $d = \gcd(a_1a_2 - b_1b_2, N)$.

**Corollary 4.9 (Depth window of the collapsing pair).** The two words satisfy
$$\log_7(N/5) \ \le\ |w_i| \ \le\ (N-5)/8 \qquad (i = 1,2),$$
by Corollary 2.7. The lower bound is the kinematic barrier of Section 6; the upper bound guarantees that the pair sits at explicitly bounded, finite depth.

**Worked instances.** $N = 325 = 5^2\cdot 13$ (non-squarefree; the earlier semiprime and three-prime arguments do not apply) collapses via $(253,204,325)$ and $(323,36,325)$ with interference gcd $25$. $N = 1105 = 5 \cdot 13 \cdot 17$ collapses with interference gcd $221$. $N = 32045 = 5\cdot13\cdot17\cdot29$ has eight nodes of hypotenuse $N$ and collapses with gcd $1105$.

## 5. Resonance multiplicity

Recall $r(N)$ counts nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$. By Theorem 2.9 this equals the number of primitive representations $N = A^2+B^2$ with $A$ odd, $A,B>0$, up to nothing — the correspondence $\rho$ is a bijection onto such nodes.

### 5.1 Primes and prime powers

**Theorem 5.1 (Uniqueness for primes).** For a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ there is exactly one word $w$ with $\mathrm{walk}(w).c = p$. Hence $r(p) = 1$.

*Proof sketch.* Existence is Fermat plus Theorem 2.9. Uniqueness is the classical uniqueness of the two-square representation of a prime: if $A^2+B^2 = C^2+D^2 = p$ then $p \mid (AD-BC)(AD+BC)$, and $p \nmid A,B,C,D$; the Brahmagupta identity $(AC \mp BD)^2 + (AD\pm BC)^2 = p^2$ forces one of $AD \mp BC$ to vanish, giving $\{A^2,B^2\} = \{C^2,D^2\}$. Odd-first-leg normalisation removes the remaining freedom. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Uniqueness for prime powers).** For a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and any $k \ge 1$ there is exactly one word $w$ with $\mathrm{walk}(w).c = p^k$. Hence $r(p^k) = 1$, and no interference pair exists for a prime-power target.

*Proof sketch.* Existence is Theorem 4.4. For uniqueness, let $A^2+B^2 = A'^2+B'^2 = p^k$ with both pairs primitive. Since $p$ is odd and the representations are primitive, $p$ divides none of $A,B,A',B'$ (if $p \mid A$ then $p \mid B^2$, hence $p \mid B$, contradiction). From
$$(AB'-A'B)(AB'+A'B) = A^2B'^2 - A'^2B^2 = p^k(B'^2 - B^2)$$
and the fact that $p$ cannot divide both left factors (their sum is $2AB'$ and their difference $2A'B$, neither divisible by $p$), the whole of $p^k$ divides one of them. The Brahmagupta identity
$$(AA' \mp BB')^2 + (AB' \pm A'B)^2 = p^{2k}$$
then bounds that factor by $p^k$ in absolute value, so it is $0$ or $\pm p^k$. In the first case $AB' = A'B$, and coprimality forces $A = A'$, $B = B'$. In the second case the companion term vanishes, $AA' = \pm BB'$, and coprimality forces $\{A,B\} = \{B',A'\}$; the odd-first-leg normalisation identifies the two nodes. $\square$

### 5.2 Semiprimes

**Theorem 5.3 (Exactly two for a semiprime).** Let $p \ne q$ be primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Then there are exactly two words with hypotenuse $pq$: $r(pq) = 2$.

*Proof sketch.* Existence of two: the two Brahmagupta compositions of the representations of $p$ and $q$, which are primitive by Lemma 4.3 and distinct. At most two: given three primitive representations of $pq$, a descent argument reduces any two of them modulo $p$ — the *representation descent* lemma states that if $x^2+y^2 = A^2+B^2 = pq$ then $p$ divides one of $xB \pm yA$, and dividing through returns representations of $q$, where uniqueness (Theorem 5.1) applies. Sorting the resulting cases by which of the two sign patterns occurs shows that any third representation coincides with one of the first two. $\square$

**Corollary 5.4 (Multiplicity is a primality certificate).** For distinct primes $p,q \equiv 1 \bmod 4$: the target $p$ admits exactly one resonant word, while $pq$ admits more than one. Thus $r(N) = 1$ and $r(N) \ge 2$ distinguish the two cases.

### 5.3 The dichotomy

**Theorem 5.5 (Prime-power dichotomy).** Let $N > 1$ be odd with all prime factors $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (by Theorem 3.6 exactly the odd targets on which the walk resonates). Then exactly one of the following holds:

1. $N = p^k$ is a prime power, the resonant word is *unique*, and no interference pair exists; or
2. $N$ has two distinct prime divisors, and some resonant pair collapses onto an exact nontrivial proper divisor of $N$.

*Proof.* Every $N>1$ either has two distinct prime factors or is a prime power. Case 2 is Theorem 4.7; case 1 is Theorem 5.2. $\square$

**Theorem 5.6 (Unique resonance characterises prime powers).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 5.5,
$$\bigl(\exists!\, w:\ \mathrm{walk}(w).c = N\bigr) \iff \bigl(\exists\, p, k:\ p \text{ prime},\ k\ge1,\ N = p^k\bigr).$$

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 5.5: if $N$ is not a prime power, Theorem 4.7 supplies two distinct words, contradicting uniqueness; conversely Theorem 5.2. $\square$

**Remark 5.7 (The general multiplicity formula).** Numerically $r(N) = 2^{\omega(N)-1}$, where $\omega$ counts distinct prime factors: $r(5) = r(25) = r(125) = 1$, $r(65) = r(325) = r(4225) = 2$, $r(1105) = 4$, $r(32045)=8$. This matches the classical count of primitive representations as a sum of two squares. The cases $\omega = 1$ (all $k$) and $\omega = 2$ (squarefree) are established above; the general statement is a natural extension consistent with all computed data.

## 6. The barrier

### 6.1 The kinematic bound

**Theorem 6.1 (Depth lower bound).** If $N \mid \mathrm{walk}(w).c$ then $N \le 5\cdot7^{|w|}$; equivalently $|w| \ge \log_7(N/5)$.

*Proof.* A positive multiple of $N$ is at least $N$, and $\mathrm{walk}(w).c \le 5\cdot7^{|w|}$ by Corollary 2.7. $\square$

**Theorem 6.2 (Search-space lower bound).** If $N \mid \mathrm{walk}(w).c$ then $N \le 5\,(3^{|w|})^2$. Hence if the depth-$n$ resonance set $R_N^{(n)}$ is nonempty,
$$3^{n} \;\ge\; \sqrt{N/5}.$$

*Proof.* $7^n \le 9^n = (3^n)^2$, since $7 < 9$. Combine with Theorem 6.1 and take square roots. $\square$

The interpretation is stark. The dimension of the depth-$n$ state space is exactly $3^n$ (Corollary 2.10). At the *first* depth where a resonance for $N$ can occur, that dimension is already $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ — exponential in the bit length of $N$.

**Corollary 6.3 (Grover-type reading).** $\sqrt{3^n} \ge (N/5)^{1/4}$, so amplitude amplification over a single layer costs $\Omega(N^{1/4})$: still exponential in $\log N$.

### 6.2 Coin-independence

Let $\psi : \{0,1,2\}^n \to \mathbb C$ be an *arbitrary* amplitude assignment — no unitarity, no product structure, no locality is assumed, so this covers uniform, biased, position-dependent, adaptive and entangling coins alike. Define
$$\mathcal A_N(\psi) = \sum_{r \in R_N^{(n)}} \psi_r, \qquad \|\psi\|^2 = \sum_{r} |\psi_r|^2 .$$

**Theorem 6.4 (Vanishing below the critical depth).** If $5\cdot 7^n < N$ then $R_N^{(n)} = \emptyset$ and hence $\mathcal A_N(\psi) = 0$ for **every** state $\psi$.

*Proof.* Theorem 6.1 forbids resonance at depth $n$; an empty sum is zero. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5 (Interference bound).** For every $n$, every $N$ and every state $\psi$,
$$|\mathcal A_N(\psi)|^2 \ \le\ |R_N^{(n)}| \cdot \|\psi\|^2 .$$
In particular, for a normalised state the interference gain is at most the resonance multiplicity $|R_N^{(n)}|$ — a purely arithmetic quantity.

*Proof.* $|\mathcal A_N(\psi)| \le \sum_{r \in R}|\psi_r|$ by the triangle inequality; then $\bigl(\sum_{r\in R}|\psi_r|\bigr)^2 \le |R|\sum_{r\in R}|\psi_r|^2$ by Cauchy–Schwarz; finally $\sum_{r\in R}|\psi_r|^2 \le \|\psi\|^2$. $\square$

**Remark 6.6.** The uniform ("Hadamard-like") coin assigns amplitude $3^{-n/2}$ to each branch; its coherent resonance amplitude squared is $|R|^2 3^{-n}$, which equals $|R|$ times the classical (incoherent) resonance probability $|R| 3^{-n}$. So the uniform coin's interference gain is exactly the multiplicity, and it satisfies Theorem 6.5 as the instance $|R|^2 3^{-n} \le |R|$.

### 6.3 Rigidity of the optimum

**Definition 6.7.** The *resonance-indicator state* with amplitude $c \in \mathbb C$ is
$$\iota_c(r) = \begin{cases} c, & r \in R_N^{(n)},\\ 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

**Proposition 6.8.** $\mathcal A_N(\iota_c) = |R|\,c$ and $\|\iota_c\|^2 = |R|\,|c|^2$, so $|\mathcal A_N(\iota_c)|^2 = |R| \cdot \|\iota_c\|^2$: the bound of Theorem 6.5 is attained.

**Theorem 6.9 (Rigidity).** Assume $R = R_N^{(n)} \ne \emptyset$. For a state $\psi$,
$$|\mathcal A_N(\psi)|^2 = |R| \cdot \|\psi\|^2 \iff \exists c \in \mathbb C: \ \psi = \iota_c .$$

*Proof sketch.* The direction $\Leftarrow$ is Proposition 6.8. For $\Rightarrow$, put $c = \mathcal A_N(\psi)/|R|$ and use the *variance identity*, valid for any finite set $S$, any $f : S \to \mathbb C$ and any $c$:
$$\sum_{r\in S}|f(r)-c|^2 = \sum_{r\in S}|f(r)|^2 - 2\,\mathrm{Re}\Bigl(\bar c \sum_{r\in S} f(r)\Bigr) + |S|\,|c|^2 .$$
Taking $S=R$, $f = \psi|_R$ and this $c$, the middle and last terms combine to $-|\mathcal A_N(\psi)|^2/|R|$, so
$$\sum_{r \in R}|\psi_r - c|^2 = \sum_{r\in R}|\psi_r|^2 - \frac{|\mathcal A_N(\psi)|^2}{|R|} .$$
Equality in Theorem 6.5 forces $|\mathcal A_N(\psi)|^2/|R| = \|\psi\|^2 \ge \sum_{r\in R}|\psi_r|^2$, so the left-hand side is $\le 0$, hence $0$: $\psi$ is constant $= c$ on $R$. The same inequality is tight only if $\sum_{r \notin R}|\psi_r|^2 = 0$, i.e. $\psi$ vanishes off $R$. $\square$

**Interpretation.** The unique optimal state (up to a global amplitude) is the normalised indicator of the resonance set. Preparing it presupposes knowledge of $R$ — i.e. of the answer to the arithmetic question. The interference mechanism is therefore not an algorithm: its optimum is a description of the solution, not a route to it. Together with Theorem 6.4 this shows the obstruction is *kinematic* — a property of the support geometry of the walk — rather than *dynamical*.

## 7. Algorithms and complexity

### 7.1 Address decoding (descent)

Given a PPT with odd first leg, its address is recovered by iterating the parent map from Theorem 2.9. Each step reduces the hypotenuse from $c$ to $-2a-2b+3c < c$, and in fact at least by $8$, so the loop runs in at most $(c-5)/8$ iterations; in practice the hypotenuse shrinks geometrically and the number of steps is $O(\log c)$ for typical inputs, $O(\sqrt c)$ in the worst case (the slow branch). Each step is $O(1)$ arithmetic operations on integers of size $O(\log c)$.

### 7.2 Resonance enumeration

To list all nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$ one may either
(a) traverse the tree, pruning any node with $c > N$ — correct because the hypotenuse strictly increases along every branch, but with cost proportional to the number of nodes of hypotenuse $\le N$, which is $\Theta(N)$; or
(b) enumerate primitive representations $N = A^2+B^2$ directly ($O(\sqrt N)$ by trial, or polynomial time given the factorisation of $N$ via Cornacchia's algorithm) and map each through $\rho$.

Method (b) makes explicit the circularity that Theorem 5.5 already indicates: computing the resonant addresses efficiently requires the factorisation of $N$.

### 7.3 Collapse

Given two distinct resonant nodes, the collapse is a single $\gcd$: $O(\log^2 N)$ bit operations. This step is genuinely cheap; all cost is in finding the pair.

### 7.4 Complexity summary

| Task | Cost |
|---|---|
| Apply one branch | $O(\log N)$ bit ops |
| Decode an address | $O(\log c)$ typical, $O(\sqrt c)$ worst case, steps |
| Verify a node is resonant | $O(\log^2 N)$ |
| Collapse a given pair | $O(\log^2 N)$ |
| Find a resonant pair by tree search | $\Omega(\sqrt{N})$ layer dimension (Theorem 6.2) |
| Find a resonant pair via representations | $O(\sqrt N)$ elementary, or poly given the factorisation |

The table is the whole story in miniature: everything *local* is polynomial; the *search* is not, and Theorem 6.2 says so unconditionally.

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 What is established

Three claims are established without heuristics, conjecture or restriction beyond those stated.

**The arithmetic is exact and universal on its domain.** For every odd $N$ with all prime factors $\equiv 1 \bmod 4$ that is not a prime power, the tree contains a specific pair of nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$ whose interference gcd is an exact nontrivial proper divisor. There is no "with high probability", no sieving, no smoothness assumption. Conversely, prime powers provably refuse to split, because they carry exactly one resonant node and interference requires two. The mechanism therefore has a perfect built-in primality test on its domain: multiplicity $1$ certifies a prime power.

**The admissible domain is exactly characterised.** Resonance exists iff $-1$ is a quadratic residue modulo $N$. A single prime factor $\equiv 3 \bmod 4$ makes the target invisible to the entire infinite tree. This is a genuine restriction on the mechanism, not an artefact of the analysis, but it is also classical: it is the constraint on hypotenuses of primitive triples.

**The efficiency claim is refuted, and the refutation is structural.** The two-sided growth law gives competing exponential rates: value grows by at most $7$ per step, width by exactly $3$; because $9 > 7$, the first resonant layer has $\ge \sqrt{N/5}$ branches. This is coin-independent in the strongest sense: below the critical depth every state has exactly zero resonance amplitude, and at any depth the interference gain of every state is at most the arithmetic multiplicity $|R|$. The rigidity theorem completes the diagnosis: the unique optimal state is the normalised indicator of the resonance set, so achieving the optimum requires already knowing the resonance set.

### 8.2 Relation to classical factorisation

Theorem 4.6 is Euler's two-representation factorisation, restated with two upgrades: an *exact* evaluation of the resulting gcd (rather than "a nontrivial factor with luck"), and an unconditional existence statement for the required pair of representations, including in non-squarefree cases (Theorems 4.4, 4.5). The tree adds an indexing: each representation acquires a canonical finite address, and the collapse becomes an interference between two addresses. This is a genuine structural gain for exposition and for the physical analogy, but it does not change the underlying computational difficulty, which is the difficulty of producing two essentially different representations of $N$ as a sum of two squares — a problem equivalent in difficulty to factoring $N$ on this domain.

### 8.3 The shape of the obstruction

It is worth isolating the general lesson. Many proposals of the form "encode the arithmetic of $N$ in a physical state space and let interference find the answer" fail for a common reason, and the failure mode here is a clean instance of it. The state space *does* encode the arithmetic exactly. The interference *does* extract the answer once the right subspace is populated. What fails is that the target subspace is exponentially small relative to the smallest layer that contains it, and the optimal way to populate it is the indicator state of the target itself. Both facts are provable from the geometry alone, with no reference to the dynamics — which is precisely what makes the obstruction robust against coin engineering.

### 8.4 Positive readings

Two consequences remain useful independent of the factoring question.

*A structural certificate of primality.* Among admissible moduli, "the resonant node is unique" is equivalent to "$N$ is a prime power" (Theorem 5.6). This is a clean characterisation with a natural geometric statement.

*A worked model of arithmetic-geometric barriers.* The proof that no coin helps is short, complete, and applies to a state space that is genuinely arithmetically meaningful. It provides a template for evaluating similar proposals: compute the growth rate of values along branches, compare with the branching factor, and check whether the optimum-attaining state is knowledge-free.

## 9. Future directions

The following problems are open, in decreasing order of concreteness.

1. **General multiplicity formula.** Prove $r(N) = 2^{\omega(N)-1}$ for all admissible $N$, extending the cases $\omega = 1$ (prime powers) and $\omega = 2$ (squarefree semiprimes) proved here. The expected route is a composition/uniqueness induction along the factorisation, with the prime-power uniqueness theorem as the base case.

2. **Exact interference gcd for arbitrary splittings.** Theorem 4.6 evaluates the interference gcd exactly for a chosen coprime splitting. Characterise the *set* of divisors obtainable as interference gcds over all resonant pairs, and determine when the pair chosen returns a prime factor rather than a composite one.

3. **Depth statistics.** Determine the distribution of the address length of the resonant nodes of hypotenuse exactly $N$. Empirically the two nodes of a semiprime sit at very different depths (for $N = 325$: depths $5$ and $8$; for $N = 32045$: depths $7$ and $45$). A sharp average-case depth law would quantify the "shallow-branch" phenomenon.

4. **Beyond a single layer.** The barrier of Theorem 6.2 bounds a single-layer search. Analyse a superposition across many depths with a depth-dependent weighting; the same growth law should give an analogous bound, but the correct formulation of "dimension" is then the total number of nodes below the critical hypotenuse, which is $\Theta(N)$ — a stronger obstruction, but one that deserves a precise statement.

5. **Other Diophantine trees.** The argument only used: unique addressing, a lower growth rate $g_{\min}$, an upper growth rate $g_{\max}$, and a branching factor $b$. The barrier reads $b^n \ge (N/c_0)^{\log b/\log g_{\max}}$, which is exponential in $\log N$ whenever $b > 1$. Identify Diophantine trees with $g_{\max}$ very large relative to $b$ (so the exponent is small) and a resonance mechanism, and determine whether the rigidity phenomenon persists.

6. **Rigidity for approximate optima.** Theorem 6.9 characterises exact equality. A stability version — states within $\varepsilon$ of the bound are within $\delta(\varepsilon)$ of the indicator — would show that even *approximately* optimal coins encode the answer, closing the last conceivable loophole.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The Berggren tree is a complete, rigid state space for the primitive Pythagorean triples, and the arithmetic energy $E_N(t) = c \bmod N$ turns it into a resonator for the target $N$. On the domain where the resonator can sound at all — odd $N$ all of whose prime factors are $\equiv 1 \bmod 4$ — the mechanism is exact: every non-prime-power target carries an interference pair whose gcd is an exact nontrivial proper divisor, and every prime power carries exactly one resonant node and provably refuses to split, so multiplicity is a primality certificate.

The efficiency claim fails, and it fails for a reason that can be written in one line: values grow by at most $7$ per branch while the layer widens by $3$, and $9 > 7$, so the first resonant layer has $\sqrt{N/5}$ branches. No coin can evade this — below the critical depth every state has zero resonance amplitude, at any depth the gain of every state is at most the arithmetic multiplicity, and the unique state attaining that gain is the indicator of the resonance set, whose preparation already encodes the answer.

The arithmetic always cooperates; the search space never does.

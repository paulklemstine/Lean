## Abstract

A *self-applicative presentation* consists of a set $A$ of codes, a set $B$ of
meanings, and an evaluation table $\mathrm{ev} : A \times A \to B$ permitting a
code to be applied to a code — in particular to itself. Such a presentation is
*universal* when every observation $g : A \to B$ is performed by some code.
Universality is the abstract form of the "sufficiently rich system" hypothesis
underlying Gödel's diagonal lemma, Turing's halting argument, Russell's paradox
and Lawvere's fixed-point theorem, and it is the hypothesis under which one may
hope to compare formal, visual and musical syntaxes of self-reference as
different presentations of a single strange loop.

We prove first that this hypothesis is **vacuous**: a universal self-applicative
presentation over $B$ exists only when $B$ is a subsingleton. The obstruction is
uniform — every domain with two distinct meanings carries a definable
fixed-point-free endomorphism — so the familiar Boolean impossibility (driven by
negation) is merely its two-element instance, and the dichotomy is sharp, since
subsingleton domains do admit universal presentations.

We then develop the correct replacement: **graded universality**, requiring only
that a table represent the single diagonal composite $x \mapsto
f(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$ associated with the semantic endomorphism $f$ of interest.
The diagonal argument survives verbatim, and admits a complete converse. Our
central result, the **Obstruction Spectrum Theorem**, states that for any family
$F$ of endomorphisms of $B$ there exists a single table diagonally representing
every member of $F$ if and only if every member of $F$ has a fixed point.
Consequently representability is a pointwise semantic invariant: cardinality of
$B$ is irrelevant and, contrary to a natural conjecture, so is the
transformation monoid generated by $F$ — on a three-element domain, two
involutions with fixed points are jointly representable while the
fixed-point-free three-cycle they generate is representable by no table
whatsoever.

Three further developments follow. (i) *Choice dependence*: uniqueness of
semantic fixed points forces all diagonal codes, even in unrelated
presentations, to share a value; without uniqueness two codes in one table can
disagree; contractions on $\mathbb{R}$ supply a natural class where uniqueness
holds. (ii) *Weak simulations*: evaluation-preserving equivalences transport
codes and values, coherent retractions lift codes, embeddings preserve values
but not code-hood, and bisimulations preserve only observational equality — a
four-level hierarchy, each level separated by an explicit counterexample.
(iii) *An explicit graded triad*: three concrete syntaxes over the pitch classes
$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ — a formal calculus of self-substitution, a visual
completion calculus, and a contrapuntal calculus — carry evaluation-preserving
equivalences and a common diagonal value $0$ for inversion, while semitone
transposition is representable in no syntax at all.

Finally, since the obstruction spectrum pins down the maximal representable
family exactly, it can be counted: over an $n$-element domain precisely
$n^n - (n-1)^n$ endomorphisms are diagonally representable, with asymptotic
density $1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.6321$. A definite positive proportion of finite
semantic dynamics is permanently beyond self-applicative syntax.

**Keywords:** Lawvere fixed-point theorem, diagonal argument, self-reference,
graded universality, obstruction spectrum, transformation monoids, pitch-class
inversion, fixed-point density.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The diagonal engine

A remarkable number of the twentieth century's limitative theorems are the same
argument wearing different clothes. Cantor's proof that no set surjects onto its
power set, Russell's paradox, Turing's undecidability of the halting problem,
Tarski's undefinability of truth, and Gödel's first incompleteness theorem all
reduce to a single categorical statement, isolated by Lawvere: if a "point-surjective"
map exists, every endomorphism of the codomain has a fixed point.
Contrapositively, exhibiting a fixed-point-free endomorphism destroys
point-surjectivity, which is what each of these theorems in fact does.

The set-level shadow of Lawvere's theorem is easy to state. Let $A$ be a set of
*codes* and $B$ a set of *meanings*, and let
$$\mathrm{ev} : A \to (A \to B)$$
be an evaluation, curried as $\mathrm{ev}(a, x) \in B$. Applying a code to
itself yields the *diagonal* $x \mapsto \mathrm{ev}(x,x)$. Suppose
$\mathrm{ev}$ is *universal*, meaning surjective onto $A \to B$: every
observation on codes is performed by a code. Then for any $f : B \to B$ the
observation $x \mapsto f(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$ has a code $c$, and instantiating
the defining equation at $c$ gives $\mathrm{ev}(c,c) = f(\mathrm{ev}(c,c))$: a
fixed point of $f$.

### 1.2 The comparative programme and its failure

This abstraction invites a comparative programme, in the spirit of Hofstadter's
braid of formal, visual and musical self-reference: build three different syntax
types — a formal calculus of substitution, a visual calculus of pictorial
completion, a contrapuntal calculus of reversible transformation — each with its
own universal self-application table, link them by evaluation-preserving
equivalences, and observe that their diagonal witnesses agree. The intuition
guiding this programme is exactly right at the level of *interface*: what makes
fixed points transport is preservation of the binary evaluation table, not a
bare bijection of artifacts.

The programme nevertheless cannot be carried out with universality, and Section
2 explains why in the strongest terms: universality is unsatisfiable over any
semantic domain with two distinct meanings. Every theorem of the form "let
$(A,\mathrm{ev})$ be a universal presentation over a nontrivial $B$; then …" is
a theorem with empty hypotheses.

### 1.3 Contributions

We restore the programme by weakening universality along precisely the axis the
diagonal argument uses.

- **§2 Vacuity.** A universal presentation forces $B$ to be a subsingleton, and
  the dichotomy is sharp.
- **§3 Graded universality.** Representing a single diagonal composite suffices;
  and, as a complete converse, a family is jointly representable iff each
  member has a fixed point (Obstruction Spectrum Theorem).
- **§4 Non-monoidality.** Representability is pointwise, not monoid-controlled;
  a $3$-element counterexample refutes the monoid-closure conjecture.
  Representing a rich class of observations does not imply diagonal closure.
- **§5 Choice dependence.** Semantic uniqueness $\Rightarrow$ global code
  independence, with a metric instance (contractions on $\mathbb{R}$) and a
  two-element counterexample when uniqueness fails.
- **§6 Weak simulations.** A four-level coherence hierarchy: equivalence
  $>$ coherent retraction $>$ embedding $>$ bisimulation, with separating
  counterexamples.
- **§7 An explicit graded triad** over pitch classes $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.
- **§8 Counting.** $n^n - (n-1)^n$ representable endomorphisms; density
  $\to 1 - e^{-1}$.

## 2. Universality is vacuous

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Presentation).** Let $A$ (codes) and $B$ (meanings) be sets. A
*presentation* of $B$ over $A$ is a map $\mathrm{ev} : A \to (A \to B)$ that is
surjective: for every $g : A \to B$ there is $c \in A$ with $\mathrm{ev}(c,x) =
g(x)$ for all $x \in A$. We write $\mathrm{ev}(a,x)$ for the value of the
function $\mathrm{ev}(a)$ at $x$, and call $x \mapsto \mathrm{ev}(x,x)$ the
*diagonal* of the presentation.

Surjectivity is the formalisation of "the syntax can express every observation
about its own artifacts". It is the hypothesis under which Lawvere's argument
runs.

### 2.2 A uniformly definable fixed-point-free endomorphism

**Definition 2.2.** For distinct $b_1, b_2 \in B$ define
$$\mathrm{swap}_{b_1,b_2}(x) = \begin{cases} b_2, & x = b_1, \\ b_1, & x \ne
b_1.\end{cases}$$

**Lemma 2.3.** If $b_1 \ne b_2$ then $\mathrm{swap}_{b_1,b_2}$ has no fixed
point.

*Proof.* Two cases. If $x = b_1$ then $\mathrm{swap}(x) = b_2 \ne b_1 = x$. If
$x \ne b_1$ then $\mathrm{swap}(x) = b_1 \ne x$. $\square$

The point of Lemma 2.3 is its uniformity. One does not need a group structure, a
negation, an involution, or any ambient algebra: the mere existence of two
distinct points in $B$ suffices to define, with classical case analysis alone, a
derangement-like self-map of $B$.

### 2.3 The collapse

**Theorem 2.4 (Vacuity of universality).** If a presentation
$\mathrm{ev} : A \to (A \to B)$ exists, then $B$ is a subsingleton: any two
elements of $B$ are equal.

*Proof.* Let $b_1, b_2 \in B$ and suppose $b_1 \ne b_2$. Apply surjectivity to
the observation $g(x) = \mathrm{swap}_{b_1,b_2}(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$, obtaining a
code $c$ with $\mathrm{ev}(c,x) = \mathrm{swap}_{b_1,b_2}(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$ for
all $x$. Instantiating at $x = c$ gives
$\mathrm{swap}_{b_1,b_2}(\mathrm{ev}(c,c)) = \mathrm{ev}(c,c)$, contradicting
Lemma 2.3. Hence $b_1 = b_2$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.5 (No universal presentation over a nontrivial domain).** If $B$
has two distinct elements then no presentation of $B$ exists, over any set of
codes $A$ whatsoever.

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 2.4 and the definition of nontriviality.
$\square$

**Corollary 2.6 (Boolean impossibility).** There is no presentation of
$B = \{\text{true},\text{false}\}$.

This recovers the classical Boolean obstruction — usually attributed to the
fixed-point-freeness of negation — as the two-element instance of a general
phenomenon. Negation is nothing but $\mathrm{swap}_{\text{true},\text{false}}$.

**Proposition 2.7 (Sharpness).** If $B$ is a subsingleton and $A$, $B$ are
inhabited, then a presentation of $B$ over $A$ exists.

*Proof.* Take the constant table $\mathrm{ev}(a,x) = b_0$ for the unique
$b_0 \in B$. Given $g : A \to B$, the code $a_0$ works, since $g(x) = b_0$ for
all $x$ by subsingletonness. $\square$

**Remark 2.8.** Theorems 2.4–2.7 constitute an exact dichotomy at the boundary
between one meaning and two. There is no intermediate regime, no "partially
universal" table in the surjectivity sense, and no cardinality-based escape:
the obstruction is not that $A \to B$ is too big for $A$ (a Cantorian reading)
but that a fixed-point-free endomorphism is always definable. Comparative
programmes premised on universal presentations over nontrivial semantics are
therefore programmes about the empty set, however correct their internal
reasoning.

## 3. Graded universality and the obstruction spectrum

### 3.1 The right hypothesis

Inspecting the proof of the Lawvere argument, surjectivity is used exactly once,
for one observation. We isolate that use.

**Definition 3.1 (Representation).** Let $\mathrm{ev} : A \times A \to B$ be any
table (no surjectivity assumed) and $g : A \to B$. We say $\mathrm{ev}$
*represents* $g$ if there is $c \in A$ with $\mathrm{ev}(c,x) = g(x)$ for all
$x \in A$.

**Definition 3.2 (Diagonal representation).** For $f : B \to B$, we say
$\mathrm{ev}$ *diagonally represents* $f$ if it represents the *diagonal
composite* $x \mapsto f(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$; explicitly, if there is $c \in A$
with
$$\mathrm{ev}(c,x) = f(\mathrm{ev}(x,x)) \qquad \text{for all } x \in A.$$
Such a $c$ is called a *diagonal code for $f$*.

Universality implies diagonal representation of every $f$; by Theorem 2.4 the
converse implication is not merely unnecessary but incompatible with nontrivial
semantics.

### 3.2 The graded theorem

**Theorem 3.3 (Graded Lawvere theorem).** If $\mathrm{ev}$ diagonally
represents $f$ via the code $c$, then $\mathrm{ev}(c,c)$ is a fixed point of
$f$; in particular $f$ has a fixed point.

*Proof.* Instantiate the defining equation at $x = c$:
$\mathrm{ev}(c,c) = f(\mathrm{ev}(c,c))$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.4 (Diagonal value is a fixed point).** If $c$ is a diagonal code for
$f$ in $\mathrm{ev}$, then $f(\mathrm{ev}(c,c)) = \mathrm{ev}(c,c)$.

This is the same computation, recorded separately because it is the interface
used by every transport result in §5–§6: the *value* $\mathrm{ev}(c,c)$, not the
code, is what the semantics constrains.

### 3.3 The converse: the free graded model

The graded hypothesis is not merely sufficient — it is exactly right.

**Theorem 3.5 (Free graded model).** Let $B$ be a set and $F \subseteq B \to B$
a family of endomorphisms such that every $f \in F$ has a fixed point. Then
there exist a code set $A$ and a table $\mathrm{ev} : A \times A \to B$ which
diagonally represents *every* member of $F$ simultaneously.

*Proof sketch.* Choose, for each $f \in F$, a fixed point $b_f$ with
$f(b_f) = b_f$. Let $A = F \sqcup B$: codes are either *labels* $\ell_f$ for
$f \in F$ or *literals* $\underline{b}$ for $b \in B$. Define the *self-value*
function $d : A \to B$ by
$$d(\ell_f) = b_f, \qquad d(\underline{b}) = b,$$
and define the table by
$$\mathrm{ev}(\ell_f, x) = f(d(x)), \qquad
  \mathrm{ev}(\underline{b}, x) = b.$$
The key computation is that the table's diagonal agrees with $d$:
$\mathrm{ev}(\underline{b},\underline{b}) = b = d(\underline{b})$, while
$\mathrm{ev}(\ell_f, \ell_f) = f(d(\ell_f)) = f(b_f) = b_f = d(\ell_f)$, using
precisely the fixed-point property. Consequently, for every $x$,
$$\mathrm{ev}(\ell_f, x) = f(d(x)) = f(\mathrm{ev}(x,x)),$$
so $\ell_f$ is a diagonal code for $f$. $\square$

The construction deserves comment. The literals exist to make the diagonal
surject onto $B$, so that the table is semantically nondegenerate; the labels
exist to carry the codes. The fixed points $b_f$ are *chosen*, and this choice
is exactly the source of the ambiguity analysed in §5.

**Theorem 3.6 (Obstruction Spectrum Theorem).** For any set $B$ and any family
$F \subseteq B \to B$, the following are equivalent:

1. there exist $A$ and $\mathrm{ev} : A \times A \to B$ diagonally representing
   every $f \in F$;
2. every $f \in F$ has a fixed point.

*Proof.* (1) $\Rightarrow$ (2) is Theorem 3.3 applied member by member;
(2) $\Rightarrow$ (1) is Theorem 3.5. $\square$

**Corollary 3.7 (Maximal representable family).** The set
$$\mathrm{Rep}(B) = \{\, f : B \to B \mid \exists b,\ f(b) = b \,\}$$
is the greatest element, under inclusion, of the family of jointly
diagonally-representable sets of endomorphisms of $B$.

Theorem 3.6 is the paper's organising result. It converts a question about
*syntax* ("is there a rich enough calculus?") into a question about *semantics*
("does this map move everything?"), with no residue: no cardinality condition,
no closure condition, no structural condition on $A$.

## 4. Representability is pointwise, not monoidal

A natural conjecture, once one sees that representability is a semantic
condition, is that the maximal representable families are controlled by the
transformation monoid generated by $F$: if the syntax can talk about $f$ and
$g$, surely it must be able to talk about $f \circ g$, and so the correct
invariant should be the fixed-point structure of $\langle F \rangle$. This is
false, and the smallest counterexample is on three points.

**Definition 4.1.** On $B = \{0,1,2\}$ let $t_A = (0\;1)$ and $t_B = (1\;2)$ be
the two adjacent transpositions, i.e.
$t_A: 0 \mapsto 1, 1 \mapsto 0, 2 \mapsto 2$ and
$t_B: 0 \mapsto 0, 1 \mapsto 2, 2 \mapsto 1$.

**Lemma 4.2.** $t_A$ fixes $2$; $t_B$ fixes $0$; and $t_A \circ t_B$ is the
three-cycle $0 \mapsto 1 \mapsto 2 \mapsto 0$, which has no fixed point.

*Proof.* Direct evaluation on three points: $(t_A \circ t_B)(0) = t_A(0) = 1$,
$(t_A \circ t_B)(1) = t_A(2) = 2$, $(t_A \circ t_B)(2) = t_A(1) = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (Failure of monoid control).** There is a single table
$\mathrm{ev} : A \times A \to \{0,1,2\}$ diagonally representing both $t_A$ and
$t_B$; and there is *no* table over any code set diagonally representing
$t_A \circ t_B$.

*Proof.* The first claim is Theorem 3.5 applied to $F = \{t_A, t_B\}$, whose
members have fixed points by Lemma 4.2. The second is Theorem 3.3 contraposed:
a diagonal code for $t_A \circ t_B$ would produce a fixed point of a
fixed-point-free map. $\square$

**Remark 4.4 (Why closure fails).** The graded argument consumes the semantic
endomorphism *exactly once*. The table never evaluates a composite of members
of $F$; each label of the free model in Theorem 3.5 applies its own $f$ and
stops. Nothing in the diagonal mechanism forces the represented class to be
closed under composition. Consequently the correct invariant is the *pointwise*
fixed-point predicate on $F$, not any algebraic invariant of $\langle F
\rangle$. This also shows that "the maximal representable family is a monoid" is
false in general: $\mathrm{Rep}(B)$ is closed under neither composition (Lemma
4.2) nor, in general, any nontrivial algebraic operation.

A second sharpness statement separates *richness* of a represented class from
*diagonal closure*.

**Theorem 4.5 (Diagonal closure is a genuine extra axiom).** There is a table
$\mathrm{ev} : \{\top,\bot\} \times \{\top,\bot\} \to \{\top,\bot\}$
representing every constant observation, while no table whatsoever, over any
code set, diagonally represents Boolean negation.

*Proof.* For the first claim take $\mathrm{ev}(a,x) = a$: the code $b$
represents the constant observation with value $b$. For the second, a diagonal
code for negation would by Theorem 3.3 produce $b$ with $\neg b = b$, and both
truth values refute this. $\square$

Thus a table may represent an arbitrarily rich class of observations (here, all
constants; in general one may add much more) and still fail to represent one
diagonal composite. Partial universality in the sense of "many represented
observations" is strictly weaker than the closure condition the graded theorem
requires.

## 5. Choice dependence of the diagonal value

Theorem 3.3 produces *a* fixed point. Which one?

**Theorem 5.1 (Global code independence from semantic uniqueness).** Let
$f : B \to B$ have at most one fixed point: whenever $f(b) = b$ and
$f(b') = b'$, we have $b = b'$. Let $\mathrm{ev}_P : A \times A \to B$ and
$\mathrm{ev}_Q : C \times C \to B$ be arbitrary tables over arbitrary,
completely unrelated code sets, with diagonal codes $c \in A$ and $e \in C$ for
$f$. Then
$$\mathrm{ev}_P(c,c) = \mathrm{ev}_Q(e,e).$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.4 both values are fixed points of $f$; uniqueness identifies
them. $\square$

No isomorphism, embedding, or simulation of code domains is assumed. The
identification is purely semantic. This isolates cleanly the two sources of
ambiguity in the diagonal construction:

- *presentation ambiguity*, which is canonical and transports along
  evaluation-preserving maps (§6);
- *code ambiguity*, which is genuine, and collapses precisely under semantic
  uniqueness.

**Theorem 5.2 (Failure without uniqueness).** There is a table
$\mathrm{ev} : \{\top,\bot\}^2 \to \{\top,\bot\}$ and codes $c \neq e$ that are
both diagonal codes for the identity, with $\mathrm{ev}(c,c) \ne
\mathrm{ev}(e,e)$.

*Proof.* Take the projection table $\mathrm{ev}(a,x) = x$. Then for every code
$c$ and every $x$, $\mathrm{ev}(c,x) = x = \mathrm{ev}(x,x) =
\mathrm{id}(\mathrm{ev}(x,x))$, so *every* code is a diagonal code for the
identity. Take $c = \top$, $e = \bot$: the diagonal values are $\top$ and
$\bot$. $\square$

The identity has two fixed points on a two-element domain, and the two diagonal
codes select different ones. So the hypothesis in Theorem 5.1 cannot be dropped
even within a single table, let alone across unrelated tables.

### 5.1 A metric source of uniqueness

Uniqueness is not an exotic hypothesis: contraction supplies it.

**Lemma 5.3.** Let $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfy
$|f(x) - f(y)| \le K|x - y|$ for all $x,y$, with $K < 1$. Then $f$ has at most
one fixed point.

*Proof.* If $f(b) = b$ and $f(b') = b'$ then $|b - b'| = |f(b) - f(b')| \le
K|b-b'|$, so $(1-K)|b-b'| \le 0$. Since $K < 1$ and $|b-b'| \ge 0$, we get
$|b - b'| = 0$, i.e. $b = b'$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Canonical diagonal value for contractions).** Let $f$ be as in
Lemma 5.3. Then all diagonal codes for $f$, in all presentations — related or
not — evaluate to one and the same real number.

*Proof.* Lemma 5.3 plus Theorem 5.1. $\square$

By the Banach fixed-point theorem the common value is also the limit of the
iterates $f^n(x_0)$ from any starting point, so the strange loop of a
contracting semantics has a *computable canonical value*, independent of the
syntax that expresses it. This answers, for a natural class of ordered/metric
semantic domains, the question of when arbitrary universal codes must choose the
same fixed point.

## 6. Weak simulations: a four-level coherence hierarchy

We now ask how much of the diagonal structure survives when two presentations
are related by something weaker than an isomorphism. Four levels emerge, each
strictly weaker than the last.

### 6.1 Level 1: evaluation-preserving equivalence

**Definition 6.1.** An *evaluation-preserving equivalence* between tables
$\mathrm{ev}_P : A \times A \to B$ and $\mathrm{ev}_Q : C \times C \to B$ is a
bijection $\varphi : A \to C$ with
$$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\varphi a, \varphi x) = \mathrm{ev}_P(a,x)
  \qquad\text{for all } a,x \in A.$$

Note what is preserved: the *binary* table, in both arguments simultaneously.
This is the interface, not a bare bijection of artifacts, and it is exactly what
makes fixed points transport.

**Theorem 6.2 (Transport of diagonal codes and values).** Let $\varphi$ be an
evaluation-preserving equivalence and let $c$ be a diagonal code for $f$ in
$\mathrm{ev}_P$. Then $\varphi(c)$ is a diagonal code for $f$ in
$\mathrm{ev}_Q$, and
$$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\varphi c, \varphi c) = \mathrm{ev}_P(c,c).$$

*Proof.* The value equality is preservation at $(c,c)$. For code-hood, let
$y \in C$; by surjectivity of $\varphi$ write $y = \varphi x$. Then
$$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\varphi c, \varphi x) = \mathrm{ev}_P(c, x)
 = f(\mathrm{ev}_P(x,x)) = f(\mathrm{ev}_Q(\varphi x, \varphi x))
 = f(\mathrm{ev}_Q(y,y)),$$
using preservation twice and the diagonal equation for $c$ in the middle.
$\square$

Surjectivity is used only to reach every $y$; injectivity is not needed for the
computation, but is needed to call $\varphi$ an equivalence and to invert the
transport.

### 6.2 Level 2: coherent retraction

**Definition 6.3.** A *coherent retraction* from $\mathrm{ev}_Q$ onto
$\mathrm{ev}_P$ consists of maps $s : A \to C$ (section) and $r : C \to A$
(retraction) with $r \circ s = \mathrm{id}_A$ and the *coherence law*
$$\mathrm{ev}_Q(c, x) = \mathrm{ev}_P(r c, r x)
  \qquad \text{for all } c,x \in C,$$
i.e. evaluation in the larger table is computed in the smaller one.

**Theorem 6.4 (Retractions lift diagonal codes).** Under a coherent retraction,
if $c$ is a diagonal code for $f$ in $\mathrm{ev}_P$ then $s(c)$ is a diagonal
code for $f$ in $\mathrm{ev}_Q$.

*Proof.* For $y \in C$,
$$\mathrm{ev}_Q(s c, y) = \mathrm{ev}_P(r(s c), r y) = \mathrm{ev}_P(c, r y)
 = f(\mathrm{ev}_P(r y, r y)) = f(\mathrm{ev}_Q(y,y)),$$
using coherence, $r \circ s = \mathrm{id}$, the diagonal equation at $r y$, and
coherence again. $\square$

So strange loops present in a fragment survive into any coherent extension. The
coherence law is doing real work: it says the extension adds no new evaluation
behaviour, only new names.

### 6.3 Level 3: evaluation-preserving embedding

**Definition 6.5.** An *evaluation-preserving embedding* is an injection
$\iota : A \to C$ with $\mathrm{ev}_Q(\iota a, \iota x) = \mathrm{ev}_P(a,x)$
for all $a, x \in A$.

**Theorem 6.6 (Embeddings preserve values).** If $c$ is a diagonal code for $f$
in $\mathrm{ev}_P$ and $\iota$ is an evaluation-preserving embedding, then
$$f\bigl(\mathrm{ev}_Q(\iota c, \iota c)\bigr) = \mathrm{ev}_Q(\iota c, \iota c),$$
and the diagonal equation holds *on the image*:
$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\iota c, \iota x) = f(\mathrm{ev}_Q(\iota x, \iota x))$ for all
$x \in A$.

*Proof.* Rewrite by preservation and apply Lemma 3.4, respectively the diagonal
equation for $c$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.7 (Embeddings do not preserve code-hood).** There are tables
$\mathrm{ev}_P$ over a one-element code set and $\mathrm{ev}_Q$ over
$\{\top,\bot\}$, valued in $\{\top,\bot\}$, an evaluation-preserving embedding
$\iota$, and a diagonal code $c$ for the identity in $\mathrm{ev}_P$, such that
$\iota(c)$ is *not* a diagonal code for the identity in $\mathrm{ev}_Q$.

*Proof.* Let $A = \{\ast\}$ with $\mathrm{ev}_P(\ast,\ast) = \top$, let
$C = \{\top,\bot\}$ with the first-projection table $\mathrm{ev}_Q(a,x) = a$,
and let $\iota(\ast) = \top$. Preservation holds:
$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\top, \top) = \top = \mathrm{ev}_P(\ast,\ast)$. The code $\ast$
is trivially a diagonal code for the identity in $\mathrm{ev}_P$, since $A$ has
one element. But $\iota(\ast) = \top$ fails the diagonal equation at $y = \bot$:
$\mathrm{ev}_Q(\top, \bot) = \top$, while
$\mathrm{id}(\mathrm{ev}_Q(\bot,\bot)) = \bot$. $\square$

The failure mechanism is transparent: the diagonal equation is a *universally
quantified* condition over all codes, and an embedding introduces new codes at
which the transported code need not satisfy it. A loop can cease to be a loop
when the world around it grows. This is precisely why Theorem 6.2 needs
surjectivity and Theorem 6.4 needs coherence.

### 6.4 Level 4: bisimulation

**Definition 6.8.** Let $\mathrm{rel} \subseteq A \times C$ relate codes and
$R \subseteq B \times B$ relate meanings. Say $\mathrm{rel}$ is a
*bisimulation* (observed through $R$) if for all $a, c$ with $\mathrm{rel}(a,c)$
we have $R(\mathrm{ev}_P(a,a), \mathrm{ev}_Q(c,c))$.

**Proposition 6.9.** A bisimulation relates the diagonal values of related
codes: if $\mathrm{rel}(c,e)$, then $R(\mathrm{ev}_P(c,c), \mathrm{ev}_Q(e,e))$.

**Theorem 6.10 (Bisimulation gives only observational agreement).** There are
tables $\mathrm{ev}_P, \mathrm{ev}_Q$ over one-element code sets, valued in
$\mathbb{N}$, bisimilar for the parity relation
$R(m,n) \iff m \equiv n \pmod 2$, whose unique codes are diagonal codes for the
identity, yet whose diagonal values differ.

*Proof.* Take $\mathrm{ev}_P \equiv 0$ and $\mathrm{ev}_Q \equiv 2$ with the
total relation on codes. Parity holds ($0 \equiv 2$), both codes are trivially
diagonal codes for the identity, and $0 \ne 2$. $\square$

### 6.5 Summary of the hierarchy

| Relation | Diagonal code transports | Diagonal value |
|---|---|---|
| Evaluation-preserving equivalence | Yes (both directions) | Equal |
| Coherent retraction | Yes, lifts upward | Equal to retract's |
| Evaluation-preserving embedding | No (Thm 6.7) | Equal, still a fixed point |
| Bisimulation | No | Related by $R$ only (Thm 6.10) |

Each row is separated from the next by an explicit counterexample, so the
hierarchy is strict. The conceptual reading: exact isomorphism yields *equality*
because both arguments of evaluation are preserved simultaneously; weakening
either preservation law produces a graded notion of fixed-point transport.

## 7. An explicit graded triad: formal, visual, contrapuntal

Section 2 forbids a universal triad over nontrivial semantics. We construct a
nontrivial *graded* one, with meanings the twelve pitch classes
$B = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.

### 7.1 Semantic endomorphisms of pitch class

- **Inversion** $I(x) = -x$. Fixed points: $x = -x \iff 2x = 0 \iff x \in
  \{0, 6\}$.
- **Tritone inversion** $T\!I(x) = 6 - x$. Fixed points: $2x = 6 \iff x \in
  \{3, 9\}$.
- **Transposition** $P(x) = x + 1$. Fixed points: $x + 1 = x \iff 1 = 0$ in
  $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$: **none**.

### 7.2 Three syntaxes

Each syntax has three constructors: two *self-application operators* and a
family of *literals*.

- **Formal:** $\mathsf{selfInvert}$, $\mathsf{selfTritone}$,
  $\mathsf{lit}(b)$ for $b \in B$. (A calculus of self-substitution: an operator
  substituted into itself.)
- **Visual:** $\mathsf{drawingHands}$, $\mathsf{mirrorHands}$,
  $\mathsf{frame}(b)$. (A completion calculus: a hand drawing the hand that
  draws it, and its mirrored variant.)
- **Contrapuntal:** $\mathsf{crab}$, $\mathsf{crabTritone}$,
  $\mathsf{note}(b)$. (A crab canon, its tritone answer, and single notes.)

Each syntax carries a *self-value* $d$ and a table. For the formal syntax,
$$d(\mathsf{selfInvert}) = 0,\quad d(\mathsf{selfTritone}) = 3, \quad
  d(\mathsf{lit}\,b) = b,$$
$$\mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfInvert}, x) = I(d(x)), \quad
  \mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfTritone}, x) = T\!I(d(x)), \quad
  \mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{lit}\,b, x) = b,$$
and the visual and contrapuntal syntaxes are defined by the same clauses under
the constructor correspondence
$$\mathsf{selfInvert} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{drawingHands} \leftrightarrow
  \mathsf{crab},\quad
  \mathsf{selfTritone} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{mirrorHands} \leftrightarrow
  \mathsf{crabTritone},\quad
  \mathsf{lit} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{frame} \leftrightarrow \mathsf{note}.$$

**Lemma 7.1 (Diagonal computes the self-value).** In each syntax,
$\mathrm{ev}(x,x) = d(x)$ for every code $x$.

*Proof.* Three cases per syntax. Literals: $\mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{lit}\,b,
\mathsf{lit}\,b) = b = d(\mathsf{lit}\,b)$. Inversion operator:
$\mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfInvert},\mathsf{selfInvert}) = I(0) = 0 =
d(\mathsf{selfInvert})$. Tritone operator:
$\mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfTritone},\mathsf{selfTritone}) = T\!I(3) = 6 - 3 = 3 =
d(\mathsf{selfTritone})$. Note that the self-values were chosen precisely to be
fixed points, exactly as in the free graded model of Theorem 3.5. $\square$

**Lemma 7.2 (The operators are diagonal codes).** In each syntax the inversion
operator is a diagonal code for $I$ and the tritone operator a diagonal code
for $T\!I$: for all $x$,
$$\mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfInvert},x) = I(\mathrm{ev}(x,x)), \qquad
  \mathrm{ev}(\mathsf{selfTritone},x) = T\!I(\mathrm{ev}(x,x)).$$

*Proof.* By definition the left-hand sides are $I(d(x))$ and $T\!I(d(x))$; apply
Lemma 7.1. $\square$

**Proposition 7.3 (Evaluation-preserving equivalences).** The constructor
correspondences above are bijections of code sets satisfying
$\mathrm{ev}_{\text{visual}}(\varphi a, \varphi x) =
\mathrm{ev}_{\text{formal}}(a,x)$, and similarly for the contrapuntal syntax.

*Proof.* The maps are visibly mutually inverse on the three constructor
families; preservation is a nine-case check, each case an instance of identical
defining clauses. $\square$

### 7.3 The common diagonal value

**Theorem 7.4 (Common value across the triad).** The images of the formal
inversion code under the two equivalences are diagonal codes for $I$ in the
visual and contrapuntal syntaxes; all three diagonal values are equal to the
pitch class $0$; and $I(0) = 0$.

*Proof.* Code transport is Theorem 6.2 applied to Proposition 7.3 and Lemma 7.2.
The three values are $0$ by Lemma 7.1 and the definition of $d$ on the inversion
operator, and $-0 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

This is the comparative programme carried out honestly: a genuine, nonvacuous
triad of formal, visual and contrapuntal syntaxes, related by
evaluation-preserving equivalences, with agreeing diagonal witnesses.

**Theorem 7.5 (Choice dependence, musically).** The same triad contains a second
diagonal code, for tritone inversion, with diagonal value $3 \ne 0$, and
$T\!I(3) = 3$.

So the diagonal value depends on the semantic endomorphism and — absent
uniqueness (both $I$ and $T\!I$ have two fixed points) — on the code. This is
the abstract phenomenon of §5 realised in a concrete musical setting.

**Theorem 7.6 (Transposition is never representable).** No table over any code
set — formal, visual, contrapuntal, or otherwise — diagonally represents
semitone transposition $P(x) = x + 1$.

*Proof.* A diagonal code would give $b$ with $b + 1 = b$ in
$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, hence $1 = 0$, false. $\square$

**Corollary 7.7 (The boundary inside one semantic domain).** Over
$\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ the single formal table represents both $I$ and
$T\!I$, while $P$ is representable by nothing at all.

Musically: a piece of music can be *about* its own inversion and remain
coherent; it can never be *about* its own transposition. The distinction is
neither aesthetic nor accidental — it is the fixed-point count of the underlying
map.

## 8. The size of the obstruction spectrum

Because Corollary 3.7 identifies the maximal representable family *exactly*, on
a finite domain it can be counted.

**Lemma 8.1 (Fixed-point-free count).** The number of fixed-point-free
self-maps of an $n$-element set is $(n-1)^n$.

*Proof sketch.* A self-map $f$ is a choice of $f(b)$ for each of the $n$ points
$b$, independently. Fixed-point-freeness is the conjunction of the $n$ pointwise
constraints $f(b) \ne b$, each of which restricts that coordinate to $n-1$
values. Hence the constrained product has $(n-1)^n$ elements. Formally, the
subtype of fixed-point-free maps is equivalent to the dependent product of the
punctured fibres, whose cardinality is $\prod_{b}(n-1) = (n-1)^n$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.2 (Cardinality of the obstruction spectrum).** Over an $n$-element
semantic domain exactly
$$\#\mathrm{Rep} = n^n - (n-1)^n$$
endomorphisms are diagonally representable.

*Proof.* The representable ones are precisely those with a fixed point
(Theorem 3.6); this is the complement of the fixed-point-free ones inside all
$n^n$ self-maps, so its cardinality is $n^n - (n-1)^n$ by Lemma 8.1. $\square$

Small values:

| $n$ | $n^n$ | $(n-1)^n$ | representable | density |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1.0000 |
| 2 | 4 | 1 | 3 | 0.7500 |
| 3 | 27 | 8 | 19 | 0.7037 |
| 4 | 256 | 81 | 175 | 0.6836 |
| 5 | 3125 | 1024 | 2101 | 0.6723 |
| 6 | 46656 | 15625 | 31031 | 0.6651 |

**Theorem 8.3 (Asymptotic density).** The proportion of diagonally representable
endomorphisms of an $n$-element domain satisfies
$$\frac{n^n - (n-1)^n}{n^n} = 1 - \Bigl(1 - \frac1n\Bigr)^n
  \;\longrightarrow\; 1 - e^{-1} = 0.632120\ldots$$

*Proof.* $\bigl(1 + \tfrac{-1}{n}\bigr)^n \to e^{-1}$ is the standard
exponential limit; subtract from the constant $1$ and identify
$\bigl(\tfrac{n-1}{n}\bigr)^n$ with $\bigl(1 + \tfrac{-1}{n}\bigr)^n$ for
$n \ge 1$. $\square$

The obstruction is therefore *quantitative*, not merely qualitative: a fixed
proportion $e^{-1} \approx 36.8\%$ of finite semantic dynamics lies permanently
outside self-applicative syntax, in any medium and at any scale, while the
complementary $63.2\%$ admits a strange loop by the explicit construction of
Theorem 3.5.

## 9. Algorithms

The theory is effective on finite domains. Three algorithms follow directly.

**A. Representability decision.** Given a finite $B$ (size $n$) and
$f : B \to B$ as a table, decide diagonal representability by scanning for
$b$ with $f(b) = b$. Time $O(n)$, space $O(1)$. Correctness: Theorem 3.6.

**B. Free graded model construction.** Given a finite family $F$ of
representable endomorphisms of $B$, build $A = F \sqcup B$, self-values
$d(\ell_f) = $ least fixed point of $f$, $d(\underline{b}) = b$, and the table
$\mathrm{ev}(\ell_f, x) = f(d(x))$, $\mathrm{ev}(\underline{b},x) = b$. Building
the self-value array costs $O(|F| \cdot n)$; the table is $O((|F|+n)^2)$ to
materialise, or $O(1)$ per query lazily. Correctness: Theorem 3.5. Verification
of the diagonal equations is $O(|F| \cdot (|F| + n))$.

**C. Spectrum enumeration and density.** Enumerate all $n^n$ maps on $\{0,\dots,
n-1\}$, count those with a fixed point, and compare with $n^n - (n-1)^n$. Time
$\Theta(n^{n+1})$ — feasible to $n = 6$ or $7$ — and it validates the closed
form of Theorem 8.2 and the approach to $1 - e^{-1}$ of Theorem 8.3.

A fourth, purely arithmetic routine computes the pitch-class triad: the diagonal
values are read off from the self-value table, and the boundary claim reduces to
checking $\gcd$-style conditions $2x \equiv 0$, $2x \equiv 6$, $1 \equiv 0$ mod
$12$.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What replaced what

The classical picture holds that self-reference arises when a system becomes
"sufficiently rich". Theorem 2.4 shows that the strongest reading of richness —
full universality — is not a demanding hypothesis but an inconsistent one over
any nontrivial semantics. What survives, and what all the classical arguments
actually use, is representability of a single diagonal composite. Theorem 3.6
shows this weaker hypothesis is not a compromise: it is *equivalent* to the
semantic condition that the endomorphism in question has a fixed point.

This relocates the entire subject. Capacity for self-reference is not a property
of a syntax; it is a property of the *semantic dynamics* the syntax is asked to
internalise. Any syntax whatsoever can host a loop for a map with a fixed point;
no syntax can host one for a map without.

### 10.2 Which invariant

The refutation of monoid control (Theorem 4.3) is the sharpest structural
finding. It rules out an entire family of plausible reformulations — orbit
structure, generated monoid, group-theoretic invariants — in favour of the
pointwise predicate "$f$ has a fixed point". The reason is a *resource* fact
about the diagonal argument: $f$ is applied once, so no closure is forced. One
can read this as an instance of a general principle: the strength of a
diagonalisation is bounded by how many times it consumes the semantic operator.

### 10.3 Canonicity

Section 5 separates two ambiguities that are easy to conflate. Transport along
evaluation-preserving equivalences is canonical (Theorem 6.2), so the choice of
*presentation* never matters. The choice of *code* does matter, and matters
exactly when the semantics has multiple fixed points (Theorems 5.1, 5.2). Where
the semantics is contracting, canonicity is restored and the diagonal value
is computable by iteration (Theorem 5.4). Guarded recursive definitions in
denotational semantics fit the same pattern, and are a natural place to look for
further instances.

### 10.4 Reading the density

Theorem 8.3 gives the subject an unexpected quantitative face. Asked "how much
self-reference is possible?", the answer over a large finite semantic domain is
$1 - e^{-1}$: neither everything nor a vanishing sliver, but a definite majority
with a definite, stable complement. The complement is where Gödel, Turing,
Tarski and the liar live; the theorem says they are not exceptional but
representative of a third of all semantic dynamics.

## 11. Future work

1. **Iterated self-application.** Extend diagonal representation to depth $k$,
   requiring a table to represent $x \mapsto f(\mathrm{ev}(x,\mathrm{ev}(x,x)))$
   and its higher analogues. The depth-$1$ argument consumes $f$ once, which is
   exactly why pointwise fixed points, and not the generated monoid, control
   representability; increasing the depth should trade fixed points for periodic
   points. Conjecture: depth-$k$ representability of $F$ holds iff every
   $f \in F$ has a point of period dividing $k$ under a suitable auxiliary
   transformation, so the spectrum stratifies into a strictly increasing chain
   measurable against the reference count $n^n - (n-1)^n$, strict for all
   $n \ge 3$.

2. **Density laws as invariants of structured semantics.** For finite semantic
   domains carrying extra structure (monoid actions, partial orders, metric
   quantisations), conjecture that the representable density converges to a
   structure-dependent constant that is a complete invariant separating the
   structures: monotone self-maps of a finite chain have density $1$, since
   every such map has a fixed point, whereas permutations have density
   $1 - e^{-1} + o(1)$ in the derangement sense. The unrestricted baseline is
   Theorem 8.3.

3. **Completeness of the coherence hierarchy.** We have proved four levels with
   separating counterexamples. Conjecture that these are the only four: that any
   evaluation-respecting relation between tables preserving diagonal data
   factors through one of equivalence, coherent retraction, embedding, or
   bisimulation, and that the associated preservation laws (codes and values;
   codes upward; values only; observations only) are exhaustive.

4. **Bounded-complexity fragments.** Grade representability by the syntactic,
   geometric, or musical complexity of the observation represented, and
   determine the minimal closure axioms under which diagonal fixed points
   persist. Theorem 4.5 shows richness and diagonal closure are independent
   axes; sharp finite counterexamples should exist when either closure condition
   is removed.

5. **Beyond sets.** The whole development is elementary and should lift to
   categories with finite products, partial maps, or enriched hom-objects. The
   expected statement: joint representability of a family of endomorphisms is
   equivalent to each member admitting a generalised point that it fixes, with
   the free graded model of Theorem 3.5 as the universal witness.

## 12. Conclusion

Full universality of a self-application table is impossible over any semantics
with two distinct meanings, and the obstruction is uniform rather than
Boolean-specific. Replacing it by representability of a single diagonal
composite recovers the whole diagonal mechanism and, remarkably, admits an exact
converse: a family of semantic endomorphisms is jointly representable precisely
when each member has a fixed point. This invariant is pointwise — not monoidal,
not cardinal — the diagonal value is canonical exactly under semantic
uniqueness, transport across presentations degrades in four controlled stages,
a nonvacuous formal/visual/contrapuntal triad over the twelve pitch classes
exists with common diagonal value $0$, and the representable share of an
$n$-element semantic domain is $n^n - (n-1)^n$, tending to $1 - e^{-1}$.

The braid is real. It is just narrower than advertised, and we can now measure
exactly how narrow.

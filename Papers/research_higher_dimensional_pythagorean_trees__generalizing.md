## Abstract

The Berggren tree organizes all primitive Pythagorean triples into an infinite ternary tree generated by three integral reflections of the Lorentz form $x_1^2 + x_2^2 - y^2$. We develop the general-$n$ theory of the analogous structure on the null cone of $x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 - y^2$, whose integer points are the primitive Pythagorean $n$-tuples.

To each null point we attach its **descent complex**: the family of sign patterns $\varepsilon \in \{\pm 1\}^n$, recorded by their minus-sets $S$, whose associated integral reflection strictly lowers the height. We prove that this family is always downward closed, hence an abstract simplicial complex on the coordinate set, and that the Pythagorean relation forces the dimension bound $\#S \le n-2$ for every face, with the strengthening that the union of two disjoint faces still satisfies $\#(S\cup T)^c \ge 2$.

Three consequences follow. (i) In dimension three at most one single sign flip descends, giving the **Exact Branching Theorem**: every Pythagorean quadruple with positive coordinates has exactly six or exactly seven children, the dichotomy being decided by the Egyptian-fraction inequality $\min\bigl(a(b+c)-bc,\; b(a+c)-ac,\; c(a+b)-ab\bigr) \le 0$; both values occur infinitely often, via the families $(1,2m,2m^2,2m^2+1)$ and $(2m,2m,2m^2-1,2m^2+1)$. Hence the higher graph is **not** a regular tree. (ii) Dually, every node in dimension $n$ has at least $n+1$ children, a bound attained exactly in the Berggren case $n=2$, which is therefore the minimally branching member of the family. (iii) In dimension four the bound $\#S \le n-2$ is sharp: a Pell family $(1,1,t,t\,;d)$ with $d^2 - 2t^2 = 2$ supplies arbitrarily large primitive quintuples whose descent complex has a two-element face.

On the metric side, one reflection multiplies the height by at most $\rho_n = (\sqrt n + 1)/(\sqrt n - 1)$. We show $\rho_2 = (1+\sqrt 2)^2 = 3+2\sqrt2$ and $\rho_3 = 2+\sqrt 3$ are norm-one units, that $2+\sqrt3$ is the fundamental unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt3]$, that $\rho_n$ strictly decreases to $1$, and that the critical exponent $\delta = \log(\text{branching})/\log \rho$ jumps across $1$ from $\delta_2 = \log 3/\log(3+2\sqrt2) \approx 0.6233$ to $\delta_3 \ge \log 6/\log(2+\sqrt3)\approx 1.3606$. Over the integers $\rho_3$ is an unattained supremum: $a+b+c < \sqrt3\,d$ strictly, by irrationality of $\sqrt3$.

Finally, height-neutral moves are exactly the fixed points of the generators — the **mirror nodes** — and they obey a divisor law: the mirror nodes for the first-coordinate reflection with given first coordinate $a$ are in bijection with the factorizations $pq = a^2$, so there are exactly $\tau(a^2)$ of them. This is the dimension-three replacement for the star-line and totient laws of the planar theory.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean quadruple, Berggren tree, integral Lorentz group, null cone, descent complex, simplicial complex, Pell equation, fundamental unit, critical exponent, hyperbolic growth.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The classical picture

A **primitive Pythagorean triple** is a triple $(a,b,c)$ of positive integers with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. Berggren (1934) observed that the three integer matrices
$$
U = \begin{pmatrix} 1&-2&2\\ 2&-1&2\\ 2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
A = \begin{pmatrix} 1&2&2\\ 2&1&2\\ 2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
D = \begin{pmatrix} -1&2&2\\ -2&1&2\\ -2&2&3\end{pmatrix}
$$
act on column vectors $(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}$, preserve the relation $a^2+b^2=c^2$, and generate from the root $(3,4,5)$ an infinite ternary tree containing every primitive triple exactly once. The tree has been rediscovered repeatedly; its regularity — three children, one parent, no repeats — is the reason it is remembered.

The three matrices are not arbitrary. Write the Lorentz form of signature $(n,1)$,
$$
Q_n(x_1,\dots,x_n,y) = x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2-y^2 ,
$$
and let $\mathcal{C}_n = \{Q_n = 0\}$ be its null cone. A primitive Pythagorean $n$-tuple is precisely a primitive integer point of $\mathcal{C}_n$ with positive coordinates. The matrices above lie in $\mathrm{O}(Q_2;\mathbb{Z})$; each is conjugate to a single reflection by a diagonal sign matrix. The ternary branching of the Berggren tree is a statement about how these reflections move the last coordinate.

### 1.2 The question

The obvious generalization replaces triples by $n$-tuples: solutions of
$$
x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2 = y^2 .
$$
For $n=3$ these are **Pythagorean quadruples** — integer edge-lengths of a rectangular box with integer space diagonal. The question this paper answers is:

> Does the Berggren tree generalize? Is there, for each $n \ge 3$, a finite generating set of integral Lorentz reflections producing a *regular* tree of primitive $n$-tuples, with a clean hyperbolic embedding and a computable metric growth exponent?

The answer is a precise mixture. The generating set exists and is finite ($2^n$ signed reflections, all involutions). The hyperbolic embedding exists, and the growth constant is an explicit quadratic unit. But the regularity fails, and it fails in a way that is completely computable: the branching number in dimension three is $6$ or $7$, decided by a harmonic inequality. The structure that survives and explains everything is the descent complex.

### 1.3 Results

Throughout, coordinates are non-negative integers and heights positive.

1. **Descent complex** (§3). The descending sign sets form a downward-closed family; every face $S$ satisfies $\#S \le n-2$; disjoint faces $S,T$ satisfy $\#(S\cup T)^c \ge 2$.
2. **Dimension three** (§4). At most one singleton descends; branching is exactly $6$ or $7$; the dichotomy is the weak harmonic law; both values occur infinitely often.
3. **Universal bound** (§5). Branching $\ge n+1$ in every dimension, with equality characterizing $n=2$.
4. **Dimension four** (§6). The face bound is sharp: a Pell family of primitive quintuples with two-element descent faces.
5. **Growth** (§7). $\rho_n = (\sqrt n+1)/(\sqrt n-1)$, closed forms and unit properties for $n=2,3$, strict monotonicity, limit $1$, critical exponent crossing $1$, and strictness over $\mathbb{Z}$.
6. **Mirrors** (§8). Neutral moves are fixed points; an infinite primitive mirror family; uniqueness of the stabilizing generator; the divisor law $\tau(a^2)$.

## 2. Setup: the null cone and its signed reflections

### 2.1 Pythagorean tuples

**Definition 2.1 (Pythagorean $n$-tuple).** A pair $(x; d)$ with $x = (x_1,\dots,x_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ and $d \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a *Pythagorean $n$-tuple* if
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2 = d^2 .
$$
It is *primitive* if $\gcd(x_1,\dots,x_n,d) = 1$, and *positive* if all $x_i > 0$ and $d>0$. For $n=2$ these are Pythagorean triples; for $n=3$, Pythagorean quadruples $a^2+b^2+c^2=d^2$.

**Definition 2.2 (Sign pattern and signed sum).** A *sign pattern* is $\varepsilon \in \{\pm 1\}^n$; we encode it by its minus-set $S = \{i : \varepsilon_i = -1\} \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$. The *signed sum* is
$$
\sigma_S(x) \;=\; \varepsilon \cdot x \;=\; \sum_{i \notin S} x_i - \sum_{i \in S} x_i .
$$

**Lemma 2.3 (Signed sum decomposition).** $\displaystyle \sigma_S(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i - 2\sum_{i \in S} x_i$. In particular $\sigma_\emptyset(x) = \sum_i x_i$.

*Proof.* Termwise, $-x_i = x_i - 2x_i$ for $i \in S$ and $x_i = x_i - 0$ otherwise; sum and split. $\square$

Elementary as it is, Lemma 2.3 is the workhorse: it converts every statement about sign patterns into a statement about the *sum of a subset of coordinates*, i.e. into combinatorics of the set $S$.

### 2.2 The signed reflections

**Definition 2.4.** For a sign pattern $\varepsilon$ define the *shift*
$$
k \;=\; k_\varepsilon(x,d) \;=\; \varepsilon \cdot x - d
$$
and the *signed reflection*
$$
R_\varepsilon(x; d) \;=\; \bigl(x_1 - \varepsilon_1 k,\ \dots,\ x_n - \varepsilon_n k;\ d - k\bigr).
$$

Concretely for $n=3$ one takes the single Lorentz reflection
$$
R = \begin{pmatrix} 0&-1&-1&1\\ -1&0&-1&1\\ -1&-1&0&1\\ -1&-1&-1&2 \end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{(acting on } (a,b,c,d)^{\mathsf T}\text{)}
$$
and conjugates it by the diagonal sign matrices $S_\varepsilon = \mathrm{diag}(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2,\varepsilon_3,1)$: the eight matrices $R_\varepsilon = S_\varepsilon R S_\varepsilon$ are the generators.

**Proposition 2.5 (Basic properties).** For every sign pattern $\varepsilon$:
1. $R_\varepsilon$ is an integral automorphism of the Lorentz form, hence maps Pythagorean tuples to Pythagorean tuples;
2. $R_\varepsilon$ is an involution, $R_\varepsilon^2 = I$, so the generated group is a quotient of a free product of copies of $\mathbb{Z}/2$;
3. $R_\varepsilon$ preserves the content $\gcd(x_1,\dots,x_n,d)$, hence preserves primitivity;
4. the height transforms by
$$
d \;\longmapsto\; d - k \;=\; 2d - \varepsilon\cdot x = 2d - \sigma_S(x).
$$

*Proof sketch.* (1) The sign matrices are orthogonal for the space part and fix the time coordinate, so they preserve $Q_n$; $R$ is a reflection in the vector $(1,\dots,1;n-1)$-type mirror and preserves $Q_n$ by direct computation; a product of form-preserving integer matrices is form-preserving. (2) $S_\varepsilon^2 = I$ and $R^2 = I$, so $(S_\varepsilon R S_\varepsilon)^2 = S_\varepsilon R^2 S_\varepsilon = I$; equivalently, applying the shift rule twice returns $k \mapsto -k \mapsto 0$. (3) Each new coordinate is an integer combination of the old ones and conversely, by (2). (4) Immediate from Definition 2.4. $\square$

Item (4) is the only thing that matters for the tree structure. It motivates:

**Definition 2.6 (Descent, neutrality, child).** Fix a positive Pythagorean tuple $(x;d)$ and a sign set $S$.
- $S$ **descends** if $2d - \sigma_S(x) < d$, i.e. $\sigma_S(x) > d$;
- $S$ is **neutral** if $\sigma_S(x) = d$ (equivalently $k=0$, equivalently $R_\varepsilon$ *fixes* the node);
- $S$ is a **child move** if $\sigma_S(x) < d$, so that the new height strictly exceeds $d$.

The **branching number** of a node is the number of child moves; the **descent complex** $\mathcal{D}(x,d)$ is the family of descending sign sets.

Because $\sigma_\emptyset(x) = \sum_i x_i \ge d$ always (indeed $(\sum x_i)^2 \ge \sum x_i^2 = d^2$ for non-negative coordinates), the all-plus pattern is never a child: every positive node has at least one parent-or-fixed direction. The whole theory now reduces to the question: *how large can $\mathcal{D}(x,d)$, together with the neutral sets, be?*

## 3. The descent complex

### 3.1 Downward closure

**Theorem 3.1 (Downward Closure).** Let $x$ have non-negative coordinates. If $T$ descends and $S \subseteq T$, then $S$ descends. Hence $\mathcal{D}(x,d)$ is an abstract simplicial complex on $\{1,\dots,n\}$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2.3, descending means $\sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in T}x_i > d$. Since $S \subseteq T$ and coordinates are non-negative, $\sum_{i \in S} x_i \le \sum_{i\in T} x_i$, so $\sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in S}x_i \ge \sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in T}x_i > d$. $\square$

Thus descent is *monotone downward* in the minus-set: adding minus signs makes descent harder. Every descent complex therefore consists of "small" sets, and the natural invariant is its dimension.

### 3.2 The dimension bound

**Lemma 3.2 (Coordinate bound).** For a Pythagorean tuple with $d \ge 0$ and any index $j$, $x_j \le d$.

*Proof.* $x_j^2 \le \sum_i x_i^2 = d^2$ since all summands are non-negative; take square roots using $d \ge 0$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.3 (Two-index lemma).** Let $(x;d)$ be a Pythagorean tuple with $d \ge 0$, and let $T \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}$ satisfy $\sum_{i\in T} x_i > d$. Then $\#T \ge 2$.

*Proof.* If $T = \emptyset$ the sum is $0 \le d$, contradiction. If $T = \{j\}$ the sum is $x_j \le d$ by Lemma 3.2, contradiction. $\square$

This is the entire arithmetic input of the combinatorial half of the paper: *a set of coordinates whose sum exceeds the height must have at least two elements*, which is nothing but "no leg exceeds the hypotenuse" plus positivity.

**Theorem 3.4 (Dimension Bound).** Let $(x;d)$ be a Pythagorean tuple with $x_i \ge 0$ and $d\ge 0$. Every face $S \in \mathcal{D}(x,d)$ satisfies
$$
\#S^c \ge 2, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \#S \le n-2 .
$$

*Proof.* Split $\sum_i x_i = \sum_{i\in S} x_i + \sum_{i \in S^c} x_i$. Descent says $\sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in S}x_i > d$, i.e.
$$
\sum_{i\in S^c} x_i - \sum_{i \in S} x_i > d,
$$
and since $\sum_{i\in S} x_i \ge 0$ we get $\sum_{i \in S^c} x_i > d$. Apply Lemma 3.3 to $T = S^c$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.5 (Disjoint Faces).** Let $(x;d)$ be a Pythagorean tuple with $d \ge 0$, and let $S, T$ be *disjoint* faces of $\mathcal{D}(x,d)$. Then $\#(S \cup T)^c \ge 2$.

*Proof.* Descent for $S$ and for $T$ read, after Lemma 2.3,
$$
\textstyle\sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in S} x_i > d, \qquad \sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i\in T} x_i > d .
$$
Adding and using disjointness, $\sum_{i\in S\cup T} x_i = \sum_{i \in S}x_i + \sum_{i\in T}x_i$, so
$$
2\sum_i x_i - 2\sum_{i \in S\cup T} x_i > 2d, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \sum_{i \in (S\cup T)^c} x_i > d .
$$
Lemma 3.3 finishes. $\square$

Theorem 3.5 is strictly stronger than applying Theorem 3.4 to $S$ and $T$ separately: it bounds the *union*, which is generally not a face.

**Remark 3.6.** In dimension two, Theorem 3.4 allows only $S=\emptyset$; the descent complex is a single point (or empty). This is exactly the source of Berggren regularity. In dimension three it allows $\emptyset$ and singletons. In dimension four, edges become possible — and §6 shows they occur.

## 4. Dimension three: exact branching

### 4.1 Uniqueness of the descending flip

**Theorem 4.1 (Unique Descent).** Let $(x;d)$ be a Pythagorean quadruple ($n=3$) with $d \ge 0$, and suppose $\{i\}$ and $\{j\}$ both descend. Then $i=j$.

*Proof.* If $i \ne j$, the singletons are disjoint faces, so by Theorem 3.5 $\#(\{i,j\})^c \ge 2$. But $\#\{i,j\} = 2$ and $n=3$, so $\#(\{i,j\})^c = 1$. Contradiction. $\square$

Thus for a quadruple the descent complex is contained in $\{\emptyset\} \cup \{\{i\}\}$ for a single $i$: it is a point, or a point plus one vertex.

### 4.2 The weak harmonic law

**Definition 4.2 (Weak defect).** A positive quadruple $(a,b,c,d)$ has a *weak defect* if some single flip fails to raise the height:
$$
d \le -a+b+c \quad\text{or}\quad d \le a-b+c \quad\text{or}\quad d \le a+b-c .
$$

**Theorem 4.3 (Egyptian-fraction form).** For a positive Pythagorean quadruple $(a,b,c,d)$,
$$
\text{weak defect} \iff \bigl(a(b+c) \le bc\bigr) \ \text{or}\ \bigl(b(a+c) \le ac\bigr)\ \text{or}\ \bigl(c(a+b) \le ab\bigr),
$$
i.e. iff $\frac1b + \frac1c \le \frac1a$, or $\frac1a+\frac1c \le \frac1b$, or $\frac1a+\frac1b \le \frac1c$.

*Proof sketch.* Take the first alternative. If $d \le -a+b+c$, then squaring and using $a^2+b^2+c^2 = d^2$:
$$
d^2 \le (b+c-a)^2 = a^2+b^2+c^2 + 2bc - 2ab-2ac = d^2 + 2\bigl(bc - a(b+c)\bigr),
$$
whence $a(b+c) \le bc$. Conversely, if $a(b+c) \le bc$ then $b+c-a > 0$ (indeed $a \le bc/(b+c) < \min(b,c)$), and the same identity gives $(b+c-a)^2 \ge d^2$; taking positive square roots yields $d \le b+c-a$. The other two alternatives are the same computation with the roles of $a,b,c$ permuted. $\square$

The equivalence is worth pausing over: a *linear* condition on the coordinates (which reflection descends) is equivalent to a *reciprocal* condition (an Egyptian-fraction inequality). The Pythagorean relation is exactly what converts one into the other.

### 4.3 The branching theorem

**Theorem 4.4 (Exact Branching, $n=3$).** Let $(a,b,c,d)$ be a positive Pythagorean quadruple. Of the eight sign patterns,
$$
\#\{\text{children}\} = \begin{cases} 6 & \text{if } (a,b,c,d) \text{ has a weak defect},\\ 7 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$

*Proof.* Children and non-children partition the eight patterns, so it suffices to show that the number of non-children is $2$ in the defect case and $1$ otherwise.

The all-plus pattern is always a non-child: $a+b+c \ge d$ because $(a+b+c)^2 \ge a^2+b^2+c^2 = d^2$.

Next, any pattern with two or three minus signs is a child. Indeed, for $\#S \ge 2$ we have $\#S^c \le 1$, so by Lemma 3.3 $\sum_{i\in S^c} x_i \le d$, hence
$$
\sigma_S(x) = \sum_{i \in S^c} x_i - \sum_{i\in S} x_i \le d - \sum_{i \in S}x_i < d
$$
using positivity of the coordinates. So all four patterns with $\ge 2$ minus signs raise the height.

It remains to count the three single flips. A single flip is a non-child exactly when $d \le \sigma_{\{i\}}(x)$, which is the corresponding clause of the weak defect. If no clause holds there are no further non-children and the count is $8-1 = 7$. If some clause holds, at least one single flip is a non-child; and *at most* one is, because two distinct single flips that are both non-children would each satisfy $d \le \sigma_{\{i\}}$, and the strict-inequality argument of Theorem 3.5 applies verbatim with $\le$ in place of $<$ (adding the two inequalities gives $\sum_{k \notin \{i,j\}} x_k \ge d$ with the two flipped coordinates subtracted, forcing a contradiction with positivity in dimension three). Hence exactly two non-children and $8-2=6$ children. $\square$

**Corollary 4.5 (Not a regular tree).** The Pythagorean quadruple graph is not vertex-regular: both branching numbers occur, and each occurs infinitely often.

**Theorem 4.6 (Six-child family).** For $m \ge 2$, the quadruple $(1,\,2m,\,2m^2,\,2m^2+1)$ is Pythagorean, and has exactly six children.

*Proof.* $1 + 4m^2 + 4m^4 = (2m^2+1)^2$. The weak defect holds in the first clause: $2m^2+1 \le -1 + 2m + 2m^2 \iff 2 \le 2m$. Apply Theorem 4.4. $\square$

**Theorem 4.7 (Seven-child family).** For $m \ge 2$, the quadruple $(2m,\,2m,\,2m^2-1,\,2m^2+1)$ is a *primitive* Pythagorean quadruple with exactly seven children.

*Proof.* Pythagorean: $4m^2+4m^2+(2m^2-1)^2 = 4m^4+4m^2+1 = (2m^2+1)^2$. Primitive: a common divisor $g$ of the last two coordinates divides their difference $2$; but $2m^2-1$ is odd, so $g = 1$. No weak defect: each clause fails, e.g. $-2m+2m+2m^2-1 = 2m^2-1 < 2m^2+1 = d$, and $2m+2m-(2m^2-1) = 4m-2m^2+1 < 2m^2+1$ for $m\ge2$. Apply Theorem 4.4. $\square$

Numerically: $m=2$ gives $(4,4,7,9)$ with seven children, and $(1,4,8,9)$ with six.

## 5. A universal branching bound

**Theorem 5.1 (Universal Branching Bound).** Let $n \ge 2$ and let $(x;d)$ be a Pythagorean $n$-tuple with all $x_i > 0$ and $d \ge 0$. Then the node has at least $n+1$ children; explicitly, every sign set $S$ with $\#S^c \le 1$ is a child move.

*Proof.* Let $\#S^c \le 1$. By Lemma 3.3 (contrapositive), $\sum_{i \in S^c} x_i \le d$. Then
$$
\sigma_S(x) = \sum_{i\in S^c} x_i - \sum_{i \in S} x_i \le d - \sum_{i\in S} x_i < d
$$
because $S \ne \emptyset$ (as $n \ge 2$ forces $\#S \ge n-1 \ge 1$) and coordinates are positive. There are exactly $n+1$ sets with $\#S^c \le 1$: the full set and the $n$ complements of singletons. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (Berggren minimality).** For $n=2$ the bound gives $3$, and by the argument of §4 (with $n=2$: only $S=\emptyset$ can fail to be a child) the branching number is exactly $3$. Thus the Berggren tree realizes the minimum possible branching in the family, and it does so precisely because the dimension bound leaves room only for the empty face.

For $n=3$ the universal bound gives $4$, well below the true value $6$; the gap measures how much extra structure Theorem 4.1 provides.

## 6. Dimension four: sharpness via Pell's equation

Theorem 3.4 permits faces of size up to $n-2$. Is it sharp? For $n=3$ it is (singletons occur, e.g. in the six-child family). For $n=4$ the question is whether a *two-element* face — a parent obtained by flipping two signs simultaneously — can occur. It can, infinitely often.

**Definition 6.1 (Pell family).** Let $\pi(t,d) = (3t+2d,\ 4t+3d)$ and define $(t_0,d_0) = (1,2)$, $(t_{k+1},d_{k+1}) = \pi(t_k,d_k)$. Attach to each the quintuple $Q_t = (1,1,t,t\,;\,d)$.

**Lemma 6.2 (Pell invariance).** $d_k^2 - 2t_k^2 = 2$ for all $k$, and $d_k \ge k+2$, $t_k \ge 1$; moreover $t_k \ge 7$ for $k \ge 1$.

*Proof.* Invariance: $(4t+3d)^2 - 2(3t+2d)^2 = 16t^2+24td+9d^2-18t^2-24td-8d^2 = d^2-2t^2$. The base point gives $4-2 = 2$. Growth: from $t\ge1, d\ge2$ one gets $t' = 3t+2d \ge 7$ and $d' = 4t+3d \ge d+1$; induct. $\square$

The first solutions are $(t,d) = (1,2),\ (7,10),\ (41,58),\ (239,338),\ (1393,1970)$.

**Lemma 6.3.** If $d^2 = 2t^2+2$ then $Q_t$ is a Pythagorean $4$-tuple: $1+1+t^2+t^2 = d^2$. It is primitive (it has a coordinate equal to $1$), and its coordinates are non-negative for $t\ge0$.

**Theorem 6.4 (Two-element face).** Let $t \ge 4$, $d>0$ with $d^2 = 2t^2+2$. Then $S = \{1,2\}$ is a face of the descent complex of $Q_t$.

*Proof.* $\sigma_S(Q_t) = -1-1+t+t = 2t-2$, and descent means $2t-2 > d$. Both sides positive, so square: $(2t-2)^2 > d^2 = 2t^2+2 \iff 4t^2-8t+4 > 2t^2+2 \iff 2(t^2-4t+1) > 0$, true for $t \ge 4$ since $t^2-4t+1 = (t-2)^2-3 \ge 1$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5 (Sharpness in dimension four).** For every $N$ there is a primitive Pythagorean quintuple with height $> N$ whose descent complex contains a face of size $2 = n-2$. Consequently the bound $\#S \le n-2$ of Theorem 3.4 is attained for $n=4$.

*Proof.* Take $k$ large so that $d_k > N$ and $t_k \ge 7 \ge 4$ (Lemma 6.2) and apply Lemma 6.3 and Theorem 6.4. $\square$

**Remark 6.6.** Two-element faces are impossible for $n=3$ (Theorem 4.1). So the descent complex is genuinely a *dimension-dependent* invariant: empty-or-a-point for triples, at most a vertex for quadruples, containing edges for quintuples. The higher trees have parents of increasing codimension.

The mechanism generalizes: putting $n-2$ coordinates equal to $1$ and two equal to $t$ turns the face condition into the pair "$d^2 - 2t^2 = n-2$ and $2t-(n-2)>d$", so sharpness in dimension $n$ becomes a representability question for the form $x^2-2y^2$. For $n=4$ the target is $2$, represented; for $n=5$ the target $3$ is *not* represented by $x^2-2y^2$ (a congruence obstruction mod 8), so a different family is needed. This is the content of Conjecture A in §10.

## 7. Metric growth: hyperbolic geometry of the moves

### 7.1 The growth constant

Projectivizing the null cone $\mathcal{C}_n \subset \mathbb{R}^{n,1}$ produces the boundary sphere $S^{n-1}$ of the hyperbolic ball $\mathbb{H}^n$; the group $\mathrm{O}(Q_n;\mathbb{Z})$ acts by isometries, and the height $d$ of a normalized null vector behaves as an exponentiated Busemann function. A reflection move therefore corresponds to a hyperbolic translation whose displacement is $\log(d'/d)$ for the height ratio $d'/d$. The relevant sharp constant is:

**Definition 7.1.** $\displaystyle \rho_n = \frac{\sqrt n + 1}{\sqrt n - 1}$ for $n \ge 2$.

The bound $d' \le \rho_n d$ for a single move comes from Cauchy–Schwarz: $d' = 2d - \varepsilon \cdot x$ with $|\varepsilon\cdot x| \le \sqrt n \sqrt{\sum x_i^2} = \sqrt n\, d$, so $d' \le (2+\sqrt n)d$ — and after normalizing the move to the descending/ascending regime and optimizing, the sharp ratio is $\rho_n$, attained on the real cone at the symmetric point $x_1=\cdots=x_n=d/\sqrt n$.

**Theorem 7.2 (Closed forms).**
$$
\rho_2 = 3 + 2\sqrt 2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2, \qquad \rho_3 = 2+\sqrt3 .
$$

*Proof.* $\frac{\sqrt2+1}{\sqrt2-1} = (\sqrt2+1)^2 = 3+2\sqrt2$; $\frac{\sqrt3+1}{\sqrt3-1} = \frac{(\sqrt3+1)^2}{2} = \frac{4+2\sqrt3}{2} = 2+\sqrt3$. $\square$

Thus the dimension-two constant is the *square of the silver ratio* $1+\sqrt2$, the classical growth constant of the Berggren tree.

**Theorem 7.3 (Unit property).** $(2+\sqrt3)(2-\sqrt3) = 1$ and $(3+2\sqrt2)(3-2\sqrt2) = 1$: both $\rho_2$ and $\rho_3$ are units of norm $1$ in the real quadratic orders $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt3]$.

**Theorem 7.4 (Fundamental unit).** $2+\sqrt3$ is the smallest unit $>1$ with positive integer coordinates in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt3]$: if $a,b$ are positive integers with $a^2-3b^2 = 1$ then $a+b\sqrt3 \ge 2+\sqrt3$.

*Proof sketch.* If $b \ge 1$ then $a^2 = 3b^2+1 \ge 4$, so $a \ge 2$, and $a+b\sqrt3 \ge 2+\sqrt3$. Minimality is thus immediate from the Pell equation $a^2-3b^2=1$ having least positive solution $(2,1)$. $\square$

So $\log \rho_3 = \log(2+\sqrt3)$ is the exact structural analogue for quadruples of the silver-ratio exponent $\log(1+\sqrt2)$ for triples: in both cases the per-move hyperbolic displacement is the logarithm of a fundamental unit of a real quadratic order.

**Theorem 7.5 (Monotonicity and limit).** $\rho_n = 1 + \dfrac{2}{\sqrt n - 1}$ is strictly decreasing in $n \ge 2$, and $\rho_n \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$.

*Proof.* The identity is immediate; $\sqrt n - 1$ strictly increases to $\infty$. $\square$

Each individual reflection therefore becomes *less* efficient at moving a node in higher dimensions.

### 7.2 The critical exponent

**Definition 7.6.** For a graph whose nodes have $k$ children and whose one-move height ratio is $\rho$, the *critical exponent* is the number $\delta$ with $k = \rho^\delta$:
$$
\delta(k,\rho) = \frac{\log k}{\log \rho}.
$$
It counts nodes per unit of hyperbolic displacement, and is the discrete-group invariant governing the convergence abscissa of the Poincaré series (and, for a genuinely discrete group, the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set).

**Theorem 7.7 (Growth exponent jump).**
$$
\delta_2 = \frac{\log 3}{\log(3+2\sqrt2)} < 1 < \frac{\log 6}{\log(2+\sqrt3)} \le \delta_3 ,
$$
and hence $\delta_2 < \delta_3$.

*Proof.* $3+2\sqrt2 > 3$, so $\log(3+2\sqrt2) > \log 3 > 0$, giving $\delta_2 < 1$. And $2+\sqrt3 < 6$ with $2+\sqrt3>1$, so $0 < \log(2+\sqrt3) < \log 6$, giving $\log 6/\log(2+\sqrt3) > 1$. $\square$

Numerically $\delta_2 \approx 1.0986/1.7627 \approx 0.6233$ and $\log6/\log(2+\sqrt3) \approx 1.7918/1.3170 \approx 1.3606$.

**Interpretation.** Two effects compete. Higher dimension *slows* each move ($\rho_3 = 2+\sqrt3 \approx 3.732 < 5.828 \approx \rho_2$) but *multiplies* the branching ($6$ or $7$ versus $3$). Theorem 7.7 says branching wins decisively: the exponent more than doubles and crosses the threshold $1$.

### 7.3 Strictness over the integers

The constant $\rho_3$ is sharp on the *real* cone. On the integer tree it is never attained.

**Theorem 7.8 (Strict integral growth).** Let $(a,b,c,d)$ be an integral Pythagorean quadruple with $a,b,c \ge 0$ and $d>0$. Then
$$
a+b+c < \sqrt3\, d ,
$$
and consequently every reflection move satisfies $d' < (2+\sqrt3)\,d$.

*Proof.* Cauchy–Schwarz gives $(a+b+c)^2 \le 3(a^2+b^2+c^2) = 3d^2$, so $a+b+c\le\sqrt3 d$ with equality iff $a=b=c$. If $a=b=c$ then $3a^2 = d^2$; for $a \ne 0$ this makes $\sqrt3 = |d|/|a|$ rational, contradicting the irrationality of $\sqrt3$; and $a=0$ forces $d=0$, excluded. Hence the inequality is strict, and $d' = 2d - \varepsilon\cdot x \le 2d + (a+b+c) < (2+\sqrt3)d$. $\square$

So the sharp real constant is an **unattained supremum** on the arithmetic tree: the integral nodes approach the extremal ray $(1,1,1;\sqrt3)$ but never reach it, precisely because that ray is irrational.

## 8. Mirror nodes and the divisor law

### 8.1 Neutral moves are fixed points

**Theorem 8.1 (Mirror characterization).** For a sign pattern $\varepsilon$ and a node $(x;d)$, the generator $R_\varepsilon$ fixes $(x;d)$ if and only if $\varepsilon \cdot x = d$, i.e. iff the move is height-neutral.

*Proof.* The shift is $k = \varepsilon\cdot x - d$ and the action is $(x_i - \varepsilon_i k;\ d-k)$. If $k=0$ this is the identity. Conversely, equality in the last coordinate forces $d - k = d$, i.e. $k=0$. $\square$

Geometrically: the neutral nodes are the lattice points on the mirror hyperplanes of the reflection group. In dimension two there are none (for a triple with positive legs, $\varepsilon\cdot x = c$ is impossible), and the group acts freely on the tree — this is the second half of Berggren regularity. In dimension three, mirrors carry infinitely many lattice points.

**Theorem 8.2 (An infinite primitive mirror family).** For every integer $m \ge 1$,
$$
\bigl(m,\ m+1,\ m(m+1),\ m^2+m+1\bigr)
$$
is a *primitive* Pythagorean quadruple, fixed by the generator with a minus sign on the first coordinate, and it has exactly six children. The case $m=1$ is $(1,2,2,3)$.

*Proof.* Pythagorean: $m^2 + (m+1)^2 + m^2(m+1)^2 = m^4+2m^3+3m^2+2m+1 = (m^2+m+1)^2$. Primitive: a common divisor of $m$ and $m+1$ divides $1$. Neutral: $-m + (m+1) + m(m+1) = m^2+m+1$, so Theorem 8.1 applies. Six children: the weak defect clause $d \le -a+b+c$ holds with equality, so by Theorem 4.4 the count is $6$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (Uniqueness of the stabilizing generator).** Let $(a,b,c,d)$ be a positive Pythagorean quadruple. Apart from the all-plus generator, at most one generator fixes it. Consequently each stabilizer in the reflection group is trivial or cyclic of order two.

*Proof.* A fixing generator is a non-child; by the proof of Theorem 4.4 there are at most two non-children, one of which is the all-plus pattern. $\square$

**Corollary 8.4.** Mirror nodes are always of the deficient (six-child) type; the maximal branching number $7$ occurs only off the mirrors.

### 8.2 The divisor law

Fix the generator that flips the first coordinate. Its mirror condition is $-a+b+c = d$.

**Theorem 8.5 (Factorization bijection).**
1. For any integers $a,p,q$ with $pq = a^2$, the quadruple $(a,\ a+p,\ a+q,\ a+p+q)$ is Pythagorean and satisfies the mirror condition $-a + (a+p) + (a+q) = a+p+q$.
2. Conversely, if $(a,b,c,d)$ is a positive Pythagorean quadruple with $-a+b+c=d$, then $p := b-a$ and $q := c-a$ are positive, satisfy $pq = a^2$, and $d = a+p+q$.

*Proof.* (1) Expand: $a^2 + (a+p)^2 + (a+q)^2 - (a+p+q)^2 = 2a^2 - 2pq = 0$. (2) Substituting $d = b+c-a$ into $a^2+b^2+c^2=d^2$ gives $a^2+b^2+c^2 = b^2+c^2+a^2+2bc-2ab-2ac$, i.e. $bc = a(b+c)$; hence $(b-a)(c-a) = bc - a(b+c) + a^2 = a^2$. Positivity: $bc = a(b+c) > ab$ gives $c>a$, similarly $b>a$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.6 (Divisor Law).** For each positive integer $a$, the number of mirror nodes (for the first-coordinate reflection) with first coordinate $a$ equals
$$
\tau(a^2) = \#\{\text{divisors of } a^2\}.
$$

*Proof.* By Theorem 8.5 the map $(a,b,c,d) \mapsto p = b-a$ is a bijection from such mirror nodes to positive divisors $p \mid a^2$, with inverse $p \mapsto (a,\ a+p,\ a+a^2/p,\ a+p+a^2/p)$. $\square$

**Examples 8.7.** $\tau(4)=3$: the mirror nodes over $a=2$ are $(2,3,6,7)$, $(2,4,4,6)$, $(2,6,3,7)$; the first is primitive. $\tau(9) = 3$ over $a=3$; $\tau(36)=9$ over $a=6$. Since $a^2$ is a square, $\tau(a^2)$ is always odd; the unpaired divisor $p=q=a$ gives the symmetric mirror node $(a,2a,2a,3a)$.

This divisor law is the higher-dimensional replacement for the classical arithmetic ("totient") counting laws attached to star-lines of the planar Berggren theory: where the plane counts with $\varphi$, space counts with $\tau$.

## 9. Algorithms

Three routines follow directly from the theory and are all we need to explore the graph computationally.

**(A) Branching classifier.** Given a positive quadruple, compute the three quantities $-a+b+c$, $a-b+c$, $a+b-c$ and compare each with $d$; return $6$ if any is $\ge d$, else $7$. Cost: $O(1)$ arithmetic operations. Correctness: Theorem 4.4. Equivalently, test $\min(bc - a(b+c),\ ac-b(a+c),\ ab-c(a+b)) \ge 0$ (Theorem 4.3).

**(B) Child enumeration / breadth-first exploration.** For each of the $2^n$ sign patterns compute $\sigma = \varepsilon\cdot x$, keep those with $\sigma < d$, and emit $R_\varepsilon(x;d) = (x_i - \varepsilon_i(\sigma - d);\ 2d-\sigma)$. Cost: $O(2^n n)$ per node. Every emitted node is a Pythagorean $n$-tuple of strictly greater height (Proposition 2.5), so a BFS by height terminates on any height cutoff.

**(C) Descent-complex computation and face profile.** For each subset $S$, test $\sigma_S(x) > d$; collect the faces; report the $f$-vector. Theorem 3.1 lets one prune: once a set fails, so do all supersets — reducing the search from $2^n$ to a traversal of the complex plus its minimal non-faces.

**(D) Pell ladder.** Iterate $(t,d) \mapsto (3t+2d, 4t+3d)$ from $(1,2)$ to generate the quintuples of §6; each step is $O(1)$ big-integer operations and the height grows geometrically with ratio $3+2\sqrt2$.

## 10. Discussion, applications, and future work

### 10.1 What generalizes and what does not

The naive hypothesis — a regular tree in every dimension — is false, and Theorem 4.4 says exactly how. What replaces regularity is a *combinatorial invariant of each node*, the descent complex, whose dimension is capped by the Pythagorean relation itself. In this language:

- $n=2$: descent complex $= \{\emptyset\}$, no neutral patterns, branching exactly $3$: a regular ternary tree, minimal by Theorem 5.1.
- $n=3$: descent complex $\subseteq \{\emptyset, \{i\}\}$; branching $6$ or $7$; neutral nodes exist and obey a divisor law.
- $n=4$: two-element faces occur infinitely often (Theorem 6.5); branching $\ge 5$ by Theorem 5.1, presumably ranging over an interval.

The transition $n=2 \to n=3$ is where the "tree" becomes a graph with a nontrivial local invariant; the transition $n=3 \to n=4$ is where the complex acquires positive dimension.

### 10.2 Applications

*Enumeration.* The moves give a certified generation procedure for primitive Pythagorean $n$-tuples: primitivity is preserved (Proposition 2.5(3)), heights strictly increase along child moves, and the exponential rate is quantified by §7. Any enumeration up to height $H$ visits $\asymp H^{\delta}$ nodes with $\delta$ as in Theorem 7.7.

*Geometry of numbers and sphere problems.* Pythagorean quadruples index integral points on spheres of square radius; the mirror family and the divisor law give explicit dense families of such points, useful as test configurations in kissing-number and lattice-covering computations.

*Hyperbolic dynamics.* The critical exponent $\delta$ is precisely the invariant governing the Poincaré series of the reflection group acting on $\mathbb{H}^n$; the jump across $1$ separates a "thin" regime ($n=2$) from a "thick" one ($n \ge 3$).

*Quadratic units.* That the per-move displacement is $\log$ of a fundamental unit ($1+\sqrt2$ for $n=2$, $2+\sqrt3$ for $n=3$) ties the metric growth of these trees to class-field-theoretic data of real quadratic orders — a link worth pushing further.

### 10.3 Future directions

**Conjecture A (sharpness of the face bound in every dimension).** For every $n \ge 3$ there exist infinitely many primitive Pythagorean $n$-tuples whose descent complex contains a face of size exactly $n-2$.

The key insight is that a face of size $n-2$ is a solution of a generalized Pell system: taking the two "large" coordinates equal to $t$ and the $n-2$ small ones equal to $1$ reduces the condition to $d^2 - 2t^2 = n-2$ together with $2t-(n-2) > d$, so sharpness in dimension $n$ is equivalent to representability of $n-2$ by the form $x^2-2y^2$ (or by an alternative shape when that fails, e.g. $n=5$, where $x^2-2y^2 = 3$ is insoluble). The case $n=4$ is a theorem via exactly this mechanism (§6), and the obstruction for the remaining $n$ is a completely explicit congruence condition; a case analysis modulo $8$ plus a second family should settle it.

**Conjecture B (the exact branching spectrum in dimension $n$).** For each $n$ the set of branching numbers realized by nodes with positive coordinates is exactly $\{2^n - k\}$, where $k$ ranges over the possible sizes of the "non-child" family, and this family is always a simplicial complex whose face numbers are governed by harmonic inequalities of the shape $x_i\bigl(\sum_{j \ne i} x_j - x_i\bigr) \le \cdots$. For $n=3$ this gives $\{6,7\}$; the conjecture is that for $n=4$ it is exactly $\{11,12,13,14,15\}$.

The key insight is that non-children are precisely the faces of the *weak* descent complex, so the branching number equals $2^n$ minus the number of faces, and the exact spectrum is a statement about which downward-closed families are realizable by null vectors.

**Further problems.**
1. *Completeness.* Does the reflection graph, started at $(1,2,2,3)$, reach every primitive quadruple? (For triples this is classical.) The height-strict-descent argument suggests yes; a full proof needs a well-founded descent to a finite set of roots.
2. *Uniqueness of parents.* In dimension three a node has one or two non-children; when it has two, the graph has a genuine cycle structure rather than a tree. Quantify the density of two-parent nodes.
3. *Exact $\delta_3$.* Theorem 7.7 bounds the critical exponent below by $\log 6/\log(2+\sqrt3)$; determining it exactly requires the *average* branching along the graph, weighted by the density of six- versus seven-child nodes — an equidistribution question for the harmonic inequality.
4. *Higher mirrors.* Compute the analogue of the divisor law in dimension $n \ge 4$: the neutral condition $\varepsilon\cdot x = d$ becomes a quadratic form identity whose solution count should be a multiplicative arithmetic function of the fixed coordinates.

## Abstract

A finite citation corpus naturally defines a hypergraph whose vertices are mathematical theorems and whose hyperedges are the theorem sets jointly cited by individual documents. We study the downward-closed simplicial complex generated by this hypergraph and compare it with the clique complex of the pairwise co-citation graph. Every genuine co-citation face becomes a graph clique, but the converse can fail because different pairs may have different witnessing documents. We prove an exact conformality criterion: pairwise co-citation reconstructs the full higher-order complex if and only if every clique has a common document witness. A three-vertex corpus shows that this condition is necessary and that pairwise projection can fill a nonexistent triangle, thereby destroying first homology. We prove that nested corpora induce simplicial filtrations and hence support persistent-homology analysis. For a corpus on $n$ theorems, the number of $k$-faces and every $k$th Betti number are bounded by $\binom{n}{k+1}$, and therefore by $n^{k+1}$. Moreover, all homology in dimensions $k\ge n$ vanishes. Thus $n^{k+1}$ is a universal coarse ceiling, not an asymptotic equivalence; an unrestricted positive power law is impossible. We conclude by separating structural topology from semantic claims about research communities and paradigm shifts, and we give algorithms for constructing complexes, diagnosing nonconformality, and computing homology from boundary ranks.

## 1. Introduction

Networks of mathematical dependence and citation are often represented as graphs. This representation captures pairwise relations but suppresses an essential fact: one document may cite several theorems simultaneously. Joint citation is a higher-order relation. A triangle in a pairwise graph can arise either because one document cites all three theorems or because three separate documents cite the three pairs. These situations have the same graph and different higher-dimensional topology.

The appropriate primitive object is therefore a finite hypergraph, or equivalently a finite family of finite citation sets. Its downward closure is a simplicial complex. This construction records a set of theorems as a face exactly when one document witnesses their joint occurrence. It also supports temporal analysis: as documents accumulate, faces can be added but never removed, producing a filtration.

This paper establishes four structural conclusions. First, the genuine complex is always contained in the clique complex formed from pairwise co-citation. Second, equality holds precisely under a common-witness condition called conformality. Third, corpus inclusion induces complex inclusion. Fourth, face counts and Betti numbers obey universal binomial bounds and vanish above the ambient dimension.

These conclusions refine two tempting interpretations. The inequality $\beta_k\le n^{k+1}$ does not imply $\beta_k\asymp n^{k+1}$; boundary ranks may reduce homology drastically, and $\beta_k=0$ for $k\ge n$. Likewise, first or second homology records incidence structure but does not intrinsically encode “schools” or “paradigm shifts.” Such labels require metadata or a generative model.

Throughout, $V$ is a finite set of theorems with $n=|V|$, and homology is taken over an arbitrary fixed field $\mathbb F$.

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1. Corpora and generated complexes

**Definition 2.1 (Co-citation corpus).** A corpus on $V$ is a finite family

$$
C=\{W_1,\ldots,W_m\},\qquad W_i\subseteq V,
$$

where $W_i$ is the set of theorems cited by the $i$th document. Repeated identical citation sets may be merged because multiplicity does not affect the underlying complex.

**Definition 2.2 (Co-citation complex).** The complex generated by $C$ is

$$
K(C)=\{S\subseteq V: \exists W\in C\text{ such that }S\subseteq W\}.
$$

A member $S$ of cardinality $k+1$ is a $k$-dimensional simplex, or $k$-face.

**Lemma 2.3 (Downward closure).** If $S\in K(C)$ and $T\subseteq S$, then $T\in K(C)$.

**Proof sketch.** Choose $W\in C$ with $S\subseteq W$. Then $T\subseteq S\subseteq W$, so the same document witnesses $T\in K(C)$. Thus $K(C)$ is a simplicial complex. $\square$

The empty set belongs to $K(C)$ whenever the corpus contains at least one document. Isolated vertices appear only when some document cites them; one may alternatively include all vertices by convention without affecting the higher-dimensional arguments.

### 2.2. Pairwise projection and flag completion

**Definition 2.4 (Pairwise co-citation graph).** The graph $G(C)$ has vertex set $V$. Distinct vertices $x,y\in V$ are adjacent when

$$
\exists W\in C\quad x\in W\text{ and }y\in W.
$$

This graph is the two-section of the citation hypergraph.

**Definition 2.5 (Clique complex).** For a graph $G$ on $V$, its clique complex $\mathrm{Cl}(G)$ consists of all finite sets $S\subseteq V$ such that every two distinct vertices in $S$ are adjacent. This construction is also called flag completion.

**Definition 2.6 (Conformal corpus).** A corpus $C$ is conformal when every clique in $G(C)$ has a common document witness:

$$
\forall S\subseteq V,\quad S\in\mathrm{Cl}(G(C))
\Longrightarrow \exists W\in C\text{ with }S\subseteq W.
$$

Conformality is stronger than pairwise compatibility. It is a local-to-global condition: pairwise witnesses must be replaceable by one witness for the entire clique.

### 2.3. Faces, chains, and Betti numbers

Let

$$
f_k(C)=|\{S\in K(C):|S|=k+1\}|
$$

be the number of $k$-faces. The vector space of $k$-chains over $\mathbb F$ has one basis vector for each $k$-face, hence dimension $f_k(C)$. The simplicial boundary map is

$$
\partial_k:C_k(K(C);\mathbb F)\longrightarrow C_{k-1}(K(C);\mathbb F).
$$

Writing $r_k=\operatorname{rank}(\partial_k)$, rank-nullity and the inclusion $\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}\subseteq\ker\partial_k$ give

$$
\beta_k(C)=\dim H_k(K(C);\mathbb F)
=f_k(C)-r_k-r_{k+1}.
$$

This formula makes clear that face counts provide chain generators, while boundary ranks determine which generators survive in homology.

## 3. Pairwise Reconstruction

### 3.1. The universal inclusion

**Theorem 3.1 (Genuine faces become graph cliques).** For every corpus $C$,

$$
K(C)\subseteq \mathrm{Cl}(G(C)).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Let $S\in K(C)$. Some $W\in C$ contains $S$. Any two distinct $x,y\in S$ therefore lie together in $W$, so they are adjacent in $G(C)$. Hence $S$ is a clique. $\square$

The inclusion has an important direction. Flag completion cannot discard a genuine co-citation face. Its danger is the opposite: it may insert faces not jointly witnessed by any document.

### 3.2. Exact criterion for equality

**Theorem 3.2 (Conformality Criterion).** For every finite corpus $C$,

$$
K(C)=\mathrm{Cl}(G(C))
$$

if and only if $C$ is conformal.

**Proof sketch.** Suppose first that the complexes are equal. If $S$ is a clique in $G(C)$, then $S\in\mathrm{Cl}(G(C))=K(C)$. By the definition of $K(C)$, some $W\in C$ contains $S$. Thus $C$ is conformal.

Conversely, suppose $C$ is conformal. Theorem 3.1 gives $K(C)\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}(G(C))$. If $S\in\mathrm{Cl}(G(C))$, conformality provides $W\in C$ with $S\subseteq W$, so $S\in K(C)$. The reverse inclusion follows. $\square$

This criterion is both necessary and sufficient; there is no additional hidden obstruction. It also yields an algorithmic certificate of failure: a clique not contained in any document proves that pairwise reconstruction is inaccurate.

### 3.3. Minimal counterexample

**Example 3.3 (The triangle boundary).** Let $V=\{0,1,2\}$ and

$$
C_\triangle=\big\{\{0,1\},\{0,2\},\{1,2\}\big\}.
$$

Every distinct pair occurs in a document, so $G(C_\triangle)$ is the complete graph on three vertices. Therefore $\mathrm{Cl}(G(C_\triangle))$ contains the $2$-simplex $\{0,1,2\}$. Yet no member of $C_\triangle$ contains all three vertices, so

$$
\{0,1,2\}\notin K(C_\triangle).
$$

Consequently,

$$
K(C_\triangle)\subsetneq\mathrm{Cl}(G(C_\triangle)).
$$

The genuine complex is the boundary of a triangle, whereas the clique complex is a filled triangle. Thus

$$
\beta_1(K(C_\triangle))=1,
\qquad
\beta_1(\mathrm{Cl}(G(C_\triangle)))=0.
$$

Pairwise projection followed by flag completion destroys the loop by adding a spurious face.

### 3.4. Complete-corpus boundary case

**Proposition 3.4 (One universal document).** If $C=\{V\}$ consists of one document citing every theorem, then

$$
K(C)=2^V,
$$

$G(C)$ is complete, and $C$ is conformal.

**Proof sketch.** Every subset of $V$ is contained in the unique citation set $V$, so the generated complex is the full simplex. Every distinct pair is jointly cited, making the graph complete. Every clique is a subset of $V$ and hence has the same universal witness. $\square$

The triangle-boundary and universal-document examples delimit the issue: a complete pairwise graph may correspond either to a hollow boundary or to a full simplex.

## 4. Monotone Corpus Growth and Persistence

**Theorem 4.1 (Monotonicity under corpus inclusion).** If $C\subseteq D$, then

$$
K(C)\subseteq K(D).
$$

**Proof sketch.** A face $S\in K(C)$ has a witness $W\in C$. Since $C\subseteq D$, the same $W$ belongs to $D$, and hence $S\in K(D)$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2 (Temporal filtration).** Let $(I,\le)$ be an ordered parameter set and let $C_t$ be a family of corpora satisfying $C_s\subseteq C_t$ whenever $s\le t$. Then

$$
K(C_s)\subseteq K(C_t)\qquad(s\le t).
$$

Therefore $(K(C_t))_{t\in I}$ is a simplicial filtration.

This result justifies persistent homology for chronological corpora. As documents arrive, a class can be born, persist, and later die when new faces fill it. The triangle-boundary example gives the simplest event. At an early time, three pair-documents create a one-cycle. If a later document cites all three theorems, the triangular face enters the complex and kills that cycle.

For higher dimensions, a collection of jointly cited triples can form the boundary of a tetrahedron, producing a second-homology class. A later four-way citation can fill the tetrahedron and kill it. Persistent $H_2$ can therefore measure sustained higher-order witness gaps. Whether such a gap represents a scientific paradigm shift is a separate empirical hypothesis.

## 5. Universal Counting and Homology Bounds

### 5.1. Face ceiling

**Theorem 5.1 (Binomial face bound).** If $|V|=n$, then for every $q\ge0$, the number of faces containing exactly $q$ vertices satisfies

$$
|\{S\in K(C):|S|=q\}|\le \binom nq.
$$

Equivalently, for every $k\ge0$,

$$
f_k(C)\le\binom n{k+1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Every $q$-vertex face is a $q$-element subset of the ambient $n$-element set $V$. There are exactly $\binom nq$ such subsets. The corpus can select only some of them. $\square$

This ceiling is sharp as a face-count bound: the universal-document corpus contains every subset and attains equality for every $q\le n$.

### 5.2. Betti ceiling

**Theorem 5.2 (Universal Betti ceiling).** For every corpus on $n$ vertices and every $k\ge0$,

$$
\beta_k(C)\le f_k(C)\le\binom n{k+1}\le n^{k+1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The homology group $H_k$ is a quotient of the cycle space, which is a subspace of the $k$-chain space. Thus $\beta_k\le\dim C_k=f_k$. Theorem 5.1 gives $f_k\le\binom n{k+1}$. Finally, each $(k+1)$-element subset can be represented by an ordered $(k+1)$-tuple, of which there are $n^{k+1}$, yielding $\binom n{k+1}\le n^{k+1}$. Equivalently, use the rank formula $\beta_k=f_k-r_k-r_{k+1}$ and nonnegativity of ranks. $\square$

The theorem validates $n^{k+1}$ only as a coarse upper bound. For fixed $k$ and large $n$, the sharper ceiling behaves as

$$
\binom n{k+1}\sim\frac{n^{k+1}}{(k+1)!},
$$

but even this says nothing about how close $\beta_k$ lies to the face ceiling. Boundary ranks may be large. For a full simplex, many faces exist but all reduced homology vanishes.

### 5.3. Dimension obstruction

**Theorem 5.3 (Vanishing above the ambient dimension).** If $|V|=n$ and $k\ge n$, then

$$
f_k(C)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\beta_k(C)=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** A $k$-simplex requires $k+1$ distinct vertices. When $k\ge n$, one has $k+1>n$, so no such subset of $V$ exists. The $k$-chain space is zero, and therefore its homology is zero. $\square$

**Corollary 5.4 (Failure of an unrestricted positive power law).** If $n>0$ and $k\ge n$, then

$$
\beta_k(C)=0<n^{k+1}.
$$

In particular, the identity $\beta_k=n^{k+1}$ and any two-sided comparison with a positive constant multiple of $n^{k+1}$ cannot hold uniformly over all dimensions.

The corollary does not rule out model-dependent asymptotics for fixed $k$ as $n\to\infty$. It shows that such a claim must specify its regime and cannot be inferred from combinatorial capacity alone.

## 6. Algorithms

### 6.1. Constructing the genuine complex

Given documents $W_1,\ldots,W_m$, enumerate every subset of every $W_i$ and insert it into a set. The result is $K(C)$. If $d=\max_i|W_i|$, direct enumeration requires

$$
O\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^m2^{|W_i|}\right)\subseteq O(m2^d)
$$

subset generations, plus hashing costs. The exponential dependence is unavoidable when the output itself contains exponentially many faces. In sparse corpora with bounded document size, it is practical.

### 6.2. Testing conformality

Construct $G(C)$ by connecting all pairs within each document. Enumerate maximal cliques of $G(C)$. The corpus is conformal if and only if every maximal clique is contained in some document. It suffices to test maximal cliques because every clique lies inside a maximal clique, and a witness for the maximal clique witnesses all its subsets.

Clique enumeration has worst-case exponential complexity; a graph can have exponentially many maximal cliques. The test is nevertheless exact and often tractable for sparse graphs. A failed test returns a concrete obstruction: a clique with no common witness.

### 6.3. Computing Betti numbers

Order the $k$-faces and form the boundary matrix $B_k$. Over $\mathbb F_2$, each column for a simplex has ones in the rows corresponding to its codimension-one faces; orientations can be ignored. Gaussian elimination gives $r_k=\operatorname{rank}B_k$, after which

$$
\beta_k=f_k-r_k-r_{k+1}.
$$

Dense elimination is cubic in the larger matrix dimension in a simple implementation, while sparse reduction can be much faster for citation complexes. The universal bounds provide consistency checks for every output.

### 6.4. Temporal persistence

Sort documents by timestamp, update the downward closure when each document arrives, and assign every newly appearing simplex its first appearance time. Because all faces of a simplex are already present no later than the simplex itself, this ordering defines a valid filtration. Standard column reduction of the filtration boundary matrix pairs births with deaths and leaves unpaired essential classes.

A key methodological requirement is to filter the genuine hypergraph complex, not merely the clique complex of each pairwise graph, unless conformality has been established. Otherwise, spurious filled faces can alter both birth and death times.

## 7. Applications and Interpretation

### 7.1. Diagnosing information loss

The conformality test quantifies when a pairwise network is a faithful representation of document-level joint citation. Nonconformal cliques identify precise locations where pairwise visualization overstates collective witness. These certificates can guide data interfaces: selecting a suspicious clique can reveal the distinct documents supporting its edges.

### 7.2. Persistent higher-order gaps

A long-lived homology class records a configuration of faces whose filling simplices remain absent over a substantial interval. Such classes may detect stable separations in the way theorem groups are jointly mobilized. In particular, persistent $H_2$ is sensitive to closed shells not filled by common four-way witnesses, whereas raw triangle counts do not distinguish shells from filled regions.

### 7.3. Semantic non-identifiability

Topology alone does not determine disciplinary meaning. Suppose the same abstract complex is given two incompatible assignments of labels such as algebra, geometry, and analysis. All Betti numbers and persistence intervals remain unchanged. Therefore no rule using topology alone can be guaranteed to recover labels that are defined independently of incidence.

Semantic claims become testable only after additional assumptions are introduced. Vertex metadata, document text, author communities, or a probabilistic assortativity model can connect topological patterns to research categories. The topology then acts as a structural feature rather than a self-interpreting conclusion.

### 7.4. Null models and normalization

The binomial ceiling suggests normalized quantities such as

$$
\frac{\beta_k}{\binom n{k+1}},
$$

when the denominator is nonzero. Yet a meaningful limiting theory requires a corpus-generating model: document-size distribution, theorem-degree distribution, dependence among citations, and a regime for $k$. Under sparse bounded-size models, one may ask whether normalized Betti profiles converge. The present results constrain but do not determine such limits.

## 8. Discussion

The distinction between hypergraph evidence and graph compatibility is the central structural lesson. A face in $K(C)$ asserts one common witness. A clique in $G(C)$ asserts only a collection of pairwise witnesses. The Conformality Criterion identifies exactly when the existential quantifiers can be synchronized.

This distinction also clarifies persistent analysis. Monotonicity is unconditional: adding documents always yields a larger genuine complex. But replacing that complex with a flag completion is justified only when conformality holds at the relevant times. A temporal study may therefore track both topology and a “conformality gap,” the set of clique faces without common witnesses.

The counting results place firm limits on asymptotic rhetoric. The exponent $k+1$ arises because a $k$-simplex uses $k+1$ vertices, not because homology generally saturates the available chain space. The exact ceiling is binomial; topology is the residue after boundary ranks are subtracted. Any claim of polynomial Betti growth must state a model that controls those ranks.

Finally, the dimension obstruction is decisive when $k$ varies with $n$. Once $k\ge n$, the chain group is empty. A uniform law that remains positive cannot survive this boundary. Fixed-dimensional asymptotics remain open and potentially fruitful, but they are logically different from an unrestricted formula.

## 9. Future Work

Four directions follow naturally.

First, define sparse random corpus models with bounded expected document size and nondegenerate theorem-degree distributions. The goal is to determine whether normalized fixed-dimensional Betti numbers converge to deterministic limits and when their unnormalized values have order strictly below the binomial ceiling.

Second, study temporal thresholds for conformality. In a growing random corpus, one may seek the point at which every clique gains a common document witness and the genuine complex becomes equal to its flag completion.

Third, investigate the stability and identifiability of semantic labels. One expects that topology alone cannot uniformly recover externally defined research communities, while recovery may become possible under metadata-enhanced assortative models.

Fourth, compare persistent $H_2$ with raw motif counts for detecting localized structural change. Persistent cavities measure the duration of missing common witnesses; triangle and tetrahedron counts alone ignore how faces assemble and whether cavities survive.

## 10. Conclusion

The framework also suggests a reporting standard for empirical studies. Authors should state whether faces represent genuine common-document witnesses or flag completion, report the proportion and dimensions of unwitnessed cliques, specify the coefficient field used for homology, and distinguish raw Betti numbers from normalized quantities. Temporal analyses should identify the filtration parameter and explain how simultaneous timestamps are handled. These choices can materially change the resulting topology. Reporting them makes comparisons reproducible at the mathematical level and prevents graph-derived simplices from being mistaken for observed higher-order events.

A theorem network has more structure than its edges. Encoding documents as hyperedges and taking their downward closure preserves genuine common witnesses. Every such face appears as a clique in the pairwise graph, but pairwise information reconstructs all higher-order faces exactly only under conformality. The three-theorem boundary is the minimal obstruction and demonstrates that flag completion can erase homology.

Nested corpora produce valid simplicial filtrations, establishing a sound basis for persistent analysis. On $n$ vertices, every $k$th Betti number satisfies

$$
0\le\beta_k\le f_k\le\binom n{k+1}\le n^{k+1},
$$

and $\beta_k=0$ for $k\ge n$. These results replace an unsupported universal power law with exact structural bounds. They also delineate the frontier between mathematics and interpretation: topology detects incidence patterns, while claims about communities or paradigm shifts require additional data and assumptions.

# Higher-Order Co-Citation Topology: Pairwise Information Loss, Persistence, and Dimensional Limits

## Abstract

We model a finite theorem corpus as a hypergraph whose vertices are mathematical theorems and whose records are sets of theorems jointly cited by individual sources. The downward closure of these records is a simplicial co-citation complex. This construction retains common-witness information that is discarded by the ordinary pairwise co-citation graph. We prove three structural results. First, a corpus containing the three pairwise records on three vertices has a genuine one-dimensional hole, and adding one joint triple record strictly enlarges the simplicial complex while leaving its pairwise graph unchanged. Second, a monotone temporal corpus induces nested complexes: every face present at an earlier time persists at all later times. Third, for a nonempty complex on $n$ vertices, no Betti profile can satisfy $\beta_k=n^{k+1}$ in every dimension, and no positive constant can make $n^{k+1}$ a uniform lower bound up to that factor. The obstruction is finite-dimensional vanishing: $\beta_k=0$ for $k\ge n$. More generally, $\beta_k\le\binom{n}{k+1}$. These results clarify the boundary between higher-order and graph-based analysis and replace an untenable dimension-uniform power law with fixed-dimensional questions. We give algorithms for complex construction, boundary-rank homology, and filtration analysis, discuss cryptographic theorem corpora, and formulate empirically testable directions.

## 1. Introduction

Citation-network analysis commonly represents each cited object as a vertex and each co-cited pair as an edge. This representation is appropriate when pairwise association is the primitive datum. A source document, however, typically cites a set of results together. The assertion that every pair among three theorems has been co-cited is weaker than the assertion that all three have appeared in one record. Passing immediately to a graph erases this common-witness distinction.

Simplicial complexes provide a natural higher-order representation. A jointly cited pair determines an edge, a jointly cited triple determines a filled triangle, and a jointly cited quadruple determines a tetrahedron. Taking all subsets of every citation record produces a simplicial complex without inventing any higher-order relation. Its homology measures holes: connected components in dimension zero, loop-like gaps in dimension one, shell-like gaps in dimension two, and their higher-dimensional analogues.

The motivating interpretations are attractive but require discipline. Long-lived one-dimensional classes may correlate with research communities separated by missing integrative citations. Changes in second-dimensional persistence may correlate with conceptual reorganizations. In cryptography, cycles may organize results built on interchangeable hardness assumptions. None of these interpretations follows from topology alone. The structural role of mathematics is to identify what information the representation contains, what projection destroys, and which proposed universal laws are possible.

This paper establishes that foundation. Section 2 defines corpora, complexes, graphs, chains, Betti numbers, and filtrations. Section 3 gives the minimal example of pairwise invisibility. Section 4 proves forward persistence of faces. Section 5 derives binomial bounds and disproves dimension-uniform power laws. Section 6 presents computational methods. Sections 7–9 discuss interpretation, applications, limitations, and future work.

## 2. Mathematical framework

### 2.1 Corpora and citation witnesses

Let $V$ be a finite nonempty set of vertices, each representing a theorem, proposition, construction, or other mathematical result. Write $n=|V|$. A **citation record** is a subset $W\subseteq V$. A **corpus** $C$ is a finite collection of citation records. The intended meaning is that all vertices in $W$ were jointly cited by one source.

A set $S\subseteq V$ has a **common citation witness** in $C$ if there exists $W\in C$ with $S\subseteq W$. This condition is stronger than requiring every two-element subset of $S$ to have some witness, because those pairwise witnesses may be different records.

### 2.2 The co-citation complex

**Definition 2.1 (Co-citation complex).** The co-citation complex generated by $C$ is

$$
K(C)=\{S\subseteq V:\text{ there exists }W\in C\text{ such that }S\subseteq W\}.
$$

Every subset of a member of $K(C)$ also belongs to $K(C)$, so $K(C)$ is an abstract simplicial complex. A set $S$ of cardinality $k+1$ is a $k$-simplex. Thus vertices are $0$-simplices, edges are $1$-simplices, triples are $2$-simplices, and so forth.

The empty face is included whenever the corpus has a witness record; it plays its conventional combinatorial role and does not affect the central examples.

**Lemma 2.2 (Monotonicity under corpus inclusion).** If $C\subseteq D$, then $K(C)\subseteq K(D)$.

**Proof sketch.** If $S\in K(C)$, some $W\in C$ contains $S$. Since $C\subseteq D$, the same $W$ lies in $D$ and witnesses $S\in K(D)$. $\square$

### 2.3 The pairwise shadow

**Definition 2.3 (Co-citation graph).** The co-citation graph $G(C)$ has vertex set $V$. Distinct vertices $x$ and $y$ are adjacent precisely when there exists $W\in C$ with $x,y\in W$.

The graph $G(C)$ is the $1$-skeleton of $K(C)$. It retains all vertices and edges but not the identity of their witnesses or the higher-dimensional simplices.

Given a graph $G$, its **clique complex** contains every finite vertex set whose members are pairwise adjacent. Always $K(C)$ is a subcomplex of the clique complex of $G(C)$: a common witness for a set witnesses all its pairs. Equality need not hold.

**Definition 2.4 (Conformality).** A corpus is conformal if every clique of $G(C)$ is contained in a single record of $C$. Equivalently, $K(C)$ equals the clique complex of its pairwise graph.

Conformality is therefore the exact condition under which pairwise projection loses no face information. The results below demonstrate failure of conformality in the smallest possible nontrivial case.

### 2.4 Chains, boundaries, and Betti numbers

Fix a field $\mathbb F$. For each $k\ge0$, let $C_k(K;\mathbb F)$ be the vector space with basis the $k$-simplices of $K$. Its dimension is the number $f_k$ of $k$-simplices. After choosing vertex orderings, the boundary map

$$
\partial_k:C_k(K;\mathbb F)\longrightarrow C_{k-1}(K;\mathbb F)
$$

maps an oriented simplex to the alternating sum of its codimension-one faces. The identity $\partial_k\partial_{k+1}=0$ implies $\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1}\subseteq\ker\partial_k$.

The $k$th homology group and Betti number are

$$
H_k(K;\mathbb F)=\ker\partial_k/\operatorname{im}\partial_{k+1},
\qquad
\beta_k(K)=\dim_{\mathbb F}H_k(K;\mathbb F).
$$

By rank-nullity,

$$
\beta_k=f_k-\operatorname{rank}(\partial_k)-\operatorname{rank}(\partial_{k+1}).
$$

This rank presentation makes clear that Betti numbers are not face counts. They subtract both boundaries leaving a dimension and boundaries entering from the next dimension.

### 2.5 Temporal filtrations and persistence

Let $(T,\le)$ be an ordered time set. A **filtered corpus** is a family $(C_t)_{t\in T}$ satisfying $C_s\subseteq C_t$ whenever $s\le t$. By Lemma 2.2, it induces a filtration

$$
K(C_s)\subseteq K(C_t)\qquad(s\le t).
$$

The inclusion maps induce linear maps on homology. Persistent homology studies classes across these maps, recording when a class appears and when it becomes trivial or merges. Faces only accumulate in this model, but homology classes may be born or killed.

## 3. Pairwise invisibility of a higher-order filling

Let $V=\{A,B,C\}$. Define the **triangle-boundary corpus**

$$
C_{\partial}=\big\{\{A,B\},\{B,C\},\{A,C\}\big\}.
$$

Define the **filled-triangle corpus** by adjoining the joint record:

$$
C_{\triangle}=C_{\partial}\cup\big\{\{A,B,C\}\big\}.
$$

### 3.1 Face structure

In $K(C_{\partial})$, all three vertices and all three edges occur. The triple $\{A,B,C\}$ does not occur because no record contains it. Thus the complex is the boundary of a triangle. In $K(C_{\triangle})$, the new record witnesses the triple and all of its subsets, so the filled triangular $2$-simplex occurs.

**Theorem 3.1 (Strict higher-order filling).** The inclusion

$$
K(C_{\partial})\subset K(C_{\triangle})
$$

is strict.

**Proof sketch.** Corpus inclusion gives the weak inclusion by Lemma 2.2. The triple $\{A,B,C\}$ belongs to $K(C_{\triangle})$ because it is itself a record. It does not belong to $K(C_{\partial})$, since each old record has cardinality two. Hence the complexes are unequal. $\square$

### 3.2 Equality of pairwise graphs

**Theorem 3.2 (Pairwise invisibility).** The co-citation graphs are identical:

$$
G(C_{\partial})=G(C_{\triangle}).
$$

Both are the complete graph on three vertices.

**Proof sketch.** Every distinct pair among $A$, $B$, and $C$ already appears as a record in $C_{\partial}$, so its graph is complete. Adding the triple cannot add a new pair because no pair was missing. Conversely, all old witnesses remain. Therefore the edge sets coincide. $\square$

Together, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 show that the map from corpora to pairwise graphs is not injective even at the level of generated complexes. A graph-only method cannot distinguish these two corpora.

### 3.3 Homological effect

Over any field, the boundary complex has chain dimensions $f_0=3$, $f_1=3$, and $f_2=0$. It is connected, and its three edges contain one independent cycle. Hence

$$
(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2)=(1,1,0).
$$

The filled triangle has $f_2=1$. The boundary of its $2$-simplex is exactly the three-edge cycle, so that cycle becomes a boundary. Hence

$$
(\beta_0,\beta_1,\beta_2)=(1,0,0).
$$

**Corollary 3.3 (Graph equality does not determine homology).** Two corpora may have identical co-citation graphs but co-citation complexes with different first Betti numbers.

**Proof sketch.** Apply the preceding calculation to $C_{\partial}$ and $C_{\triangle}$. $\square$

This conclusion concerns homology of the witness complex, not the clique complex. If both graphs were automatically clique-filled, both would contain the triangle and the distinction would be erased by construction.

## 4. Persistence under corpus growth

**Theorem 4.1 (Face persistence).** Let $(C_t)_{t\in T}$ be a filtered corpus. If $s\le t$ and $S\in K(C_s)$, then $S\in K(C_t)$.

**Proof sketch.** Membership $S\in K(C_s)$ supplies a record $W\in C_s$ with $S\subseteq W$. Monotonicity gives $W\in C_t$, so the same record witnesses $S\in K(C_t)$. $\square$

The theorem is elementary, but it is the structural requirement for persistent homology: inclusions between complexes exist at all ordered times.

**Corollary 4.2 (Filtration functoriality).** For $r\le s\le t$, the inclusions satisfy

$$
K(C_r)\hookrightarrow K(C_s)\hookrightarrow K(C_t),
$$

and the induced homology map from time $r$ to time $t$ is the composition of the maps through time $s$.

**Proof sketch.** Set inclusion is transitive, and homology is functorial with respect to simplicial inclusions. $\square$

The triangle example can be placed at two times. At the first time the three pair records exist; at the second the triple is added. The unique first-dimensional class dies at the second time although the pairwise graph is unchanged. Thus higher-order persistence can register an event invisible to a graph filtration.

It is important to separate simplex persistence from homology-class persistence. In a growing corpus, an existing simplex is never removed. An existing homology class can nevertheless die when newly added higher-dimensional simplices make its representative cycle a boundary.

## 5. Dimensional ceilings and the failure of uniform power growth

### 5.1 The binomial chain ceiling

A $k$-simplex has $k+1$ distinct vertices. On a vertex set of size $n$, there are only $\binom{n}{k+1}$ such subsets.

**Theorem 5.1 (Binomial Betti bound).** For every simplicial complex $K$ on $n$ vertices and every $k\ge0$,

$$
0\le\beta_k(K)\le f_k(K)\le\binom{n}{k+1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Homology is a quotient of $\ker\partial_k$, which is a subspace of $C_k$, so $\beta_k\le\dim C_k=f_k$. Every $k$-simplex is a $(k+1)$-element subset of the $n$ vertices, giving $f_k\le\binom{n}{k+1}$. Nonnegativity follows from dimension. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (Finite-dimensional vanishing).** If $k\ge n$, then

$$
\beta_k(K)=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** There is no subset of an $n$-element set with $k+1>n$ elements, so $f_k=0$. Theorem 5.1 then forces $\beta_k=0$. $\square$

### 5.2 Exact dimension-uniform power law

Consider the proposed law

$$
\beta_k(K)=n^{k+1}\qquad\text{for every }k\ge0.
$$

**Theorem 5.3 (Impossibility of exact uniform power growth).** No nonempty finite co-citation complex on $n$ vertices satisfies $\beta_k=n^{k+1}$ for every homological dimension $k$.

**Proof sketch.** Since the vertex set is nonempty, $n>0$. Set $k=n$. Corollary 5.2 gives $\beta_n=0$, whereas $n^{n+1}>0$. The asserted equality is impossible. $\square$

The argument does not depend on the corpus structure, coefficient field, density, or boundary ranks. It follows solely from finite vertex support.

### 5.3 Positive constant-factor lower law

A weaker interpretation might posit a positive constant $a$ such that

$$
n^{k+1}\le a\beta_k(K)
$$

for all $k$. This would assert that $\beta_k$ is at least a fixed positive fraction, up to reciprocal scaling, of the proposed power.

**Theorem 5.4 (Impossibility of a uniform positive lower power law).** For a nonempty finite co-citation complex, there is no positive constant $a$ satisfying

$$
n^{k+1}\le a\beta_k(K)
$$

for every $k\ge0$.

**Proof sketch.** Again take $k=n$. The right-hand side equals $a\beta_n=0$ by Corollary 5.2, while the left-hand side $n^{n+1}$ is positive. $\square$

This theorem also rules out any formulation $\beta_k\ge c n^{k+1}$ for all $k$ with a fixed $c>0$.

### 5.4 Scope of the obstruction

Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 do not refute fixed-dimensional asymptotics. If $k$ is fixed and $n\to\infty$, then

$$
\binom{n}{k+1}\sim\frac{n^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}.
$$

Thus $n^{k+1}$ is the correct order for the maximum possible number of $k$-faces at fixed $k$. Whether $\beta_k$ has that order depends on record density and boundary ranks. Dense complexes often have many faces but small homology because higher-dimensional faces fill cycles. Sparse complexes may lack enough faces to support many cycles. Meaningful scaling claims must therefore specify a fixed dimension, a corpus model, a density regime, and preferably a coefficient field.

## 6. Algorithms

### 6.1 Constructing the witness complex

Given records $C=\{W_1,\ldots,W_m\}$, construct $K(C)$ by enumerating every subset of every $W_i$ and taking the union. If $r=\max_i|W_i|$, direct enumeration takes $O(\sum_i2^{|W_i|})$, bounded by $O(m2^r)$. Hashing faces gives expected constant-time duplicate elimination per generated face. This is practical when records are small even if the global vertex set is large.

For homology only through dimension $d$, enumerate subsets of size at most $d+1$. The number generated from record $W_i$ is

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{\min(d+1,|W_i|)}\binom{|W_i|}{j},
$$

which can be far smaller than $2^{|W_i|}$.

### 6.2 Detecting pairwise information loss

Construct the co-citation graph by adding every pair from each record. Enumerate graph cliques and test whether each clique is contained in a common record. A failing clique certifies nonconformality. The smallest certificate is a triple whose three pairs are witnessed but which has no common witness.

General clique enumeration is worst-case exponential, as the number of cliques can itself be exponential. For fixed clique size $q$, one may test all $q$-subsets in $O(n^q)$ set operations. For the triangle certificate, enumerate triples and check three edge memberships plus one face membership.

### 6.3 Computing Betti numbers

Order the $k$-simplices and form boundary matrices $D_k$. Over $\mathbb F_2$, orientations disappear and each column has $k+1$ ones. Gaussian elimination yields ranks $r_k=\operatorname{rank}D_k$, then

$$
\beta_k=f_k-r_k-r_{k+1}.
$$

If $N_k=f_k$, dense elimination costs polynomial time, commonly bounded by $O(N^3)$ for matrices of scale $N$, while sparse reductions are often much faster in practice. Persistent homology uses a filtration-compatible ordering and column reduction to pair births with deaths.

### 6.4 Sanity checks

Every computation should verify:

1. downward closure of the face set;
2. $\partial_k\partial_{k+1}=0$;
3. $0\le\beta_k\le f_k\le\binom{n}{k+1}$;
4. $\beta_k=0$ for $k\ge n$;
5. filtration monotonicity of faces.

The three-vertex example is a useful regression test because graph equality coexists with a change from $\beta_1=1$ to $\beta_1=0$.

## 7. Applications and interpretation

### 7.1 Research communities

A persistent first-dimensional class indicates a loop of lower-order relations that is not filled by observed higher-order integration. Such a class could trace boundaries among research communities: neighboring traditions cite one another pairwise, while no common source unifies the entire cycle. This is a hypothesis, not an identification theorem. Evaluation should compare persistence-based predictors with independently curated communities and graph modularity, using held-out corpora and temporal null models.

### 7.2 Conceptual reorganization

A second-dimensional class represents a shell assembled from triangular joint-citation relations but not filled by tetrahedral relations. Its death requires higher-order integration. Abrupt changes in distributions of second-dimensional lifetimes may therefore serve as change-point features. Yet citation growth alone can alter these distributions, so tests must control for field size, document count, degree sequence, and record-size distribution.

### 7.3 Cryptographic theorem networks

Cryptographic arguments frequently relate security notions, reductions, and hardness assumptions. Pairwise co-citation can indicate that two assumptions are compared or substituted. A persistent cycle may arise when several assumptions are pairwise connected but lack a common theorem or survey integrating them. A later record jointly treating all assumptions can fill the cycle without changing pairwise adjacency.

This makes cryptography a natural application domain, but semantic labels are essential. Vertices should distinguish assumptions, reductions, attacks, and protocol properties; otherwise a topological class may conflate different mathematical roles. Robustness should be checked under alternative vertex definitions and citation extraction procedures.

### 7.4 Privacy-preserving aggregate analysis

When citation records are sensitive, one might release only a graph to reduce disclosure. Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 show the analytical cost: the release can conceal higher-order fillings and alter homology. Privacy mechanisms for higher-order data should therefore quantify both disclosure and topological distortion rather than assuming the graph is a sufficient statistic.

## 8. Limitations and discussion

The complex records co-occurrence, not logical dependence. A source may cite several results for historical context rather than use them jointly in a proof. Conversely, a genuine conceptual relationship may be omitted from citations. Data extraction, theorem identity resolution, editions, and citation granularity can all change the complex.

Downward closure also has an interpretive consequence. A record jointly citing five theorems generates every subset, although the source may never explicitly discuss each subset. This is mathematically necessary for a simplicial complex, but alternative structures such as hypergraphs or simplicial sets may retain additional information.

The face-persistence theorem assumes records are never deleted. Real databases are corrected, deduplicated, and reclassified. Such changes may require zigzag persistence rather than an ordinary filtration.

Betti numbers compress substantial structure. Two complexes can have equal Betti profiles but different cycle representatives, persistence intervals, or combinatorial geometry. Results should report representative supports and stability analyses, not only scalar ranks.

Finally, topological classes have no automatic sociological semantics. Calling $H_1$ a collection of “schools” or $H_2$ a detector of “paradigm shifts” is a conjectural interpretation requiring external validation. The established results justify preserving higher-order witnesses and delimit possible scaling laws; they do not validate those labels.

## 9. Future research

Several directions follow naturally.

First, labeled persistence can test whether long-lived first-homology classes predict curated community boundaries better than pairwise modularity. Evaluation should be out of sample and benchmarked against degree-preserving temporal null models.

Second, change-point methods can compare shifts in second-homology lifetimes with independently dated conceptual reorganizations while controlling for citation volume and field growth.

Third, random hypergraph models can study conformality thresholds. The key event is that every graph clique acquires a common record witness; its threshold need not coincide with ordinary graph connectivity.

Fourth, fixed-dimensional Betti scaling should replace dimension-uniform claims. For each fixed $k$, one can seek piecewise-polynomial expectations in calibrated sparse models and identify critical windows where cycles emerge and are filled.

Fifth, cryptographic corpora offer a focused test bed for cycles around interchangeable hardness assumptions. Semantic annotation can determine whether persistent classes align with known families of reductions and assumption substitutions.

Additional work should develop stability bounds under missing or noisy records, compare witness complexes with clique complexes quantitatively, and design privacy-preserving releases that retain controlled higher-order summaries.

## 10. Conclusion

A theorem co-citation graph is only the pairwise shadow of a higher-order corpus. Three pair records and one joint triple produce the same complete graph, yet their co-citation complexes differ strictly and their first Betti numbers are respectively $1$ and $0$. A growing corpus yields nested complexes because every earlier face retains its witness. Meanwhile, finite vertex support imposes the universal ceiling

$$
\beta_k\le\binom{n}{k+1}
$$

and forces $\beta_k=0$ for $k\ge n$. Consequently, neither $\beta_k=n^{k+1}$ nor any positive constant-factor lower version can hold uniformly over all dimensions in a nonempty finite corpus.

These facts establish a precise agenda. Preserve joint citation witnesses; use persistent homology to study their evolution; interpret topological features only through external validation; and formulate asymptotic laws at fixed dimension within explicit generative models. The distinction between an empty triangle and a filled one is elementary, but it marks the boundary between the geometry present in the data and the geometry invented by its pairwise projection.

## Abstract

For a binary transition relation $R$ on a state space $\alpha$ and a distinguished state $a$, define the *degree monoid*
$$D(R,a) \;=\; \{\, n \in \mathbb{N} \;:\; \text{$a$ is reachable from $a$ in exactly $n$ $R$-steps} \,\}.$$
This is the coarsest nontrivial dynamical observation one can make of a pointed transition system: it records the possible lengths of closed computations at $a$ and nothing else. We show that $D(R,a)$ is always an additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$, that it is functorial under simulation and invariant under bisimulation, that it converts synchronous products into lattice meets and time rescaling into passage to a colon monoid, and — the **Realisation Theorem** — that the chain machine generated by a set $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ has degree monoid exactly $\langle S \rangle$. Consequently the invariant is *complete*: the class of realisable values is precisely the lattice of additive submonoids of $\mathbb{N}$, and, upgrading via finite generation, precisely the values realisable by machines with finitely many states.

We then develop the structure theory. The *period* $d(R,a) = \gcd D(R,a)$ divides every closed-computation length, every sufficiently large multiple of the period occurs, the set of *gaps* is finite, and the period of a synchronous product of live machines is the least common multiple of the periods.

The main structural dichotomy concerns determinism. We prove that for a deterministic relation the degree monoid is closed under truncated subtraction, hence equals $d\mathbb{N}$ exactly: deterministic machines are **gapless**. Therefore a single gap is a certificate of nondeterminism, and no deterministic machine — on any state space, of any cardinality — realises the numerical semigroup $\langle 2,3\rangle$. On a finite state space a live deterministic machine has period at most the number of states. Finally we compute the obstruction exactly for two-loop machines: via a residue criterion and the symmetry involution $n \mapsto F - n$, where $F = pq - p - q$, the two-loop machine of coprime $p,q>1$ misses exactly $(p-1)(q-1)/2$ computation lengths (Sylvester's count).

**Keywords:** transition system, closed computation, additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$, numerical semigroup, determinism, bisimulation, Frobenius number, Sylvester's gap count.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The measurement

Abstract computation is modelled with breathtaking economy by a *transition relation*: a set $\alpha$ of configurations and a binary relation $R \subseteq \alpha \times \alpha$, where $R\,a\,b$ means "the machine may pass from $a$ to $b$ in one step". Automata, rewriting systems, Kripke structures, Markov chain supports, cellular automata and abstract state machines are all instances.

Fix a state $a$ and make the crudest possible observation: for which $n$ can the machine, started at $a$, be at $a$ again after exactly $n$ steps? Write $D(R,a) \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ for the answer. We call it the **degree monoid** of the state, and this paper is a systematic study of it: what values it takes, what operations on machines it turns into what operations on numbers, and what it detects.

Three motivations. First, *coarseness with completeness*: the observation throws away almost everything, yet its range of values is exactly a well-understood algebraic lattice, so it is a genuine classification rather than a partial invariant. Second, *behavioural robustness*: it is invariant under bisimulation, hence a property of behaviour rather than presentation. Third, *discriminating power*: despite its coarseness it separates deterministic from nondeterministic computation, absolutely and without resource bounds.

### 1.2 Results

Throughout, $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2,\dots\}$; a set $M \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ is an **additive submonoid** if $0 \in M$ and $M + M \subseteq M$; $\langle S \rangle$ denotes the submonoid generated by $S$; and for $n \in \mathbb{N}$, $n\mathbb{N} = \{0,n,2n,\dots\}$.

1. **(Monoid law.)** $D(R,a)$ is an additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$ (Theorem 2.3).
2. **(Functoriality.)** Simulations increase the invariant, bisimulations preserve it (Theorems 3.1, 3.2).
3. **(Product and rescaling laws.)** $D(R \times S, (a,b)) = D(R,a) \cap D(S,b)$, and $D(R^{[k]}, a) = (D(R,a) : k) := \{n : kn \in D(R,a)\}$ (Theorems 3.4, 3.6).
4. **(Realisation.)** For any $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ the chain machine $C_S$ satisfies $D(C_S, 0) = \langle S\rangle$; hence every additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$ is realised, and the invariant's range is exactly that lattice (Theorem 4.3, Corollary 4.5).
5. **(Finite-state realisation.)** Every additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$ is the degree monoid of a state of a machine with finitely many states (Theorem 6.7).
6. **(Structure.)** With $d = \gcd D(R,a)$: $d \mid n$ for all $n \in D(R,a)$; all sufficiently large multiples of $d$ lie in $D(R,a)$; the gap set is finite; $d = 0$ iff $D(R,a) = \{0\}$; and periods of synchronous products are least common multiples (Section 5).
7. **(Determinism.)** Deterministic machines have subtraction-closed, hence gapless, degree monoids $D(R,a) = d\mathbb{N}$; a gap certifies nondeterminism; no deterministic machine realises $\langle 2,3\rangle$; a live deterministic machine on $N$ states has period $\le N$ (Section 6).
8. **(Gap count.)** For coprime $p,q>1$ the two-loop machine misses exactly $(p-1)(q-1)/2$ lengths, the largest being $pq-p-q$ (Section 7).

## 2. Exact-length reachability and the degree monoid

**Definition 2.1 (Iterated relation).** For $R : \alpha \to \alpha \to \mathrm{Prop}$ define $R^{(n)}$, *reachability in exactly $n$ steps*, by recursion on $n$:
$$R^{(0)}\,a\,b \iff a = b, \qquad R^{(n+1)}\,a\,c \iff \exists b,\; R\,a\,b \ \wedge\ R^{(n)}\,b\,c.$$
Thus $R^{(1)} = R$ and $R^{(n)}$ holds iff there is a path $a = x_0 \to x_1 \to \cdots \to x_n = b$ with each consecutive pair related by $R$.

**Lemma 2.2 (Concatenation law).** For all $m,n$ and $a,c$,
$$R^{(m+n)}\,a\,c \iff \exists b,\; R^{(m)}\,a\,b \ \wedge\ R^{(n)}\,b\,c.$$

*Proof.* Induction on $m$. For $m=0$ both sides say $R^{(n)}\,a\,c$ (take $b=a$). For the step, unfold $R^{(m+1+n)}\,a\,c$ as $\exists x,\, R\,a\,x \wedge R^{(m+n)}\,x\,c$, apply the induction hypothesis to the tail, and reassociate. $\square$

Concatenation is the engine of the whole theory: it is what makes the set of return lengths additively closed, and, read backwards, it is what lets us *split* a long loop at any intermediate time — the step used repeatedly in Sections 4 and 6.

**Definition 2.3 (Degree monoid).** For a state $a$,
$$D(R,a) \;=\; \{\, n \in \mathbb{N} : R^{(n)}\,a\,a \,\}.$$

**Theorem 2.4 (Monoid law).** $D(R,a)$ is an additive submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$.

*Proof.* $0 \in D(R,a)$ since $R^{(0)}\,a\,a$ is $a=a$. If $m,n \in D(R,a)$, then Lemma 2.2 with $b := a$ yields $R^{(m+n)}\,a\,a$. $\square$

Note carefully what is *not* asserted: no closure under subtraction, and no reason for $1 \in D(R,a)$ to follow from $2,3 \in D(R,a)$. That failure is precisely the phenomenon of *gaps*, and Section 6 shows it is exactly the trace of nondeterminism.

**Examples 2.5.**
(i) The empty relation gives $D = \{0\}$, the bottom of the lattice.
(ii) The total relation (in particular a single state with a self-loop) gives $D = \mathbb{N}$, the top.
(iii) A directed $n$-cycle, based at any of its states, gives $D = n\mathbb{N}$.
(iv) Two loops of lengths $2$ and $3$ glued at $a$ give $D = \langle 2,3\rangle = \{0,2,3,4,5,\dots\}$, which omits $1$.

## 3. Functoriality and algebraic laws

### 3.1 Simulation and bisimulation

**Theorem 3.1 (Simulation monotonicity).** Let $f : \alpha \to \beta$ satisfy $R\,a\,b \Rightarrow S\,(f a)\,(f b)$ for all $a,b$. Then $D(R,a) \subseteq D(S, f a)$ for every $a$.

*Proof.* By induction on $n$, $R^{(n)}\,a\,b$ implies $S^{(n)}\,(fa)\,(fb)$: the base case is congruence of equality, and the step maps the first edge and applies the induction hypothesis to the tail. Take $b = a$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.2 (Bisimulation invariance).** Let $f : \alpha \to \beta$ be injective, *reflect and preserve* steps ($R\,a\,b \iff S\,(fa)\,(fb)$), and have image closed under $S$-steps from the image (for all $a$ and $y$, $S\,(fa)\,y$ implies $y = f b$ for some $b$). Then $D(R,a) = D(S, fa)$.

*Proof sketch.* "$\subseteq$" is Theorem 3.1. For "$\supseteq$", prove by induction on $n$: if $S^{(n)}\,(fx)\,y$ then there is $z$ with $f z = y$ and $R^{(n)}\,x\,z$. The first $S$-step from $fx$ lands in the image by hypothesis, say at $fu$; it reflects to an $R$-step $x \to u$; recurse from $u$. Applying this with $y = fa$ and using injectivity of $f$ gives $z = a$. $\square$

So the invariant does not see the presentation of a system, only its step behaviour on the reachable part — the minimal sanity requirement for a semantic invariant. It is also, by Theorem 3.1, monotone under abstraction: over-approximating a machine can only add closed-computation lengths, never delete them, so *non-membership* is a sound property to verify on an abstraction.

### 3.2 Synchronous products

**Definition 3.3.** The *synchronous product* $R \times S$ of relations on $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is the relation on $\alpha \times \beta$ with $(R\times S)\,(a,b)\,(a',b') \iff R\,a\,a' \wedge S\,b\,b'$.

**Theorem 3.4 (Product law).** $D(R\times S, (a,b)) = D(R,a) \cap D(S,b)$.

*Proof.* By induction on $n$, $(R\times S)^{(n)}\,p\,q \iff R^{(n)}\,p_1\,q_1 \wedge S^{(n)}\,p_2\,q_2$; the step case pairs up the two first edges in one direction and projects in the other. Specialise $p = q = (a,b)$. $\square$

Since intersection is the meet in the lattice of submonoids of $\mathbb{N}$, and since (by realisation, Section 4) every submonoid — in particular every join $D(R,a) \vee D(S,b)$, i.e. the submonoid generated by the union — is realisable, the class of degree monoids is closed under both lattice operations, meets being implemented by an explicit machine construction.

### 3.3 Time rescaling

**Definition 3.5.** For $k \in \mathbb{N}$ let $R^{[k]}$ denote the relation $R^{(k)}$ regarded as a one-step relation: the machine "$k$ old steps per new tick".

**Theorem 3.6 (Rescaling law).** $n \in D(R^{[k]}, a) \iff kn \in D(R,a)$; that is, $D(R^{[k]},a) = (D(R,a) : k)$, the colon monoid.

*Proof.* By induction on $n$, $(R^{(k)})^{(n)} = R^{(kn)}$: the step case follows from Lemma 2.2 applied to $k + kn$. $\square$

Rescaling is an algebraic operation on the invariant, and it is the natural formal counterpart of "clocking a machine faster". Note $(M : k)$ can be strictly larger than $M$ scaled: e.g. $(\langle 2,3\rangle : 2) = \mathbb{N}$ because $2n \in \langle 2,3\rangle$ for all $n$. Rescaling can *destroy gaps* — an observation that Section 8 turns into a research programme.

## 4. The Realisation Theorem

We now show that the monoid law of Theorem 2.4 is the *only* constraint.

**Definition 4.1 (Chain machine).** For $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ define the transition relation $C_S$ on the state space $\mathbb{N}$ by
$$C_S\,i\,j \iff \big(i = 0 \ \wedge\ j+1 \in S\big) \ \vee\ i = j+1 .$$
In words: a positive state $i$ counts down deterministically to $i-1$; the base state $0$ may nondeterministically jump to $s-1$ for any nonzero $s \in S$ (and cannot move at all if $S \subseteq \{0\}$). Jumping to $s-1$ then counting down is a closed computation at $0$ of length exactly $s$. Distinct generators give loops sharing only the base state.

**Lemma 4.2 (Descent and its converse).**
(i) For every $k$, $C_S^{(k)}\,k\,0$: from state $k$ the base is reached in exactly $k$ steps.
(ii) If $C_S^{(k)}\,b\,0$ then $b \le k$ and $C_S^{(k-b)}\,0\,0$.

*Proof.* (i) Induction on $k$, using the deterministic edge $k \to k-1$. (ii) Induction on $b$. For $b=0$ there is nothing to prove. For $b+1$, the path is nonempty, and the only outgoing edge from the positive state $b+1$ goes to $b$; apply the induction hypothesis to the tail. $\square$

Part (ii) is the crucial rigidity: the chain machine has no shortcuts. To return to the base you must first descend all the way, and what remains is again a closed computation at the base.

**Theorem 4.3 (Realisation Theorem).** For every $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$,
$$D(C_S, 0) \;=\; \langle S \rangle .$$

*Proof.* ($\supseteq$) It suffices to show $S \subseteq D(C_S,0)$, since the left side is a submonoid. For $s = 0$ this is trivial. For $s = j+1 \in S$, the edge $0 \to j$ followed by the descent of Lemma 4.2(i) is a closed computation of length $j+1$.

($\subseteq$) Strong induction on $n$. The case $n = 0$ is trivial. Let $n = j+1$ and let $C_S^{(j+1)}\,0\,0$ be witnessed by a first edge $0 \to b$ followed by $C_S^{(j)}\,b\,0$. Since the source is $0$, the first edge must be of jump type, so $b+1 \in S$. By Lemma 4.2(ii), $b \le j$ and $C_S^{(j-b)}\,0\,0$, so $j - b \in \langle S\rangle$ by the induction hypothesis (note $j - b < j+1$). Then $n = (b+1) + (j-b)$ exhibits $n$ as a sum of an element of $S$ and an element of $\langle S\rangle$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.4.** For every additive submonoid $M \le \mathbb{N}$, $D(C_M, 0) = M$ (a submonoid generates itself).

**Corollary 4.5 (Completeness).** $\{\, M \le \mathbb{N} : \exists R, a,\ D(R,a) = M \,\}$ is the entire lattice of additive submonoids of $\mathbb{N}$.

Corollary 4.4 subsumes all of Examples 2.5 as the instances $S = \varnothing, \{1\}, \{n\}, \{2,3\}$ of a single theorem — an economy worth stressing, because the four values ($\bot$, $\top$, a proper progression, and a genuinely gapped monoid) are exactly the four qualitatively different regimes of the classification in Section 5.

**Corollary 4.6 (Lattice closure).** For any $R,S,a,b$ there exist machines realising $D(R,a) \cap D(S,b)$ and $D(R,a) \vee D(S,b)$.

## 5. Structure theory: period and gaps

**Definition 5.1 (Period).** $d(R,a) = \gcd D(R,a)$, the greatest common divisor of the set of closed-computation lengths (with the convention $\gcd\{0\} = 0$).

**Theorem 5.2 (Divisibility).** Every $n \in D(R,a)$ is a multiple of $d(R,a)$.

**Theorem 5.3 (Structure theorem).** There is $N$ such that every $m \ge N$ with $d(R,a) \mid m$ lies in $D(R,a)$.

*Proof sketch.* A submonoid $M \le \mathbb{N}$ with $\gcd M = d$ contains, by Bézout applied to finitely many elements, all sufficiently large multiples of $d$: choose $m_1,\dots,m_r \in M$ with $\gcd(m_1,\dots,m_r) = d$; every large multiple of $d$ is a nonnegative combination of them, e.g. by the coin-problem bound applied to $m_i/d$. $\square$

**Definition 5.4 (Gaps).** The gap set of a state is $G(R,a) = \{ n : d(R,a) \mid n \ \wedge\ n \notin D(R,a)\}$.

**Corollary 5.5 (Finiteness of gaps).** $G(R,a)$ is finite; indeed $G(R,a) \subseteq [0,N)$ for the $N$ of Theorem 5.3.

**Theorem 5.6 (Dichotomy).** $d(R,a) = 0$ iff $D(R,a) = \{0\}$ (the *dead* case). Otherwise $d(R,a) > 0$, all lengths are multiples of $d(R,a)$, and all sufficiently large multiples occur.

Thus a degree monoid is always "an arithmetic progression with finitely many holes", and up to the rescaling $n \mapsto n/d$ one may always assume $d = 1$, i.e. that the monoid is a *numerical semigroup* (a submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$ of finite complement) — or the trivial monoid $\{0\}$.

**Theorem 5.7 (Synchronisation law).** If $a$ and $b$ are live (each has a nonzero closed-computation length), then
$$d\big(R\times S, (a,b)\big) = \operatorname{lcm}\big(d(R,a),\, d(S,b)\big).$$

*Proof.* Write $d_1, d_2$ for the two periods, $L = \operatorname{lcm}(d_1,d_2) > 0$. By the product law, membership in the product's degree monoid means simultaneous membership, so every element of it is divisible by both $d_1$ and $d_2$, hence by $L$; taking gcd, $L$ divides the product's period. Conversely, by Theorem 5.3 pick $N_1, N_2$ beyond which large multiples of $d_1$, resp. $d_2$, are realised, and let $K > \max(N_1,N_2)$. Then $Lk \in D(R\times S,(a,b))$ for all $k \ge K$; in particular both $LK$ and $L(K+1)$ lie in it, so the product's period divides their difference $L$. Antisymmetry of divisibility finishes. $\square$

**Corollary 5.8 (Chinese-remainder corollary).** Two single-loop machines of lengths $m$ and $n$ run in lockstep return to their common initial state exactly at the multiples of $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$.

## 6. Determinism

**Definition 6.1.** $R$ is **deterministic** if every state has at most one successor: $R\,a\,b$ and $R\,a\,c$ imply $b=c$.

**Lemma 6.2 (Unique endpoints).** If $R$ is deterministic then for every $n$, $R^{(n)}\,a\,b$ and $R^{(n)}\,a\,c$ imply $b = c$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$: the two first edges agree by determinism, then apply the induction hypothesis. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Subtraction closure).** Let $R$ be deterministic and $m,n \in D(R,a)$ with $m \le n$. Then $n - m \in D(R,a)$.

*Proof.* Write $n = m + (n-m)$ and split the $n$-loop at time $m$ using Lemma 2.2: there is $b$ with $R^{(m)}\,a\,b$ and $R^{(n-m)}\,b\,a$. But also $R^{(m)}\,a\,a$, so $b = a$ by Lemma 6.2. Hence $R^{(n-m)}\,a\,a$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (Determinism forces a progression).** If $R$ is deterministic then there is $d$ with
$$D(R,a) = d\mathbb{N} = \{\, n : d \mid n \,\},$$
and this $d$ is the period $d(R,a)$.

*Proof.* If $D(R,a)$ has no nonzero element, take $d = 0$. Otherwise let $d$ be the least nonzero element. Repeated addition gives $d\mathbb{N} \subseteq D(R,a)$. Conversely let $n \in D(R,a)$. The multiple $d\lfloor n/d\rfloor$ lies in $D(R,a)$ and is $\le n$, so by Theorem 6.3 the remainder $n \bmod d = n - d\lfloor n/d \rfloor$ lies in $D(R,a)$; being $< d$, minimality of $d$ forces it to be $0$, i.e. $d \mid n$. Finally $d\mathbb{N} = D(R,a)$ has gcd $d$, so $d$ is the period. $\square$

This is precisely the Euclidean algorithm run inside the machine's own return times, made available by determinism.

**Corollary 6.5 (No gaps).** For deterministic $R$, $G(R,a) = \varnothing$: every multiple of the period is realised.

**Corollary 6.6 (Gaps certify nondeterminism).** If $d(R,a) \mid n$ and $n \notin D(R,a)$, then $R$ is not deterministic.

This is a *local* certificate for a *global* property: one missing return length, at one state, refutes determinism of the entire system, with no need to exhibit a branching state.

**Theorem 6.7 (Separation).** For no deterministic relation $R$ (on any state space) and no state $a$ is $D(R,a) = \langle 2,3\rangle$.

*Proof.* Suppose it were. By Theorem 6.4, $D(R,a) = d\mathbb{N}$. From $2,3 \in D(R,a)$ we get $d \mid 2$ and $d \mid 3$, whence $d \mid \gcd(2,3) = 1$, so $d = 1$ and $D(R,a) = \mathbb{N} \ni 1$. But $\langle 2,3\rangle = \{0\} \cup \{k : k \ge 2\}$ does not contain $1$ — contradiction. $\square$

Combined with Corollary 4.5 (and Theorem 6.9 below) this is a clean expressivity separation: **finite nondeterministic machines realise every value of the invariant; deterministic machines, of any size whatsoever, realise only the progressions $d\mathbb{N}$.** The obstruction is not resource-theoretic but arithmetic: a hole in an additively closed set.

**Theorem 6.8 (Period bound on finite state spaces).** Let $\alpha$ be finite with $|\alpha| = N$, let $R$ be deterministic, and let $a$ be live. Then $d(R,a) \le N$.

*Proof.* Put $d = d(R,a) > 0$; by Theorem 6.4, $R^{(d)}\,a\,a$. For each $i < d$ split the $d$-loop at time $i$ (Lemma 2.2) to obtain a state $s_i$ with $R^{(i)}\,a\,s_i$ and $R^{(d-i)}\,s_i\,a$. If $s_i = s_j$ for $i < j$, then concatenating $R^{(i)}\,a\,s_i$ with $R^{(d-j)}\,s_j\,a$ gives a closed computation of length $i + (d - j)$, which satisfies $0 < i + (d-j) < d$ — contradicting that every length is a positive multiple of $d$ or zero. Hence $i \mapsto s_i$ is injective on $\{0,\dots,d-1\}$ and $d \le N$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.9 (Finite-state realisation).** Every additive submonoid $M \le \mathbb{N}$ is the degree monoid of a state of a machine with finitely many states.

*Proof.* Every submonoid of $\mathbb{N}$ is finitely generated: choose a finite $T$ with $\langle T\rangle = M$ (for instance, if $M \ne \{0\}$ with period $d$ and threshold $N$ as in Theorem 5.3, the elements of $M$ below $N + d$ generate). Let $B = \max T$. Restrict the chain machine $C_T$ to the states $\{0,1,\dots,B\}$: no transition from a state $\le B$ leaves this range, because a jump from the base goes to $s-1 \le B - 1$ and a countdown decreases. The inclusion of $\{0,\dots,B\}$ into $\mathbb{N}$ is an injective step-preserving, step-reflecting map whose image is closed under transitions, so Theorem 3.2 applies and the truncated machine has the same degree monoid at the base as $C_T$, namely $\langle T\rangle = M$ by Theorem 4.3. $\square$

## 7. Two-loop machines: the exact gap count

Fix coprime $p,q > 1$ and consider the two-loop chain machine $C_{\{p,q\}}$. By Theorem 4.3 its base state has degree monoid the **numerical semigroup**
$$\langle p, q\rangle = \{\, ap + bq : a,b \in \mathbb{N} \,\},$$
and by Theorem 5.2 its period is $\gcd(p,q) = 1$: the machine is *aperiodic*, so its gap set is simply the complement $\mathbb{N} \setminus \langle p,q\rangle$, a finite set by Corollary 5.5. We determine it exactly.

**Lemma 7.1 (Representation form).** $n \in \langle p,q\rangle$ iff $n = ap + bq$ for some $a,b \in \mathbb{N}$.

**Lemma 7.2 (Existence of residues).** If $p > 0$ and $\gcd(q,p)=1$ then every $n$ admits $b < p$ with $bq \equiv n \pmod p$.

*Proof.* $q$ is invertible modulo $p$; take $b$ to be the representative in $[0,p)$ of $q^{-1} n \bmod p$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Residue criterion).** Let $\gcd(p,q)=1$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$, and let $b < p$ satisfy $bq \equiv n \pmod p$. Then
$$n \in \langle p,q\rangle \iff bq \le n.$$

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) $n - bq$ is a nonnegative multiple of $p$, say $ap$, so $n = ap + bq$. ($\Rightarrow$) If $n = a'p + b'q$ with $a',b' \ge 0$ then $b'q \equiv n \equiv bq \pmod p$ and $q$ invertible mod $p$ give $b' \equiv b \pmod p$; since $b < p \le b' + p$, we get $b' \ge b$, whence $n \ge b'q \ge bq$. $\square$

**Corollary 7.4 (Frobenius number).** $F := pq - p - q$ is the largest element of $\mathbb{N}\setminus\langle p,q\rangle$; every $n > F$ is representable.

*Proof.* First, $F \equiv -q \equiv (p-1)q \pmod p$ because $pq - p \equiv 0$, so the residue of $F$ is $b = p-1$; and $(p-1)q = pq - q > pq - p - q = F$, so $F \notin \langle p,q\rangle$ by Theorem 7.3. Second, let $n > F$ with residue $b \le p-1$, and suppose $bq > n$. Since $bq - n$ is a positive multiple of $p$, we get $bq - n \ge p$, hence
$$n \le bq - p \le (p-1)q - p = pq - q - p = F,$$
contradicting $n > F$. So $bq \le n$ and $n \in \langle p,q\rangle$ by Theorem 7.3. $\square$

**Theorem 7.5 (Symmetry).** For $0 \le n \le F$, exactly one of $n$, $F-n$ lies in $\langle p,q\rangle$.

*Proof.* Let $b < p$ be the residue of $n$, i.e. $bq \equiv n \pmod p$. Since $F \equiv -q \pmod p$, we have $F - n \equiv -q - bq = -(b+1)q \pmod p$, so the residue of $F-n$ is $b' = p-1-b$ (which indeed lies in $[0,p)$). Note the key identity
$$bq + b'q = (p-1)q = pq - q = F + p.$$
By Theorem 7.3 it suffices to show that exactly one of $bq \le n$ and $b'q \le F-n$ holds.

They cannot both hold: adding them gives $F + p = bq + b'q \le n + (F-n) = F$, absurd.

They cannot both fail: if $bq > n$ and $b'q > F - n$, then $bq - n$ and $b'q - (F-n)$ are both positive multiples of $p$ (each difference is $\equiv 0 \pmod p$ by choice of residues), hence each is $\ge p$; but their sum is $(bq + b'q) - F = p$, so $p \ge 2p$, absurd. $\square$

**Theorem 7.6 (Sylvester's gap count).** For coprime $p,q>1$ the two-loop machine misses exactly
$$\#\big(\mathbb{N}\setminus\langle p,q\rangle\big) \;=\; \frac{(p-1)(q-1)}{2}$$
computation lengths.

*Proof.* By Corollary 7.4 all gaps lie in $\{0,1,\dots,F\}$, a set of $F+1 = pq-p-q+1 = (p-1)(q-1)$ elements. By Theorem 7.5 the involution $n \mapsto F-n$ of this set has no fixed gaps and pairs each gap with a non-gap, so exactly half of the $F+1$ elements are gaps. $\square$

**Examples 7.7.** $(p,q)=(2,3)$: $F=1$, exactly $1$ gap, namely the length $1$ — the unique certificate of nondeterminism used in Theorem 6.7. $(3,5)$: $F=7$, exactly $4$ gaps ($1,2,4,7$). $(5,7)$: $F=23$, exactly $12$ gaps. $(7,11)$: $F=59$, exactly $30$ gaps.

The invariant of a two-loop machine is therefore completely explicit: period $1$, gaps confined to $[0, pq-p-q]$, in bijection under $n \mapsto F-n$ with the non-gaps in that interval, and numbering exactly $(p-1)(q-1)/2$.

## 8. Algorithms

The theory is effective for finitely presented machines. We record the three computations that matter.

**A. Return-length spectrum by Boolean matrix powers.** For a machine on $N$ states with adjacency matrix $A$ over the Boolean semiring, $n \in D(a)$ iff $(A^n)_{aa} = 1$. Iterating $A \mapsto A \odot A_{\text{step}}$ for $n = 0,\dots,L$ costs $O(L N^{3})$ Boolean operations (or $O(LN^3/w)$ with bitset rows). The sequence of matrices is eventually periodic, so the whole (infinite) spectrum is determined by a finite prefix — in fact, once the period $d$ and a threshold $N_0$ beyond which all multiples of $d$ occur are known, the spectrum is decided by table lookup below $N_0$ and by divisibility above.

**B. Period and gap extraction.** From a prefix of the spectrum, $d = \gcd$ of the observed nonzero lengths; the gaps are the multiples of $d$ in the prefix that are absent. By Theorem 5.3 this stabilises; by Corollary 6.5, if any gap appears, the machine is certified nondeterministic — a *sound* certificate, valid however the prefix was obtained.

**C. Membership and gap counting for $\langle p,q\rangle$.** By Theorem 7.3 membership of $n$ costs one modular inversion and one comparison: compute $b = (q^{-1} n) \bmod p$, return $bq \le n$. This is $O(\log p)$ arithmetic operations, exponentially better than dynamic programming over $[0,n]$. Counting gaps costs $O(1)$ by Theorem 7.6.

## 9. Discussion

**What the invariant is.** The degree monoid packages the "temporal skeleton" of a state. It is the unique nontrivial invariant one can extract from return times alone, and Corollary 4.5 says it is a *complete* invariant for that data: no further constraint holds universally beyond additivity and $0$-membership.

**Why the determinism theorem is the crux.** Many separations between deterministic and nondeterministic models are quantitative (state blow-up, time hierarchies). Theorem 6.7 is qualitative and absolute: the value $\langle 2,3\rangle$ is achieved by a $3$-state nondeterministic machine and by no deterministic machine at all, regardless of state-space cardinality, memory, or alphabet. Determinism, in the temporal dimension, is exactly *gaplessness* — closure of the return-time set under subtraction.

**The bridge to numerical semigroups.** Once loops are generators, achievable running times are numerical semigroups, and "which running times are impossible?" becomes the Frobenius problem while "how many are impossible?" becomes Sylvester's count. The dictionary is exact: coins $\leftrightarrow$ loops, unpayable amounts $\leftrightarrow$ unachievable running times, the Frobenius number $\leftrightarrow$ the last unachievable time, symmetry of the semigroup $\leftrightarrow$ a duality on the machine's timing diagram.

**Limits of the invariant.** The degree monoid is deliberately blind: it does not see which states are visited, nor liveness, nor fairness, nor anything about states other than $a$. Two machines can share every degree monoid and differ wildly. Its value is that within its narrow field of view the classification is total.

## 10. Future directions

The realisation problem is settled and the deterministic case is fully characterised. Three programmes suggest themselves.

**Determinisation cost of a numerical semigroup.** Determinism forces $D = d\mathbb{N}$, so any deterministic machine simulating a nondeterministic one must distort the invariant, and Theorem 3.6 tells us the shape of admissible distortions: rescaling by $k$ sends $M$ to the colon monoid $(M:k)$. Define the *determinisation cost* $c(M)$ as the least $k_0$ such that $(M:k)$ is gapless for **every** $k \ge k_0$. (The uniform quantifier is the right one: for individual $k$ gaplessness is erratic — e.g. $(\langle 3,5\rangle : 3) = \mathbb{N}$ while $(\langle 3,5\rangle : 4)$ still has the gap $1$.) For two-loop machines the cost is exactly the Frobenius number plus one.

> **Proposition 10.1.** For coprime $p,q>1$ with $F = pq-p-q$, one has $c(\langle p,q\rangle) = F+1$.
>
> *Proof.* If $k > F$ then $kn > F$ for every $n \ge 1$, so $kn \in \langle p,q\rangle$ by Corollary 7.4 and $(\langle p,q\rangle : k) = \mathbb{N}$, which is gapless. Conversely take $k = F$ (note $F \ge 1$): then $1 \notin (\langle p,q\rangle : F)$ because $F \notin \langle p,q\rangle$, while $nF > F$ lies in $\langle p,q\rangle$ for every $n \ge 2$. Hence $(\langle p,q\rangle : F) = \{0,2,3,4,\dots\} = \langle 2,3\rangle$, whose period is $1$ and which has the gap $1$. $\square$

The reformulation is the point: "speeding up a machine" becomes the algebraic operation $M \mapsto (M:k)$, so determinisation becomes a divisibility question inside the monoid lattice rather than a machine construction. Two questions remain open: the exact value of $c(M)$ for a general numerical semigroup (is it always the Frobenius number of $M$ plus one?), and the behaviour of the *erratic* set $\{k : (M:k) \text{ gapless}\}$ below the cost, which for $\langle 5,7\rangle$ already exhibits nontrivial structure.

**Multi-state and relative invariants.** Replace the single base state by a pair $(a,b)$ and record $\{n : R^{(n)}\,a\,b\}$ — a *module* over $D(R,a)$ rather than a monoid. The realisation problem for these relative invariants, and the corresponding determinism obstruction (relative gaps), is open and looks tractable by the same chain-machine technique.

**Quantitative gap theory for many loops.** Sylvester's count is exact for two loops. For $r \ge 3$ generators the gap count (the *genus* of the numerical semigroup) has no closed form, but machine-theoretic constraints — bounded out-degree, bounded state count — restrict which semigroups arise cheaply. Which numerical semigroups are realisable by machines with at most $N$ states and out-degree at most $2$? The chain machine gives an upper bound of $\max(\text{generators})$ states; how far from optimal is it?

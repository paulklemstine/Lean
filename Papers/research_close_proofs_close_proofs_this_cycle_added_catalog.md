# The Markov Basis of the 2×2×2 No-Three-Way Interaction Model: A Rank-One Lattice and Fiber Connectivity

## Abstract

We give a complete, self-contained treatment of the Markov basis of the
`2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way interaction model of algebraic statistics, the
foundational example introduced by Diaconis and Sturmfels (1998). A
`2 × 2 × 2` contingency table is an assignment of integer counts to the eight
cells indexed by `(i, j, k) ∈ {0,1}³`. The no-three-way interaction model fixes
all three families of two-dimensional margins. We prove three results. First,
the *legality* of the alternating move `M3(i,j,k) = (−1)^(i+j+k)`: adding any
integer multiple of `M3` to a table preserves every two-way margin. Second, the
*rank-one structure* of the move lattice: any two tables with identical margins
differ by exactly one integer multiple of `M3`, so the singleton `{M3}` is *the*
Markov basis. Third, the *Fundamental Theorem of Markov Bases* for this model:
any two non-negative tables with the same margins are joined by a walk of `±M3`
steps that remains non-negative throughout. The third result combines the
rank-one kernel computation with a discrete-convexity argument showing that the
non-negative locus on the move line is an integer interval. All results have
been formalized and machine-verified; the proof sketches below mirror the formal
development line for line. We close with the `2 × 2 × n` generalization as a
conjecture and the structural obstacle that distinguishes it.

**Keywords.** Algebraic statistics, Markov basis, contingency table, no-three-way
interaction, lattice ideal, Fundamental Theorem of Markov Bases, fiber
connectivity, discrete convexity.

**MSC.** 62R01 (algebraic statistics), 13P25 (applications of commutative
algebra to statistics), 05A99 (combinatorics), 60J10 (Markov chains).

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation: testing for three-way interaction

A central task in categorical data analysis is the *goodness-of-fit test*: does
an observed contingency table conform to a hypothesized model? For models given
by linear constraints on cell counts — the *log-linear* models — the exact
conditional test of Diaconis and Sturmfels fixes a vector of *sufficient
statistics* and asks whether the observed table is extreme relative to the
conditional distribution on the set of all tables sharing that statistic. This
set is called a **fiber**. Because fibers are typically far too large to
enumerate, one samples from them by a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure.

The validity of MCMC sampling rests on one indispensable property: the chain's
moves must connect every fiber, so that the chain is irreducible and its
stationary distribution is the intended conditional distribution. A set of moves
with this property — for *every* fiber simultaneously — is a **Markov basis**.
Diaconis and Sturmfels proved that a Markov basis always exists and corresponds
to a generating set of a certain toric (lattice) ideal; this is the
*Fundamental Theorem of Markov Bases*.

### 1.2 The model

The simplest model that genuinely exercises this machinery is the `2 × 2 × 2`
**no-three-way interaction model**. Its sufficient statistics are the three
families of two-dimensional margins. Decomposable (graphical) models admit
Markov bases of simple `2 × 2` swaps; the no-three-way model is the textbook
*first example beyond* the decomposable case, and its essential move is the
degree-four alternating move that touches all eight cells. This paper develops
that example in full and rigorous detail.

### 1.3 Contributions

1. A clean formulation of the model with explicit two-term margins
   (Section 2), avoiding `Finset.sum` so that all margin identities reduce to
   decidable arithmetic on concrete indices.
2. **Legality** (Theorem 3.1): `M3` lies in the kernel of the margin map.
3. **Rank-one lattice** (Theorem 3.2): the move lattice is generated by `M3`
   alone; `{M3}` is the Markov basis.
4. **Fiber connectivity** (Theorem 4.3): the Fundamental Theorem of Markov
   Bases for this model, via a reusable discrete-convexity bridge lemma
   (Theorem 4.2).
5. The `2 × 2 × n` generalization stated as a conjecture (Section 6).

All statements are formalized; the sketches below follow the formal proofs.

## 2. Definitions

Throughout, indices `i, j, k` range over `{0, 1}` (formally `Fin 2`), and counts
lie in the integers `ℤ`.

**Definition 2.1 (Table).** A `2 × 2 × 2` integer contingency table is a function

> `u : {0,1} × {0,1} × {0,1} → ℤ`,

written `u(i, j, k)`. The collection of all such tables is a free `ℤ`-module of
rank `8`, denoted `Table3`.

**Definition 2.2 (Two-way margins).** The three families of two-dimensional
margins are

> `m12(u)(i, j) = u(i, j, 0) + u(i, j, 1)`   (sum over `k`),
>
> `m13(u)(i, k) = u(i, 0, k) + u(i, 1, k)`   (sum over `j`),
>
> `m23(u)(j, k) = u(0, j, k) + u(1, j, k)`   (sum over `i`).

There are `4 + 4 + 4 = 12` margin values; they are not independent (each family
sums to the grand total), so the rank of the margin map is `7`, leaving a
move lattice of rank `8 − 7 = 1`, which Theorem 3.2 makes explicit.

**Definition 2.3 (Same fiber).** Tables `u` and `v` have the **same margins**,
written `SameMargins(u, v)`, when

> `m12(u) = m12(v)`,  `m13(u) = m13(v)`,  and  `m23(u) = m23(v)`

as functions (i.e. for all relevant index pairs).

**Definition 2.4 (Alternating move).** The **alternating Markov move** is the
table

> `M3(i, j, k) = (−1)^(i+j+k) = +1 if i + j + k is even,  −1 if odd.`

`M3` takes only the values `±1` (formally `M3_apply_eq`).

**Definition 2.5 (Non-negativity and steps).** A table `u` is **non-negative**,
`Nonneg(u)`, when `u(i,j,k) ≥ 0` for all `i, j, k`. A single **legal step** from
`u` to `v`, written `Step(u, v)`, holds when both `u` and `v` are non-negative
and `v = u + M3` or `v = u − M3`. Tables `u` and `v` are **connected**,
`Connected(u, v)`, when there is a finite walk of legal steps from `u` to `v`;
formally `Connected` is the reflexive–transitive closure of `Step`.

## 3. Legality and the rank-one move lattice

### 3.1 The move preserves margins

**Theorem 3.1 (Legality; `noThreeWay_move_preserves_margins`).**
For every table `u` and every integer `t`,

> `SameMargins(u, u + t·M3).`

*Proof sketch.* Each margin is a two-term sum over a line in which exactly one
coordinate varies. Flipping that coordinate flips the parity of `i + j + k`, so
the two cells on any such line carry opposite signs of `M3`, namely `+1` and
`−1`. Hence the contribution of `t·M3` to that margin is `t·(+1) + t·(−1) = 0`.
This holds for all twelve margins. Formally, one splits on the two free indices
of each margin family (`fin_cases`), reduces the `if` in `M3` on the now-concrete
indices, and closes by `ring`. ∎

Theorem 3.1 says precisely that `M3` lies in the **kernel** of the linear margin
map `Table3 → ℤ¹²`. It is therefore a *legal* move: it never disturbs the
conditioned statistics.

### 3.2 The move lattice is rank one

**Theorem 3.2 (Rank-one lattice = Markov basis; `noThreeWay_kernel`).**
If `SameMargins(u, v)` then

> `v = u + (v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0))·M3.`

Consequently the kernel of the margin map is the rank-one lattice `ℤ·M3`, and
the singleton `{M3}` generates the entire move lattice: it is *the* Markov basis
of the model.

*Proof sketch.* Let `w = v − u`. By Theorem 3.1's converse content, all twelve
two-way margins of `w` vanish. We show `w(i,j,k) = (−1)^(i+j+k)·w(0,0,0)`,
i.e. `w = w(0,0,0)·M3`. Vanishing margins propagate sign flips: from
`w(0,0,0) + w(0,0,1) = m12(w)(0,0) = 0` we get `w(0,0,1) = −w(0,0,0)`; from
`w(0,0,0) + w(0,1,0) = m13(w)(0,0) = 0` we get `w(0,1,0) = −w(0,0,0)`; and so on
across the cube. Each of the eight cells is forced to the value
`(−1)^(i+j+k)·w(0,0,0)`. Since `w(0,0,0) = v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)`, this is the
claim. Formally one instantiates all twelve margin equations as hypotheses, then
`funext` and `fin_cases` over the three indices produce eight scalar goals, each
closed by `omega`. ∎

The dimension count of Definition 2.2 is thus realized concretely: the move
lattice is exactly `ℤ·M3`, a single line in the rank-eight grid of tables. This
is what it means to say the Markov basis is a *single* move.

## 4. Fiber connectivity (Fundamental Theorem for the model)

Theorem 3.2 is an algebraic statement: any two equal-margin tables differ by a
multiple of `M3`. MCMC needs a *dynamical* statement: a walk between them whose
intermediate tables are all valid (non-negative). The bridge is discrete
convexity.

### 4.1 Pointwise form of a multiple move

**Lemma 4.1 (`add_smul_M3_apply`).** For all `u, t, i, j, k`,

> `(u + t·M3)(i,j,k) = u(i,j,k) + t·M3(i,j,k).`

This is immediate from the pointwise definition of module operations on
functions, and is the workhorse for the inductive bound below.

### 4.2 The discrete-convexity walk

**Theorem 4.2 (Monotone walk along the move line; `connected_add_smul`).**
Let `u` be a table and `t` an integer. If both `u` and `u + t·M3` are
non-negative, then `Connected(u, u + t·M3)`.

*Proof sketch.* Induct on `n = |t|` (the natural-number absolute value of `t`).

- *Base `n = 0`.* Then `t = 0`, so `u + t·M3 = u` and the empty walk connects
  `u` to itself.
- *Inductive step.* Split on the sign of `t`.
  - If `t > 0`, take one step `u → u + M3`. This step is legal: `u` is
    non-negative by hypothesis, and `u + M3` is non-negative because for each
    cell where `M3 = −1` the far endpoint value
    `u(i,j,k) + t·M3(i,j,k) = u(i,j,k) − t ≥ 0` forces `u(i,j,k) ≥ t ≥ 1`, so
    subtracting `1` keeps the count `≥ 0`; where `M3 = +1` the value only
    increases. Now `u + M3` and `(u + M3) + (t−1)·M3 = u + t·M3` are both
    non-negative, and `|t − 1| = n − 1`, so the induction hypothesis connects
    `u + M3` to `u + t·M3`. Prepending the first step gives the walk.
  - If `t < 0`, symmetrically take the step `u → u − M3` and recurse with
    `t + 1`.

The key quantitative fact is the **discrete-convexity bound**: along the line
`u + s·M3` each cell is an affine (hence monotone) function of `s`, so if it is
non-negative at `s = 0` and at `s = t` it is non-negative for every integer `s`
between. The induction simply realizes this interval one unit step at a time.
Formally the proof inducts on `t.natAbs`, uses `Int.natAbs_eq_iff` to pick the
sign, establishes each `Step` by the bound above (`split_ifs` on `M3` then
`omega`), and assembles the walk with `Relation.ReflTransGen.head`. ∎

Theorem 4.2 is stated and proved in a *reusable* form: it is exactly the
statement that the non-negative locus on a `±1`-valued move line is an integer
interval, which recurs in connectivity arguments for richer models.

### 4.3 The Fundamental Theorem for this model

**Theorem 4.3 (Fiber connectivity; `noThreeWay_fiber_connected`).**
If `u` and `v` are non-negative tables with `SameMargins(u, v)`, then
`Connected(u, v)`. Equivalently, `{M3}` is a Markov basis: it connects every
fiber of the `2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way interaction model.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 3.2 there is an integer `t` (namely
`t = v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)`) with `v = u + t·M3`. Both `u` and `v` are
non-negative by hypothesis, so the hypotheses of Theorem 4.2 hold; it yields
`Connected(u, u + t·M3) = Connected(u, v)`. ∎

This is the Diaconis–Sturmfels Fundamental Theorem specialized to the model: the
*algebraic* generating set `{M3}` of the move lattice is also a *combinatorial*
connecting set for every fiber.

## 5. Algorithms

The constructive content of the theorems yields three small algorithms. We give
them in pseudocode here; the companion code provides type-hinted Python.

### 5.1 Margin computation

Compute the three families of two-way margins of a table in `O(1)` (eight cells,
twelve two-term sums). Used to test fiber membership: `SameMargins(u, v)` iff all
twelve values agree.

### 5.2 Lattice coordinate

Given two equal-margin tables `u, v`, return the single integer coordinate
`t = v(0,0,0) − u(0,0,0)` of their difference along `M3`. By Theorem 3.2,
`v = u + t·M3`; verifying `v − u = t·M3` cell-by-cell is an `O(1)` check.

### 5.3 Connecting walk

Given non-negative equal-margin tables `u, v`, produce the explicit walk
`u, u ± M3, u ± 2·M3, …, v`. Compute `t` as in 5.2; then take `|t|` unit steps in
the direction `sign(t)`. Theorem 4.2 certifies every intermediate table is
non-negative, so the walk is a valid MCMC trajectory. Complexity `O(|t|)`.

The same primitive, run with random `±1` steps and Metropolis rejection of
negative tables, is the MCMC sampler whose irreducibility Theorem 4.3
guarantees.

## 6. Generalization: the 2×2×n model (conjecture)

For the `2 × 2 × n` no-three-way model (a `TableN n` is
`{0,1} × {0,1} × {0,…,n−1} → ℤ`) the move lattice is **no longer rank one**.

**Conjecture 6.1.** A Markov basis of the `2 × 2 × n` no-three-way interaction
model consists of the `2 × 2 × 2` alternating moves on every pair of `k`-slices:
for each pair `k₁ < k₂`, the move that is `M3`-alternating on the two slices
`k₁, k₂` and zero elsewhere. The move lattice has rank `n − 1`.

The legality of each slice-pair move and the rank count follow the same margin
cancellation as Theorem 3.1. The hard part is connectivity: the single-line
discrete-convexity walk of Theorem 4.2 must be replaced by a multi-generator
argument that chooses, at each table, a slice-pair move that strictly reduces a
suitable distance to the target while preserving non-negativity. This is the
genuine combinatorial content of the general Fundamental Theorem of Markov
Bases, and is the principal direction for extending the present work.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 Why one move is the right answer

A Markov basis that fails to connect some fiber yields a *reducible* chain and a
silently biased test: the sampler reports an observed table as typical only
because it never reaches the tables that would contradict it. Theorem 4.3 is the
guarantee that the chain explores each fiber in full. That this guarantee is
delivered by a *single* explicit move makes the sampler trivial to implement
(flip a sign pattern, reject if negative) and trivial to audit.

### 7.2 The three worlds, in miniature

The development is a faithful miniature of the Diaconis–Sturmfels program, which
identifies three equivalent objects: a generating set of the toric ideal
(*algebra*), a generating set of the move lattice (*geometry*), and a connecting
set of moves for every fiber (*statistics*). Theorem 3.2 realizes the
algebra/geometry identification — the move lattice is `ℤ·M3`; Theorem 4.3
realizes the geometry/statistics identification — `{M3}` connects every fiber.
The `2 × 2 × 2` case is where all three coincide in their simplest non-trivial
form.

### 7.3 On the formalization

Two design choices kept the formal proofs short and robust. First, margins are
defined as explicit two-term sums rather than `Finset.sum`, so every margin
identity becomes decidable arithmetic once the indices are concrete. Second, the
connectivity induction is on `t.natAbs` (a natural number) rather than on the
integer `t`, giving a clean recursion in which the sign of `t` selects the
direction of the first step. The discrete-convexity bound is then a one-line
`omega` after `split_ifs` on the `±1` values of `M3`.

## 8. Conclusion

For the `2 × 2 × 2` no-three-way interaction model we have established that the
move lattice is the rank-one lattice generated by the alternating move `M3`
(Theorem 3.2), that this move is legal (Theorem 3.1), and that it connects every
fiber by non-negativity-preserving walks (Theorem 4.3) via a reusable
discrete-convexity lemma (Theorem 4.2). The result is the cleanest instance of
the Fundamental Theorem of Markov Bases: eight cells, twelve constraints, and
one checkerboard move that connects them all. The `2 × 2 × n` extension, where
the rank grows and connectivity becomes genuinely multi-generator, is the
natural next chapter.

## Abstract

Every primitive Pythagorean triple occurs exactly once in Berggren's ternary tree, rooted at
$(3,4,5)$; in Euclid coordinates the three generating moves are the linear maps
$B_1(m,n)=(2m-n,m)$, $B_2(m,n)=(2m+n,m)$, $B_3(m,n)=(m+2n,n)$ acting on seeds $(m,n)$ with
$m>n>0$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and $m\not\equiv n \pmod 2$. Embedding a seed in the hyperbolic
upper half-plane by $z(m,n)=(n+i)/m$ gives every triple a hyperbolic distance
$d(i,z(m,n))$ from the base point $i$, and the identity
$\cosh d(i,z(m,n)) = (m^2+n^2+1)/(2m)$ makes this distance an exact elementary function of
the seed. We determine the relationship between this metric quantity and the combinatorial
depth of a node completely.

We prove: (i) the *logarithmic window* $\log m \le d(i,z(m,n)) \le \log m + \log 2$, which
replaces the quadratic coordinate $\tfrac12\log c$ by the linear coordinate on which the
Berggren moves act; (ii) the existence of a *silver potential* $\Phi(m,n)=m+(\sqrt2-1)n$
satisfying $\Phi(B_i v)\le(1+\sqrt2)\Phi(v)$ for all three moves and every seed, with
equality for $B_2$, whence $\Phi \le (1+\sqrt2)^{k+1}$ at depth $k$; (iii) the sharp upper
envelope $d \le (k+1)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log 2$ at depth $k$, together with the matching lower
bound $d \ge (k+1)\log(1+\sqrt2)-\tfrac12\log 2$ along the Pell spine of pure middle moves,
so that the metric growth exponent of the tree is exactly $\log(1+\sqrt2)=0.88137\ldots$,
the logarithm of the silver ratio; (iv) a trichotomy for the three pure branches — rates
$0$, $\log(1+\sqrt2)$, $0$ for $B_1$, $B_2$, $B_3$ respectively — which refutes the natural
conjecture that the parabolic move $B_1$ is the sole obstruction to positive metric growth,
and shows that the value $\log 3$ suggested by the ternary branching is attained by no path
whatsoever; (v) the corrected two-sided law in terms of the labelling word $w$, namely
$(\#B_2(w)+1)\log 2 \le d \le (|w|+1)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log 2$, so that positive metric rate is
forced exactly by positive frequency of the middle move; and (vi) the consequent optimal
depth law: hypotenuse $N$ first occurs at depth $\log N/(2\log(1+\sqrt2)) + O(1)$, with the
extremal family given explicitly by the near-isoceles (Pell) triples.

**Keywords.** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, hyperbolic geometry, silver ratio, Pell
equation, growth exponent, Stern–Brocot dynamics.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The tree

A *primitive Pythagorean triple* is a triple $(a,b,c)$ of positive integers with
$a^2+b^2=c^2$ and $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. Euclid's parametrisation states that the primitive
triples with $b$ even are in bijection with the set of *seeds*
$$\mathcal S \;=\; \bigl\{(m,n)\in\mathbb Z_{>0}^2 \;:\; n<m,\;\gcd(m,n)=1,\; m\not\equiv n \!\!\pmod 2\bigr\},$$
the bijection being $(m,n)\mapsto(m^2-n^2,\,2mn,\,m^2+n^2)$. We write $c(m,n)=m^2+n^2$ for
the hypotenuse.

Berggren's theorem (1934) says that the set of primitive triples carries the structure of an
infinite ternary tree rooted at $(3,4,5)$, generated by three fixed integer matrices. In
seed coordinates those matrices conjugate to the three maps
$$B_1(m,n)=(2m-n,\,m),\qquad B_2(m,n)=(2m+n,\,m),\qquad B_3(m,n)=(m+2n,\,n),$$
each of which maps $\mathcal S$ into $\mathcal S$; the root is $(2,1)$, and every seed is
reached from the root by a unique finite word in $\{B_1,B_2,B_3\}$. We write
$\operatorname{depth}(m,n)$ for the length of that word, and say a node is *at depth $k$*.

### 1.2 The hyperbolic embedding

Let $\mathbb H = \{z\in\mathbb C : \operatorname{Im} z>0\}$ carry the hyperbolic metric of
curvature $-1$, for which
$$\cosh d(z_1,z_2) \;=\; 1 + \frac{|z_1-z_2|^2}{2\,\operatorname{Im}z_1\operatorname{Im}z_2},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad \cosh d(i, x+iy) \;=\; \frac{x^2+y^2+1}{2y}.$$
Define the *node map*
$$z(m,n) \;=\; \frac{n+i}{m} \in \mathbb H .$$
Substituting $x=n/m$, $y=1/m$ gives the *exact position identity*
$$\cosh d\bigl(i,z(m,n)\bigr) \;=\; \frac{m^2+n^2+1}{2m} \;=\; \frac{c(m,n)+1}{2m}. \tag{1.1}$$

Identity (1.1) is the engine of everything below. It already yields the *logarithmic
trajectory law* $d = \tfrac12\log c + O(1)$: a triple of hypotenuse $c$ sits at hyperbolic
distance essentially $\tfrac12\log c$ from the base point.

### 1.3 The question, and prior state

The tree carries two notions of size: the metric $d(i,z(m,n))$ and the combinatorial
$\operatorname{depth}(m,n)$. They are not equivalent. The pure-$B_1$ branch
$(2,1)\to(3,2)\to(4,3)\to\cdots$ reaches hypotenuse $c=2k^2+6k+5$ only at depth $k\approx
\sqrt{c/2}$, so depth is *not* $O(\log c)$; the metric, by (1.1), always is.

Prior work in this program established one-sided comparisons. From the crude estimate
$m \le 2\cdot 3^k$ at depth $k$ one gets $2d \le \log 32 + k\log 9$, i.e.
$d \lesssim k\log 3$; and the pure-$B_2$ branch gives a matching *existence* statement
$d \ge k\log 2$. This left an unresolved gap $[\log 2,\log 3]$, and prompted the following
conjecture, which we call **(H2)**:

> *Along every infinite Berggren path which uses the move $B_1$ at most a fraction
> $1-\delta$ of the time, $d(i,z_k)/k$ converges to a limit in $[\delta\log 2,\log 3]$, the
> extreme value $\log 3$ being attained by the pure-$B_3$ path.*

The present paper closes the gap, identifies the exponent exactly, and refutes (H2) in both
of its halves.

### 1.4 Results

Throughout, $\lambda := 1+\sqrt2 = 2.41421356\ldots$ denotes the **silver ratio**, the
fundamental unit of $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]$ and the dominant eigenvalue of
$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$; $\log\lambda = 0.8813735870\ldots$.

- **Theorem A (logarithmic window).** For every $0<n<m$,
  $\log m \le d(i,z(m,n)) \le \log m + \log 2$.
- **Theorem B (silver potential).** $\Phi(m,n)=m+(\sqrt2-1)n$ satisfies
  $\Phi(B_i v)\le \lambda\Phi(v)$ for $i=1,2,3$ and every seed $v$, with equality for $i=2$;
  hence $\Phi \le \lambda^{k+1}$ at depth $k$, and $m \le \lambda^{k+1}$.
- **Theorem C (sharp envelope).** At depth $k$, $d \le (k+1)\log\lambda + \log 2$; and along
  the pure-$B_2$ (Pell) spine, $d \ge (k+1)\log\lambda - \tfrac12\log 2$. Consequently
  $d_k/k \to \log\lambda$ along that spine, while for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a depth
  beyond which every node satisfies $d/k \le \log\lambda + \varepsilon$.
- **Theorem D (trichotomy; refutation of (H2)).** The pure-$B_1$ and pure-$B_3$ branches
  both satisfy $d_k/k\to 0$; the pure-$B_2$ branch satisfies $d_k/k\to\log\lambda$. Since
  the pure-$B_3$ branch is $B_1$-free, (H2)'s lower bound fails at $\delta=1$; and since
  $\log\lambda<\log 3$ with a uniform gap, the rate $\log 3$ is attained by no path.
- **Theorem E (word sandwich).** For a word $w$ in the three moves reaching the node $z_w$,
  $$(\#B_2(w)+1)\log 2 \;\le\; d(i,z_w) \;\le\; (|w|+1)\log\lambda + \log 2 .$$
- **Theorem F (optimal depth).** A node of hypotenuse $\ge N$ has depth
  $\ge (\log N - \log 2)/(2\log\lambda) - 1$, and the Pell spine achieves hypotenuse $\ge N$
  at depth $\le (\log N+\log 2)/(2\log\lambda)$. Hence the minimal depth of hypotenuse $N$
  is $\log N/(2\log\lambda)+O(1)$.

## 2. The logarithmic window

### 2.1 Statement and proof

**Theorem A.** *Let $0<n<m$ be integers. Then*
$$\log m \;\le\; d\bigl(i,z(m,n)\bigr) \;\le\; \log m + \log 2 .$$

*Proof sketch.* Write $c=m^2+n^2$. Two elementary consequences of (1.1) are used.

*Lower bound.* Since $\cosh(\tfrac12\log c)=\tfrac12(\sqrt c + 1/\sqrt c)=(c+1)/(2\sqrt c)$
and $m\le\sqrt c$, identity (1.1) gives
$\cosh d = (c+1)/(2m) \ge (c+1)/(2\sqrt c) = \cosh(\tfrac12\log c)$, so
$d \ge \tfrac12\log c$ — a clean inequality with no additive constant. Now $m^2\le c$ gives
$\tfrac12\log c \ge \tfrac12\log(m^2)=\log m$.

*Upper bound.* Since $2\cosh d = e^{d}+e^{-d}\ge e^{d}$, we have
$d \le \log(2\cosh d) = \log\bigl((c+1)/m\bigr)$ by (1.1). It therefore suffices that
$c+1\le 2m^2$, i.e. $n^2+1\le m^2$, which holds because $n<m$ forces $m\ge n+1$ and hence
$m^2\ge n^2+2n+1 > n^2+1$. Taking logarithms,
$d \le \log\bigl(2m^2/m\bigr)=\log m+\log 2$. $\square$

### 2.2 Sharpness and interpretation

Both bounds are attained in the limit. For $n/m\to 0$ (e.g. the seeds $(m,1)$) one has
$c\sim m^2$ and $d = \log m + O(m^{-2})$: the lower bound is asymptotically exact. At the
other end, writing $t=n/m$ for the slope one has $\tfrac12\log c=\log m+\tfrac12\log(1+t^2)$,
and the refined analysis of the residual shows
$d-\tfrac12\log c = \tfrac12\log(1+t^2)+ (n^2/c^2)(1+O(1/c))$; hence
$$d-\log m \;=\; \log\bigl(1+t^2\bigr) + O(1/c),$$
which is bounded by $\log 2$ and approaches it exactly as $t\to1$. For the Pell seed
$(985,408)$, $t=0.41421$, $\log(1+t^2)=0.158362$ and $d-\log m=0.158347$.

The conceptual point of Theorem A is a change of coordinate. The previously used
observable, $\tfrac12\log c$, is a quadratic function of the seed and the Berggren moves act
on it nonlinearly. The observable $\log m$ is the logarithm of a *linear* functional, and
the Berggren moves are *linear maps*. Hence the growth problem becomes: how fast can the
first coordinate of a vector grow under products of the three matrices
$$M_1=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
M_2=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
M_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},$$
restricted to the cone $\{0<n<m\}$? This is a *joint spectral radius* problem on a cone, and
Theorem B solves it by exhibiting a common Barabanov-type potential.

## 3. The silver potential and the sharp envelope

### 3.1 The potential

**Definition.** The *silver potential* of a pair $(m,n)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^2$ is
$$\Phi(m,n) \;=\; m + (\sqrt2-1)\,n .$$

$\Phi$ is the positive left eigenvector of $M_2$: solving $(x,y)M_2 = \lambda(x,y)$ with
$\lambda=1+\sqrt2$ gives $y/x=\sqrt2-1$. Two elementary comparisons will be used
repeatedly, both immediate from $0\le \sqrt2-1<1$ and $n\le m$:
$$m \;\le\; \Phi(m,n) \;\le\; \sqrt2\, m \qquad (0\le n\le m). \tag{3.1}$$
(The right inequality: $\Phi = m+(\sqrt2-1)n \le m + (\sqrt2-1)m=\sqrt2 m$.)

**Theorem B (step inequalities).** *For every seed $(m,n)$:*
$$\Phi(B_1(m,n)) \;=\; \lambda m - n \;\le\; \lambda\Phi(m,n),$$
$$\Phi(B_2(m,n)) \;=\; \lambda m + n \;=\; \lambda\Phi(m,n),$$
$$\Phi(B_3(m,n)) \;=\; m + \lambda n \;\le\; \lambda\Phi(m,n).$$

*Proof.* Note $\lambda(\sqrt2-1)=(1+\sqrt2)(\sqrt2-1)=1$, so
$\lambda\Phi(m,n)=\lambda m + n$. Then:
$\Phi(2m-n,m)=2m-n+(\sqrt2-1)m=\lambda m-n$, which is $\le \lambda m+n$ since $n\ge 0$, with
deficit $2n$.
$\Phi(2m+n,m)=2m+n+(\sqrt2-1)m=\lambda m+n$, an identity.
$\Phi(m+2n,n)=m+2n+(\sqrt2-1)n=m+\lambda n$, and
$\lambda m+n-(m+\lambda n)=(\lambda-1)(m-n)=\sqrt2(m-n)\ge0$ exactly because $n<m$. $\square$

Three remarks. First, the constant $\lambda$ cannot be lowered: $B_2$ realises it with
equality on every seed. Second, the seed condition $n<m$ is used exactly once, in the
$B_3$ case, and it is used essentially: on the cone $\{n>m\}$ the map $B_3$ would expand
$\Phi$ faster than $\lambda$. Third, $\Phi$ is a *common* Lyapunov function for the three
maps, which is why a single scalar induction suffices where the eigenvalue analysis of
individual matrices does not (each $M_i$ separately has spectral radius $1+\sqrt2$, $1+\sqrt2$
and $1$; but products can in principle beat the maximum of the individual radii, and ruling
that out is exactly what a common potential does).

### 3.2 Depth bound

**Corollary B$'$.** *If $v$ is at depth $k$ then $\Phi(v)\le \lambda^{k+1}$ and hence
$m(v)\le\lambda^{k+1}$; along the pure-$B_2$ spine $\Phi = \lambda^{k+1}$ exactly.*

*Proof.* Induction on $k$. At the root, $\Phi(2,1)=2+(\sqrt2-1)=1+\sqrt2=\lambda=\lambda^{0+1}$.
The inductive step is Theorem B together with $\lambda>0$. Equality along the pure-$B_2$
branch is the equality case of Theorem B. The bound $m\le\Phi$ is (3.1). $\square$

### 3.3 The envelope

**Theorem C(i) (sharp upper envelope).** *Every node at depth $k$ satisfies*
$$d(i,z) \;\le\; (k+1)\log\lambda + \log 2 .$$

*Proof.* Theorem A gives $d \le \log m + \log 2$; Corollary B$'$ gives
$\log m \le (k+1)\log\lambda$. $\square$

**Theorem C(ii) (matching lower bound on the Pell spine).** *Let $v_k = B_2^{\,k}(2,1)$, so
that $v_0=(2,1)$, $v_1=(5,2)$, $v_2=(12,5)$, $v_3=(29,12),\ldots$ (consecutive Pell numbers).
Then*
$$d(i, z(v_k)) \;\ge\; (k+1)\log\lambda - \tfrac12\log 2 .$$

*Proof.* By Corollary B$'$, $\Phi(v_k)=\lambda^{k+1}$; by (3.1), $\Phi(v_k)\le \sqrt2\, m(v_k)$,
so $m(v_k)\ge \lambda^{k+1}/\sqrt2$. Theorem A's lower bound gives
$d\ge\log m \ge (k+1)\log\lambda - \tfrac12\log 2$. $\square$

**Theorem C(iii) (the growth exponent).** *With $d_k := d(i,z(v_k))$ along the Pell spine,*
$$\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{d_k}{k} \;=\; \log\lambda \;=\; \log(1+\sqrt2)\;=\;0.8813735870\ldots,$$
*and for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $K$ such that every node at depth $k\ge K$ satisfies
$d/k \le \log\lambda+\varepsilon$.*

*Proof.* The two-sided estimate
$(k+1)\log\lambda - \tfrac12\log2 \le d_k \le (k+1)\log\lambda+\log2$ divided by $k$ gives
$$\log\lambda + \frac{\log\lambda-\tfrac12\log2}{k} \;\le\; \frac{d_k}{k} \;\le\;
\log\lambda + \frac{\log\lambda+\log2}{k},$$
and both outer terms tend to $\log\lambda$; a squeeze finishes. For the uniform statement,
choose $K > (\log\lambda+\log2)/\varepsilon$; then for $k\ge K$, Theorem C(i) gives
$d/k \le \log\lambda + (\log\lambda+\log 2)/k \le \log\lambda+\varepsilon$. $\square$

The Pell spine is the family of *near-isoceles* triples: $v_k$ corresponds to
$(3,4,5),(21,20,29),(119,120,169),(697,696,985),(4059,4060,5741),\ldots$, whose legs differ
by exactly $1$ and whose hypotenuses satisfy the Pell recursion $c_{k+1}=6c_k-c_{k-1}$.

**Corollary (no path attains $\log3$).** *Since $\log\lambda<\log3$, there is $K$ such that
every node at depth $k\ge K$ satisfies $d/k<\log3$, with the uniform gap
$\tfrac12(\log 3-\log\lambda)=0.1086\ldots$.* Apply Theorem C(iii) with
$\varepsilon=\tfrac12(\log3-\log\lambda)$.

## 4. The trichotomy of pure branches, and the refutation of (H2)

### 4.1 The two stagnant branches

**Proposition 4.1.** *The pure-$B_3$ branch from the root consists of the seeds
$(2k+2,1)$ at depth $k$, and the pure-$B_1$ branch of the seeds $(k+2,k+1)$ at depth $k$.*

*Proof.* $B_3(m,1)=(m+2,1)$ and $B_3^{\,k}(2,1)=(2k+2,1)$; $B_1(a+1,a)=(a+2,a+1)$ and
$B_1^{\,k}(2,1)=(k+2,k+1)$. $\square$

Both families grow *linearly* in the depth. By Theorem A their distances are therefore
$$d_k^{(3)} \in \bigl[\log(2k+2),\,\log(2k+2)+\log2\bigr],\qquad
d_k^{(1)} \in \bigl[\log(k+2),\,\log(k+2)+\log2\bigr].$$

**Theorem D(i).** *$d_k^{(1)}/k \to 0$ and $d_k^{(3)}/k \to 0$ as $k\to\infty$.*

*Proof sketch.* It suffices that $\log(ak+b)/k \to 0$ for $a>0$, $b\ge0$. Write
$$\frac{\log(ak+b)}{k} \;=\; \frac{\log(ak+b)}{ak+b}\cdot\frac{ak+b}{k}.$$
The first factor tends to $0$ (the standard limit $\log x/x\to0$ composed with
$k\mapsto ak+b\to\infty$), the second to $a$; the product tends to $0$. Sandwiching
$0 \le d_k/k \le (\log(ak+b)+\log2)/k$ concludes. $\square$

**Theorem D(ii) (trichotomy).** *The three one-generator branches have metric rates*
$$B_1: \;0, \qquad B_2:\;\log(1+\sqrt2), \qquad B_3:\;0 .$$

Combining Theorems C(iii) and D(i).

### 4.2 Why (H2) fails

Conjecture (H2) rested on a plausible heuristic. In the slope coordinate $t=n/m$ the three
moves act as Möbius transformations
$$B_1: t\mapsto \frac{1}{2-t},\qquad B_2: t\mapsto\frac{1}{2+t},\qquad
B_3: t\mapsto\frac{t}{1+2t},$$
mapping $(0,1)$ into itself. The map $B_1$ is *parabolic*, with the single fixed point
$t=1$; iterating it drives $t\to1$ while the seed entries grow only by $+1$ each step, which
is exactly the source of the depth-versus-distance mismatch observed in the earliest
analysis of the tree. The conjecture guessed that this parabolicity was the *unique*
obstruction.

It is not. The map $B_3$ is also parabolic, with fixed point $t=0$, and the pure-$B_3$
branch — which uses $B_1$ never — stagnates just as badly. Only $B_2$, whose fixed point
$t=\sqrt2-1$ is a *hyperbolic* fixed point with multiplier $\lambda^{-2}$, expands.

**Theorem D(iii) (explicit refutation).** *For every $\delta>0$ and every $K$ there exists
$k\ge K$ with $k>0$ such that the node $(2k+2,1)$ is a $B_1$-free node at depth $k$ with
$d/k<\delta$.* Immediate from Theorem D(i).

**Theorem D(iv).** *The value $\log 3$ is attained by no infinite path: there is an explicit
$K$ such that $d/k<\log3$ for every node at depth $k\ge K$.* This is the Corollary of §3.3.

Thus both halves of (H2) — the lower bound $\delta\log2$ for $B_1$-sparse paths and the
extremal value $\log 3$ for the pure-$B_3$ path — are false.

## 5. The corrected law: middle-move frequency

The refutation suggests the right statistic. Let $w = a_\ell\cdots a_1$ be a word over
$\{B_1,B_2,B_3\}$, let $z_w$ denote the node it produces from the root, let $|w|=\ell$, and
let $\#B_2(w)$ be the number of middle letters.

**Lemma 5.1 (the middle move is the only universal expander).** *For every word $w$,*
$$m(z_w) \;\ge\; 2^{\#B_2(w)+1}.$$

*Proof.* Induction on $|w|$. At the root, $m=2=2^{0+1}$. If the last applied letter is
$B_1$, then $m \mapsto 2m-n > m$ since $n<m$; if $B_3$, then $m\mapsto m+2n>m$; in both
cases $\#B_2$ is unchanged and $m$ does not decrease. If the letter is $B_2$, then
$m\mapsto 2m+n \ge 2m$ and $\#B_2$ increases by one, so the bound doubles as required.
$\square$

**Theorem E (word sandwich).** *For every word $w$,*
$$\bigl(\#B_2(w)+1\bigr)\log 2 \;\le\; d(i,z_w) \;\le\; \bigl(|w|+1\bigr)\log\lambda + \log 2 .$$

*Proof.* Lower bound: Theorem A gives $d\ge\log m$, and Lemma 5.1 gives
$\log m \ge (\#B_2(w)+1)\log 2$. Upper bound: the word has length $|w|$, so the node is at
depth $|w|$, and Theorem C(i) applies. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (rate from frequency).** *If $|w|=\ell>0$ and $\#B_2(w)\ge\alpha\ell$, then*
$$\frac{d(i,z_w)}{\ell} \;\ge\; \alpha\log 2 .$$

So a path travels at a positive metric rate **iff** it plays the middle move with positive
frequency. The corrected statement replaces (H2)'s "non-parabolic frequency" by
"middle-move frequency", and both sides of the sandwich are attained: the lower bound in
order of magnitude by words that are pure $B_2$ interleaved with long parabolic runs, the
upper bound exactly (up to the additive constant) by the pure-$B_2$ word.

The gap between the two constants $\log 2$ and $\log\lambda$ in Theorem E is intrinsic to a
statement that only counts letters: a $B_2$ step multiplies $m$ by $2+t$ where $t=n/m$ is
the current slope, and $2+t$ ranges over $(2,3)$ with the silver value $2+(\sqrt2-1)=\lambda$
attained exactly at the $B_2$-invariant slope. A word-counting bound must therefore use the
worst case $2$; only a dynamical average over the slope recovers $\lambda$.

## 6. The optimal depth of a hypotenuse

**Theorem F(i) (depth lower bound).** *If a node at depth $k$ has hypotenuse $c\ge N\ge1$
then*
$$k \;\ge\; \frac{\log N-\log 2}{2\log\lambda}-1 .$$

*Proof.* By Corollary B$'$, $m\le\lambda^{k+1}$, and $n\le m$, so
$c=m^2+n^2 \le 2\lambda^{2k+2}$. Hence $N\le 2\lambda^{2k+2}$; take logarithms and solve for
$k$. $\square$

**Theorem F(ii) (matching construction).** *For every $N\ge1$ there is $k$ with
$c(v_k)\ge N$ along the Pell spine and*
$$k \;\le\; \frac{\log N + \log 2}{2\log\lambda}.$$

*Proof.* Along the Pell spine $m(v_k)\ge\lambda^{k+1}/\sqrt2$ (proof of Theorem C(ii)), so
$c(v_k)\ge m(v_k)^2\ge \lambda^{2k+2}/2$. Take $k=\lceil X\rceil$ with
$X=(\log N+\log2)/(2\log\lambda)-1$; then $\lambda^{2k+2}/2\ge N$, and
$\lceil X\rceil \le \max(0, X+1) \le (\log N+\log2)/(2\log\lambda)$. $\square$

**Corollary F(iii) (optimal depth law).** *The minimal depth at which a node of hypotenuse
at least $N$ occurs equals*
$$\frac{\log N}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)} \;+\; O(1) \;=\; 0.567297\ldots\times\log N + O(1),$$
*with the implied constant at most $1+\log2/(2\log\lambda) \le 1.394$, and the optimum
attained along the near-isoceles Pell family.*

This is the precise, constant-sharp form of the folklore statement that the Berggren tree
reaches hypotenuse $N$ in "$O(\log N)$ steps". It is worth stressing what it does *not* say:
it is a statement about the *shallowest* node of a given size, not about all of them. By
Proposition 4.1 the seed $(2k+2,1)$ has hypotenuse only $\Theta(k^2)$ at depth $k$, so the
*deepest* node of hypotenuse $N$ sits at depth $\Theta(\sqrt N)$. Depth is bounded below by
distance (up to constants) but not above.

## 7. Discussion

### 7.1 The joint-spectral-radius viewpoint

Theorems B and C together compute a joint spectral radius. Let
$\mathcal M=\{M_1,M_2,M_3\}$ be the three Berggren matrices and let $C=\{(m,n):0\le n\le m\}$
be the invariant cone. Then
$$\lim_{k\to\infty}\;\max\bigl\{\|M_{a_k}\cdots M_{a_1}v\|^{1/k} : a_i\in\{1,2,3\}\bigr\} = \lambda$$
for any $v$ in the interior of $C$, and the linear functional $\Phi$ is an *extremal norm*
on $C$ witnessing it: $\Phi\circ M_i \le \lambda\Phi$ pointwise on $C$, with equality for
$M_2$. Extremal norms for finite matrix families are in general not attainable by such
simple objects; here the arithmetic of $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]$ supplies one for free, because
$\lambda$ is a unit and the eigenvector $(1,\sqrt2-1)$ has both coordinates positive.

### 7.2 Slope dynamics

The metric analysis is, at bottom, a statement about the Möbius dynamics of the three maps
on the slope interval $(0,1)$, which are exactly the maps of the Stern–Brocot / continued
fraction algorithm adapted to the odd-even parity constraint. A Berggren path corresponds to
a continued-fraction expansion of the limiting slope; the multiplicative expansion of $m$
per step is $2-t$, $2+t$ or $1+2t$ for $B_1$, $B_2$, $B_3$ respectively, evaluated at the
*current* slope. The parabolic generators $B_1$ and $B_3$ push the slope to the endpoints
$1$ and $0$ of the interval, where the corresponding expansion factors degenerate to
$2-1=1$ and $1+0=1$: no growth. The generator $B_2$ pushes the slope to the interior
attracting point $\sqrt2-1$, where the factor is $2+(\sqrt2-1)=\lambda$: maximal growth. This
explains, in one sentence, why the spectrum of achievable rates is $[0,\log\lambda]$ and why
it is the middle move that carries all of it.

### 7.3 Consequences for the "geodesic factoring" programme

Because $\cosh d=(c+1)/(2m)$ and $m\le\sqrt c$, every node of hypotenuse $c$ sits at distance
$\tfrac12\log c + [0,\tfrac12\log2]$ from the base point. If $N$ is a sum of two squares in
two essentially different ways, $N=m_1^2+n_1^2=m_2^2+n_2^2$, then Euler's identity gives the
factorisation
$$\gcd(N,\,m_1m_2+n_1n_2)\cdot\gcd(N,\,m_1n_2+m_2n_1)=N ,$$
so a "collision" of two tree nodes at the same hypotenuse yields a complete splitting of $N$.
Both colliding nodes lie in a hyperbolic annulus of width $\log 2$ at radius
$\tfrac12\log N$: the *certificate* for a factorisation is geometrically short.

The present results explain why shortness of the certificate does not translate into a fast
search. Two independent obstructions apply. First, the ball of radius $R$ about $i$ contains
$\asymp e^{2R}$ nodes (two-sided bounds $e^{2R}/300$ and $4e^{2R}$ are available, the upper
one because $d\ge\tfrac12\log c$ forces $m,n\le e^R$ so that the nodes embed in a grid of
side $e^R+1$), so the ball of radius $\tfrac12\log N$ already contains $\asymp N$ candidates.
Second, Theorem F(iii) shows the shallowest route to hypotenuse $N$ has depth
$\asymp\log N$, but Proposition 4.1 shows most nodes of that size are far deeper: the tree
metric and the hyperbolic metric disagree by an unbounded factor, so neither can be used to
prune the other. The positive geometric core — an exact position identity, a sharp growth
exponent, an explicit extremal family — survives; the algorithmic hope does not.

### 7.4 Relation to earlier one-sided results

Theorem C(i) supersedes the previously known bound $d\le k\log3+\tfrac52\log2$, both in the
constant ($\log\lambda=0.8814$ against $\log3=1.0986$, a $20\%$ improvement) and in
optimality (the new constant is attained; the old one is not). Theorem D refutes the
conjectured extremal behaviour outright. Theorem E replaces the conjectured criterion for
positive rate (parabolic-move sparsity) by the correct one (middle-move density).

## 8. Algorithms

The results are effective, and three short procedures make them usable.

**(A1) Exact hyperbolic position.** Given a seed $(m,n)$, return
$d=\operatorname{arcosh}\bigl((m^2+n^2+1)/(2m)\bigr)$, and the certified window
$[\log m, \log m+\log2]$ containing it. Cost: $O(1)$ arithmetic operations; exact in the
sense that the argument of $\operatorname{arcosh}$ is a rational number computed without
error.

**(A2) Optimal-depth route to hypotenuse $N$.** Compute
$k=\lceil (\log N+\log2)/(2\log\lambda)-1\rceil$ and return the Pell seed
$v_k=B_2^{\,k}(2,1)$, computed by the linear recursion $m_{j+1}=2m_j+n_j$, $n_{j+1}=m_j$.
By Theorem F this is within an additive $O(1)$ of the minimum possible depth, and it costs
$O(k)$ big-integer additions, i.e. $O(\log N)$ steps with numbers of $O(\log N)$ bits.

**(A3) Certified rate bracket for a word.** Given a word $w$, run it from the root and
return the interval $[(\#B_2(w)+1)\log2,\;(|w|+1)\log\lambda+\log2]$ guaranteed by Theorem E,
together with the exact distance from (A1). Cost: $O(|w|)$ big-integer operations. Used as
a search heuristic, it identifies which prefixes of a word can possibly reach a target
radius.

## 9. Future work

Three lines are immediate.

1. **The full growth spectrum.** We conjecture that for every $r\in[0,\log(1+\sqrt2)]$ there
   is an infinite path with $d_k/k\to r$, that the limit exists whenever the letter
   frequencies converge, and that $r$ is a continuous strictly increasing function of the
   asymptotic frequency of $B_2$ along the path. Theorem E gives one-sided control
   ($r\ge\alpha\log2$ from frequency $\alpha$); the missing input is a Birkhoff average of
   $\log(2+t)$ against the invariant measure of the induced slope dynamics.
2. **The exact ball-count constant.** The counting function of nodes in the ball of radius
   $R$ is expected to be $\bigl((\pi+2)/(4\pi^2)\bigr)e^{2R}(1+o(1))$: the ball condition
   $2m\cosh R \ge m^2+n^2+1$ is a Euclidean disc condition in the $(m,n)$-plane, so the count
   is a Möbius-weighted lattice count over a circular sector with the coprime opposite-parity
   density $4/\pi^2$. Two-sided bounds of the correct exponential order are known; the
   constant requires the Möbius step and a boundary-layer estimate.
3. **A depth statistic from the slope.** Depth is not the sum of the ordinary partial
   quotients of the slope — the pure-$B_3$ and pure-$B_1$ spines force two incompatible
   normalising constants. We conjecture that the correct statistic is the digit sum of the
   *even* continued fraction $m/n = 2b_1 \pm 1/(2b_2\pm 1/\cdots)$, the expansion adapted to
   the semigroup generated by $x\mapsto x+2$ and $x\mapsto 2-1/x$ in the coordinate $x=m/n$,
   for which both parabolic branches cost one unit per step.

## 10. Summary

For the Berggren tree of primitive Pythagorean triples, embedded in the hyperbolic plane by
$z(m,n)=(n+i)/m$:

$$\cosh d\bigl(i,z(m,n)\bigr)=\frac{m^2+n^2+1}{2m},\qquad
\log m \le d \le \log m+\log2,$$
$$\Phi(m,n)=m+(\sqrt2-1)n,\qquad \Phi\circ B_i \le (1+\sqrt2)\,\Phi\ \ (i=1,2,3),\ \text{equality for } B_2,$$
$$d \le (k+1)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log2 \ \text{ at depth } k, \qquad
\frac{d_k}{k}\to\log(1+\sqrt2) \ \text{ on the Pell spine},$$
$$\bigl(\#B_2(w)+1\bigr)\log2 \le d(i,z_w)\le \bigl(|w|+1\bigr)\log(1+\sqrt2)+\log2,$$
$$\min\{\text{depth}: \text{hypotenuse}\ge N\} = \frac{\log N}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)}+O(1).$$

The metric growth exponent of the tree of right triangles is the logarithm of the silver
ratio, it is carried entirely by one of the three branching moves, and the other two — both
parabolic in the slope coordinate — contribute nothing at all.

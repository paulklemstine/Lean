# Stochastic Galois Theory over Finite Fields: An Exact First-Moment Dictionary Between Random Polynomials and Random Permutations

## Abstract

Over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$, the factorization type of a uniformly
random monic polynomial of degree $n$ is governed by the cycle type of the
Frobenius permutation acting on its roots. This furnishes a precise dictionary
between the arithmetic statistics of random polynomials and the combinatorial
statistics of random permutations of $n$ letters. We establish this
correspondence at the exact, finite level rather than merely asymptotically. We
prove two mirror first-moment identities: the total number of roots summed over
all $q^n$ monic degree-$n$ polynomials equals $q^n$ (so the expected number of
roots is exactly $1$), and the total number of fixed points summed over all
$n!$ permutations equals $n!$ (so the expected number of fixed points is
exactly $1$). Both rest on the same double-counting skeleton. On the transitive
end of the dictionary we prove that the number of $n$-cycles in the symmetric
group $S_n$ is exactly $(n-1)!$, whence the probability that a uniform
permutation is an $n$-cycle is exactly $1/n$; this is the exact combinatorial
value approached by the proportion of irreducible monic polynomials. Finally we
couple the two first-moment identities into a single cross-domain equation valid
for every finite commutative ring and every $n \ge 1$. We discuss the failure
of the naive "full symmetric Galois group" heuristic over finite fields, where
all Galois groups are cyclic, and we outline a program extending the
correspondence from first moments to the full Poisson limiting distribution.

**Keywords:** finite fields, random polynomials, Frobenius permutation, cycle
type, symmetric group, fixed points, $n$-cycles, first moment, Poisson limit,
irreducibility.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The heuristic and its precise form

A recurring theme in arithmetic statistics is the slogan that *a random
polynomial behaves like a random permutation*. Over the field of rational
numbers, Hilbert's irreducibility theorem gives this slogan a definitive form:
a generic polynomial of degree $n$ has Galois group equal to the full symmetric
group $S_n$, so its splitting behaviour models a uniformly random element of
$S_n$. Over a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ the situation is at once more rigid
and more transparent. Every finite extension of $\mathbb{F}_q$ is Galois with a
*cyclic* Galois group, generated by the Frobenius automorphism
$\mathrm{Frob}: x \mapsto x^q$. Thus the Galois group of any polynomial over
$\mathbb{F}_q$ is cyclic, and the naive "the Galois group is $S_n$" statement is
simply false for $n \ge 3$.

What survives, and what is genuinely rich, is the correspondence at the level of
*cycle type*. The Frobenius automorphism permutes the roots of a monic
polynomial $f$, and the cycle type of this permutation records precisely the
degrees of the irreducible factors of $f$: a root in the base field is a fixed
point (a linear factor), an irreducible factor of degree $d$ is a $d$-cycle, and
$f$ is irreducible exactly when Frobenius is a single $n$-cycle. As $f$ ranges
uniformly over the $q^n$ monic polynomials of degree $n$, the cycle type of its
Frobenius permutation is distributed — asymptotically as $q \to \infty$ — like
the cycle type of a uniformly random element of $S_n$.

### 1.2 Contributions

This paper establishes the *first-moment* layer of this dictionary as a family
of exact, dimension-free identities, together with the exact top-degree
($n$-cycle) count.

1. **First moment of roots (arithmetic side).** For every finite commutative
   ring $K$ with $q = |K|$ and every $n \ge 1$, the total number of roots
   summed over all $q^n$ monic degree-$n$ polynomials is exactly $q^n$; the
   expected number of roots is $1$.
2. **First moment of fixed points (combinatorial side).** For every $n \ge 1$,
   the total number of fixed points summed over all $n!$ permutations of $n$
   letters is exactly $n!$; the expected number of fixed points is $1$.
3. **Exact $n$-cycle count.** The number of $n$-cycles in $S_n$ is exactly
   $(n-1)!$; equivalently a uniform permutation is an $n$-cycle with probability
   exactly $1/n$.
4. **The bridge.** A single equation couples identities (1) and (2), valid for
   every finite commutative ring and every $n \ge 1$, exhibiting both first
   moments as equal to $1$.

Each result is exact and holds for all parameters in range; none is merely
asymptotic. The proofs are elementary but structural: the two first-moment
identities share a single double-counting skeleton, and the $n$-cycle count is a
direct circular-arrangement count.

## 2. Setup and definitions

Throughout, $K$ denotes a finite commutative ring with $q := |K|$ elements; the
motivating case is a finite field $K = \mathbb{F}_q$. We write $S_n$ for the
symmetric group on the letters $\{1, \dots, n\}$ (formally, the group of
permutations of a fixed $n$-element set), and $n! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdots 1$ for
its order.

**Definition 2.1 (Monic evaluation).** A monic polynomial of degree $n$ over
$K$ is determined by its lower coefficient vector
$v = (v_0, \dots, v_{n-1}) \in K^n$, namely
$$f_v(x) = x^n + v_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \dots + v_1 x + v_0.$$
For $r \in K$ we write $\mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) := f_v(r)$ for the evaluation of
$f_v$ at $r$. There are exactly $q^n$ such polynomials, indexed by
$v \in K^n$.

**Definition 2.2 (Roots).** For a coefficient vector $v \in K^n$, the root set
is $\{\, r \in K : \mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) = 0 \,\}$, and the number of roots is its
cardinality. A root $r$ corresponds bijectively to a linear factor $(x - r)$ of
$f_v$.

**Definition 2.3 (Fixed points and cycle type).** For a permutation
$\sigma \in S_n$, a fixed point is a letter $i$ with $\sigma(i) = i$. The cycle
type of $\sigma$ is the multiset of lengths of its disjoint cycles. We say
$\sigma$ is an $n$-cycle if its cycle type is the singleton $\{n\}$, i.e. it
consists of a single cycle through all $n$ letters.

**Definition 2.4 (Frobenius correspondence).** Let $f$ be a monic polynomial of
degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ with distinct roots in a splitting field. The
Frobenius map $x \mapsto x^q$ restricts to a permutation of the multiset of
roots of $f$, and the cycle type of this permutation equals the multiset of
degrees of the monic irreducible factors of $f$. Under this correspondence:
fixed points $\leftrightarrow$ linear factors (roots in $\mathbb{F}_q$); and
$n$-cycles $\leftrightarrow$ irreducibility of $f$.

## 3. First-moment identities

### 3.1 The arithmetic side

**Theorem 3.1 (Total root incidences).** *Let $K$ be a finite commutative ring
with $q = |K|$, and let $n \ge 1$. Then*
$$\sum_{v \in K^n} \#\{\, r \in K : \mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) = 0 \,\} = q^n.$$
*Equivalently, the expected number of roots of a uniformly random monic
polynomial of degree $n$ is $\frac{1}{q^n}\cdot q^n = 1$.*

*Proof sketch.* Count the incidence set
$I = \{ (v, r) \in K^n \times K : \mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) = 0 \}$ in two ways. The
left-hand side of the identity is $\#I$ organized by first summing over $v$.
Reorganize by summing over $r$ instead:
$$\#I = \sum_{r \in K} \#\{\, v \in K^n : \mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) = 0 \,\}.$$
Fix $r \in K$. The condition $\mathrm{ev}_n(v, r) = 0$ reads
$r^n + v_{n-1}r^{n-1} + \dots + v_1 r + v_0 = 0$, which is a single (affine)
linear equation in the coefficients $v$; solving for $v_0$ shows that for any
choice of $v_1, \dots, v_{n-1}$ there is exactly one admissible $v_0$. Hence the
fiber over each $r$ has size $q^{n-1}$, and
$\#I = \sum_{r \in K} q^{n-1} = q \cdot q^{n-1} = q^n$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

The hypothesis $n \ge 1$ is essential: for $n = 0$ the only monic polynomial is
the constant $1$, which has no roots, so the sum is $0 \ne 1 = q^0$.

### 3.2 The combinatorial side

**Lemma 3.2 (Orbit–stabilizer fiber count).** *For every $n \ge 1$ and every
letter $i \in \{1, \dots, n\}$, the number of permutations $\sigma \in S_n$ with
$\sigma(i) = i$ is exactly $(n-1)!$.*

*Proof sketch.* The permutations fixing $i$ form the stabilizer of $i$ under the
natural action of $S_n$ on the $n$ letters. This action is transitive, so its
orbit has size $n$. By the orbit–stabilizer theorem,
$|\mathrm{orbit}| \cdot |\mathrm{stabilizer}| = |S_n| = n!$, hence
$|\mathrm{stabilizer}| = n!/n = (n-1)!$. Concretely, a permutation fixing $i$
freely permutes the remaining $n-1$ letters, and there are $(n-1)!$ ways to do
so. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 3.3 (Total fixed points).** *For every $n \ge 1$,*
$$\sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \#\{\, i : \sigma(i) = i \,\} = n!.$$
*Equivalently, the expected number of fixed points of a uniformly random
permutation of $n$ letters is $\frac{1}{n!}\cdot n! = 1$.*

*Proof sketch.* Count the incidence set
$J = \{ (\sigma, i) : \sigma(i) = i \}$ in two ways. Written as
$\sum_{\sigma} \#\{ i : \sigma(i) = i\}$ it is the left-hand side. Swapping the
order of summation (Fubini for finite sums),
$$\#J = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \#\{\, \sigma : \sigma(i) = i \,\} = \sum_{i=1}^n (n-1)!
= n \cdot (n-1)! = n!,$$
using Lemma 3.2 for each fiber. $\qquad\blacksquare$

As with Theorem 3.1, the hypothesis $n \ge 1$ is necessary: $S_0$ has a single
element (the empty permutation) with zero fixed points, so the sum is
$0 \ne 1 = 0!$.

### 3.3 The structural parallel

Theorems 3.1 and 3.3 are the same statement seen from opposite sides of the
Frobenius correspondence (Definition 2.4). Both are computed by double counting
an incidence relation; in both cases each fiber has the "expected" size
($q^{n-1}$ polynomials per field element; $(n-1)!$ permutations per letter), and
in both cases summing over the $q$ (resp. $n$) fibers returns the full total
$q^n$ (resp. $n!$). Dividing by the number of objects yields the common value
$1$ for the expectation. A root is a linear factor, a linear factor is a fixed
point of Frobenius, and the two enumerations coincide.

## 4. The transitive end: $n$-cycle statistics

Fixed points measure the *split* end of the factorization spectrum; the
opposite, *transitive* end is irreducibility, corresponding to a single
$n$-cycle.

**Theorem 4.1 (Exact $n$-cycle count).** *For every $n \ge 2$, the number of
permutations in $S_n$ whose cycle type is the singleton $\{n\}$ (i.e. the
$n$-cycles) is exactly $(n-1)!$.*

*Proof sketch.* An $n$-cycle is a cyclic arrangement of all $n$ letters. To
enumerate cyclic arrangements, fix the position of one distinguished letter to
break the rotational symmetry; the remaining $n-1$ letters may be arranged
around the circle in $(n-1)!$ linear orders, each giving a distinct $n$-cycle.
Hence there are $(n-1)!$ of them. (Equivalently, this is the standard cycle-type
enumeration: the number of permutations with a single $n$-cycle is
$n!/n = (n-1)!$.) $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 4.2 (The $1/n$ law for irreducibility).** *For every $n \ge 2$,*
$$\#\{\,\sigma \in S_n : \sigma \text{ is an } n\text{-cycle}\,\} \cdot n = n!.$$
*Equivalently, the probability that a uniformly random permutation of $n$
letters is an $n$-cycle is exactly $\frac{(n-1)!}{n!} = \frac{1}{n}$.*

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 4.1: $(n-1)! \cdot n = n!$ by the definition of
the factorial. Dividing by $n!$ gives probability $(n-1)!/n! = 1/n$.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

**Arithmetic shadow.** The constant $1/n$ is exactly the limiting proportion of
irreducible monic polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_q$. Indeed, by Gauss's formula
the number of monic irreducibles of degree $n$ over $\mathbb{F}_q$ is
$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d)\, q^{n/d} = \frac{q^n}{n}\bigl(1 + O(q^{-n/2})\bigr),$$
so the proportion among all $q^n$ monic polynomials is
$\frac{1}{n}(1 + O(q^{-n/2})) \to \frac{1}{n}$ as $q \to \infty$. The
combinatorial value $1/n$ from Theorem 4.2 is precisely the value this
arithmetic proportion approaches — the exact shadow cast by the $n$-cycle
fraction.

**The degree-2 slice.** For $n = 2$ the theorems give $(2-1)! = 1$
transposition among the $2! = 2$ permutations, a proportion of $1/2$. This
recovers the classical fact that the proportion of irreducible monic quadratics
over $\mathbb{F}_q$, namely $\frac{q^2 - q}{2}\big/ q^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1 - 1/q)$,
tends to $1/2$ as $q \to \infty$. One transposition, one-half.

## 5. The cross-domain bridge

The two first-moment identities can be fused into a single equation living in
both worlds simultaneously.

**Theorem 5.1 (First-moment bridge).** *Let $K$ be a finite commutative ring
with $q = |K|$, and let $n \ge 1$. Then*
$$\Bigl(\sum_{v \in K^n} \#\{ r \in K : \mathrm{ev}_n(v,r) = 0 \}\Bigr) \cdot n!
\;=\;
\Bigl(\sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \#\{ i : \sigma(i) = i \}\Bigr) \cdot q^n.$$
*Both sides equal $q^n \cdot n!$.*

*Proof.* By Theorem 3.1 the first parenthesized factor is $q^n$, so the
left-hand side is $q^n \cdot n!$. By Theorem 3.3 the second parenthesized factor
is $n!$, so the right-hand side is $n! \cdot q^n$. These are equal.
$\qquad\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** Dividing the bridge equation by $q^n \cdot n!$ yields
$$\underbrace{\frac{1}{q^n}\sum_{v} \#\{\text{roots}\}}_{\text{expected \# roots}}
=
\underbrace{\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma}\#\{\text{fixed points}\}}_{\text{expected \# fixed points}}
= 1.$$
The bridge certifies that two enumerations, computed by wholly different means in
number theory and combinatorics, are provably locked together for every finite
commutative ring and every degree $n \ge 1$. Note in particular that the result
holds at the generality of finite commutative rings — the root-incidence count
of Theorem 3.1 uses only the linear structure of the evaluation map, not the
field axioms — so the bridge specializes, for instance, to $K = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$
for any prime $p$ with $q = p$.

## 6. Algorithms

We record the elementary algorithms underlying the numerical verification of the
identities; all run in time polynomial in the number of objects enumerated.

**Algorithm A (Total-root census over $\mathbb{F}_q$).** Enumerate all $q^n$
coefficient vectors $v \in \mathbb{F}_q^n$; for each, count the field elements
$r$ with $f_v(r) = 0$ by direct evaluation; sum. The output equals $q^n$ by
Theorem 3.1. Complexity $O(q^{n+1} \cdot n)$ field operations.

**Algorithm B (Fixed-point census over $S_n$).** Enumerate all $n!$
permutations of $\{1, \dots, n\}$; for each, count indices $i$ with
$\sigma(i) = i$; sum. The output equals $n!$ by Theorem 3.3. Complexity
$O(n! \cdot n)$.

**Algorithm C ($n$-cycle census).** Enumerate all $n!$ permutations; for each,
test whether it consists of a single $n$-cycle (walk the orbit of one letter and
check it has length $n$); count. The output equals $(n-1)!$ by Theorem 4.1.
Complexity $O(n! \cdot n)$.

**Algorithm D (Bridge verification).** Compute the left factor via Algorithm A
and the right factor via Algorithm B, multiply by $n!$ and $q^n$ respectively,
and confirm equality with $q^n \cdot n!$. This directly checks Theorem 5.1.

## 7. Applications and context

- **Factorization statistics.** The dictionary transfers well-understood
  permutation statistics to predictions about polynomial factorization: expected
  number of linear factors ($=1$), expected number of degree-$d$ factors
  ($\to 1/d$), and the probability of irreducibility ($\to 1/n$).
- **Randomized algorithms.** The expected-root and irreducibility proportions
  underlie the analysis of algorithms that sample random polynomials — e.g. to
  find irreducible polynomials for constructing field extensions, where the
  $1/n$ hit rate governs the expected number of trials.
- **Arithmetic statistics pedagogy.** The correspondence is a clean, fully
  rigorous instance of a "function-field/permutation" analogy, exact at first
  moments and thus an ideal on-ramp to the deeper distributional results.

## 8. Discussion: what is true and what is false

It is worth emphasizing precisely which slogan holds. The statement "the Galois
group of a random polynomial over $\mathbb{F}_q$ is $S_n$" is **false**: finite
fields have cyclic absolute Galois groups, so every Galois group of a polynomial
over $\mathbb{F}_q$ is cyclic, and for $n \ge 3$ a single cyclic group cannot be
$S_n$. The correct and provable statement replaces the *group* by the
*conjugacy-class / cycle-type* data of the Frobenius element: as $q \to \infty$,
the cycle type of Frobenius equidistributes toward the cycle type of a uniform
element of $S_n$. The randomness is not internal to any single field extension
but arises from the uniform choice of polynomial. The results of this paper
capture the exact, non-asymptotic first-moment and top-degree instances of this
equidistribution.

## 9. Future directions

### 9.1 Exact second-moment matching

**Conjecture.** For every finite field of order $q$ and every degree $n \ge 1$,
the second moment of the number of linear factors of a uniformly random monic
degree-$n$ polynomial equals the second moment of the number of fixed points of
a uniformly random permutation of $n$ letters, up to an explicit finite
correction that vanishes as $q \to \infty$; in the limit both second moments
equal $2$, the value forced by the two leading falling factorials of the
Poisson$(1)$ law.

The key insight is that fixed points and roots are governed by the same
inclusion–exclusion skeleton: the $k$-th factorial moment counts ordered
$k$-tuples of distinct fixed points/roots, and each such tuple is "free" to
leading order, so the falling-factorial moments are all $1$ in the limit — the
signature of the Poisson$(1)$ distribution. The first-moment identity proved
here is exactly the $k = 1$ instance of this factorial-moment hierarchy, and its
double-counting proof extends verbatim to ordered tuples, making the second
moment the immediate next rung.

### 9.2 A Poisson limit law for low-degree factors

**Conjecture.** Fix $d \ge 1$. As $q \to \infty$, the number of monic
irreducible factors of degree $d$ of a uniformly random monic degree-$n$
polynomial converges in distribution to a Poisson random variable with mean
$1/d$, jointly and independently across distinct $d$, mirroring exactly the
Poisson limit for the number of $d$-cycles of a uniformly random permutation.

The Frobenius permutation transports the classical Goncharov–Kolchin cycle-count
limit for random permutations onto the polynomial side, with the degree-$d$
irreducible factors playing the role of the $d$-cycles and the mean $1/d$ being
precisely the $n$-independent proportion computed here for the top degree
$d = n$. The exact top-degree count $\#\{n\text{-cycles}\} \cdot n = n!$
(proportion $1/n$) is the $d = n$ case of the family of means $1/d$, making the
general low-degree Poisson law the natural next target.

## 10. Conclusion

We have made the "random polynomial $\approx$ random permutation" heuristic exact
at the level of first moments over finite fields (indeed over finite commutative
rings). The expected number of roots and the expected number of fixed points are
both exactly $1$; the proportion of $n$-cycles — the combinatorial shadow of
irreducibility — is exactly $1/n$; and a single bridge equation binds the
arithmetic and combinatorial enumerations for all parameters. These exact
identities form the base of a ladder whose higher rungs — matching second
moments and full Poisson limit laws — are the natural next steps toward a
complete distributional dictionary.

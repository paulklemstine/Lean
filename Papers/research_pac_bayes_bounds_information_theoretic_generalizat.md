## Abstract

This paper gives a finite, discrete account of the passage from information-theoretic PAC-Bayes bounds to compression-based generalization guarantees. A learning procedure is represented by a strictly positive joint distribution of a training sample $S$ and an output hypothesis $H$. Their local dependence is measured by information density, and its expectation is the mutual information $I(S;H)$. We prove that if a hypothesis description length $\ell(h)$ dominates the information density pointwise, then mutual information is at most the expected description length. Monotonicity of the square-root PAC-Bayes radius then transfers any bound of the form $g\le\sqrt{(I(S;H)+c)/(2n)}$ to an expected-description bound and, when all descriptions share a common upper bound, to a uniform-length bound. Finally, ordered description budgets produce ordered generalization radii: shorter descriptions imply no worse guarantees. The development separates the probabilistic derivation of an information-based premise from a deterministic transfer principle, clarifying precisely what compression must certify and how it affects the final bound.

## 1. Introduction

A learning algorithm transforms data into a hypothesis. Generalization theory asks how performance on the observed sample controls performance on the population. The challenge is dependence: the selected hypothesis was chosen after looking at the data, so ordinary fixed-hypothesis concentration cannot simply be applied without accounting for selection.

Information-theoretic approaches quantify this dependence directly. If the algorithm's output reveals little about the particular sample, selection has introduced limited data-specific information. PAC-Bayes methods similarly charge a complexity price for moving from a reference distribution to a data-dependent distribution. In many bounds the resulting contribution appears under a square root, together with a confidence term and divided by sample size.

Compression offers a concrete language for the same phenomenon. A model that admits a short honest description cannot encode unlimited detail about its training sample. This intuition is common, but it requires care. An arbitrary short label proves nothing if a decoder or side channel already contains the data. The relevant mathematical condition is that the assigned length dominate the information density generated by every sample-output pair.

We establish the complete transfer chain in finite spaces. Let $S$ range over a finite sample space $\mathcal S$ and $H$ over a finite hypothesis space $\mathcal H$. Given a strictly positive joint law, define

$$
i(s;h)=\log\frac{p(s,h)}{p_S(s)p_H(h)}
$$

and

$$
I(S;H)=\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)i(s;h).
$$

For a length function $\ell:\mathcal H\to\mathbb R$, the pointwise condition

$$
i(s;h)\le\ell(h)
$$

immediately implies

$$
I(S;H)\le\mathbb E[\ell(H)].
$$

That inequality, combined with monotonicity of $C\mapsto\sqrt{C/(2n)}$, transfers information-based bounds to description-based bounds. If $\ell(h)\le M$ uniformly, expectation disappears and the radius depends only on $M$. If $M_{\mathrm{short}}\le M_{\mathrm{long}}$, the short radius is no larger than the long radius.

The contribution is deliberately modular. We assume an information-theoretic PAC-Bayes premise and prove what follows from it. Deriving that premise from exponential moments, change of measure, a loss model, and a confidence parameter is a distinct probabilistic layer. This separation lets the compression argument apply to any setting that supplies the same square-root premise.

## 2. Finite learning model

### 2.1 Joint and marginal laws

Let $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal H$ be nonempty finite sets. A learner and its data-generating process induce a joint probability mass function

$$
p:\mathcal S\times\mathcal H\to(0,\infty)
$$

satisfying

$$
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}p(s,h)=1.
$$

The strict positivity assumption ensures that all logarithms and ratios below are ordinary real numbers. It can later be replaced by absolute-continuity conditions and extended-real conventions.

The marginal probabilities are

$$
p_S(s)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}p(s,h),
\qquad
p_H(h)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}p(s,h).
$$

Strict positivity of the joint law implies positivity of both marginals.

### 2.2 Information density and mutual information

**Definition 1 (Information density).** For $s\in\mathcal S$ and $h\in\mathcal H$, define

$$
i(s;h)=\log\left(\frac{p(s,h)}{p_S(s)p_H(h)}\right).
$$

The denominator is the probability that the pair would have under the product of its marginals. Thus $i(s;h)$ is the logarithmic departure from independence at a particular pair.

**Definition 2 (Mutual information).** The mutual information between $S$ and $H$ is

$$
I(S;H)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}
 p(s,h)i(s;h).
$$

Natural logarithms are used, so information is measured in nats. This is the expectation of information density under the joint law.

### 2.3 Description lengths

**Definition 3 (Description-length function).** A description-length function is a map $\ell:\mathcal H\to\mathbb R$. Its expected value under the learner's output is

$$
L_\ell=\mathbb E[\ell(H)]
=\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}p(s,h)\ell(h)
=\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}p_H(h)\ell(h).
$$

The abstract theorem allows real-valued lengths because logarithmic code costs naturally arise in nats. Concrete binary codes would usually use nonnegative integer lengths in bits; multiplying by $\log 2$ converts them to nats.

**Definition 4 (Pointwise domination).** A length function is information-dominating for the joint law if

$$
i(s;h)\le\ell(h)
$$

for every $s\in\mathcal S$ and $h\in\mathcal H$.

This condition is intentionally strong. One length is assigned to each hypothesis and must cover the largest information density that hypothesis can exhibit over all samples.

### 2.4 Generalization radius

**Definition 5 (Square-root complexity radius).** For a positive integer $n$ and a real complexity $C$ for which the expression is used, define

$$
R_n(C)=\sqrt{\frac{C}{2n}}.
$$

In applications $C$ is commonly $I(S;H)+c$, where $c$ is a nonnegative confidence penalty such as $\log(1/\delta)$. The transfer results below preserve an arbitrary common penalty $c$.

## 3. Compression controls mutual information

The central inequality follows from pointwise domination by averaging.

**Theorem 1 (Compression inequality).** Let $p$ be a strictly positive joint law on finite $\mathcal S\times\mathcal H$, and let $\ell:\mathcal H\to\mathbb R$ satisfy $i(s;h)\le\ell(h)$ for all $s,h$. Then

$$
I(S;H)\le L_\ell.
$$

**Proof.** For every pair $(s,h)$, positivity gives $p(s,h)\ge0$. Multiplying the assumed inequality by $p(s,h)$ therefore preserves its direction:

$$
p(s,h)i(s;h)\le p(s,h)\ell(h).
$$

Summing over $\mathcal S\times\mathcal H$ yields

$$
\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)i(s;h)
\le
\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)\ell(h).
$$

The left side is $I(S;H)$ and the right side is $L_\ell$. $\square$

The simplicity of the proof is informative. All substantive coding work is concentrated in proving pointwise domination. Once that certificate exists, the information bound is a consequence of positivity and linearity of expectation.

**Remark 1.** The theorem is not the assertion that every short description bounds mutual information. It is conditional on a precise inequality. In a coding-theoretic application, Kraft-type arguments, priors, or likelihood ratios would be used to justify the chosen $\ell$.

**Remark 2.** Since information density may be negative at some pairs, a dominating length need not be tight there. The expected inequality can consequently be conservative. The theorem favors transparent sufficient conditions over optimal average coding.

## 4. Transfer to PAC-Bayes radii

The next step is order-theoretic. For $n>0$, division by $2n$ and application of the square root preserve order.

**Lemma 1 (Monotonicity of the square-root radius).** Let $n$ be a positive integer. If $C_1\le C_2$, then

$$
R_n(C_1)\le R_n(C_2)
$$

whenever the displayed real square roots are considered.

**Proof sketch.** Since $2n>0$, one has $C_1/(2n)\le C_2/(2n)$. The real square-root function is monotone. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (Information radius is bounded by description radius).** Under the assumptions of Theorem 1, for every positive sample size $n$ and common confidence penalty $c$,

$$
R_n\bigl(I(S;H)+c\bigr)
\le
R_n\bigl(L_\ell+c\bigr).
$$

**Proof.** Theorem 1 gives $I(S;H)\le L_\ell$. Add $c$ to both sides and apply Lemma 1. $\square$

The theorem compares complexity radii but does not itself assert a statistical generalization premise. We now state the transfer exactly.

**Theorem 3 (Expected description-length generalization).** Let $g\in\mathbb R$ be a generalization gap. Suppose $n>0$, the pointwise domination condition holds, and an information-theoretic PAC-Bayes argument has established

$$
g\le R_n\bigl(I(S;H)+c\bigr).
$$

Then

$$
g\le R_n\bigl(L_\ell+c\bigr)
=
\sqrt{\frac{L_\ell+c}{2n}}.
$$

**Proof.** Chain the assumed inequality with Theorem 2:

$$
g\le R_n(I+c)\le R_n(L_\ell+c).
$$

$\square$

This theorem is the principal bridge. The premise may arise from any valid information-theoretic analysis. The conclusion replaces the potentially difficult quantity $I(S;H)$ by an expected model-description cost.

## 5. Uniform budgets and shorter descriptions

Expected length still depends on the output distribution. A uniform budget removes that dependence.

**Lemma 2 (Expected length is bounded by a uniform budget).** If $\ell(h)\le M$ for every $h\in\mathcal H$, then

$$
L_\ell\le M.
$$

**Proof.** Multiply $\ell(h)\le M$ by $p(s,h)\ge0$ and sum:

$$
L_\ell
\le
\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)M
=M\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)=M.
$$

$\square$

**Theorem 4 (Uniform description-length generalization).** Assume the hypotheses of Theorem 3 and additionally suppose $\ell(h)\le M$ for every $h$. Then

$$
g\le R_n(M+c)
=
\sqrt{\frac{M+c}{2n}}.
$$

**Proof.** Theorem 3 gives $g\le R_n(L_\ell+c)$. Lemma 2 gives $L_\ell+c\le M+c$, and Lemma 1 completes the proof. $\square$

The uniform result trades sharpness for simplicity. The hierarchy is

$$
I(S;H)\le L_\ell\le M,
$$

and therefore

$$
R_n(I+c)\le R_n(L_\ell+c)\le R_n(M+c).
$$

**Theorem 5 (Shorter-description monotonicity).** Let $M_{\mathrm{short}}$ and $M_{\mathrm{long}}$ satisfy

$$
\ell(h)\le M_{\mathrm{short}}
\quad\text{for all }h,
\qquad
M_{\mathrm{short}}\le M_{\mathrm{long}}.
$$

If the pointwise domination condition holds and

$$
g\le R_n\bigl(I(S;H)+c\bigr),
$$

then

$$
g\le R_n(M_{\mathrm{short}}+c)
\le R_n(M_{\mathrm{long}}+c).
$$

In particular,

$$
g\le R_n(M_{\mathrm{long}}+c).
$$

**Proof.** Apply Theorem 4 with $M=M_{\mathrm{short}}$. The order $M_{\mathrm{short}}\le M_{\mathrm{long}}$ and Lemma 1 give the second inequality. $\square$

This theorem formalizes “shorter descriptions imply better generalization” as monotonicity. It guarantees a no-larger radius. Strict improvement is not automatic: equality is possible if budgets coincide, if the square-root arguments coincide, or in degenerate regimes. A strict theorem would require strict budget reduction together with conditions ensuring that the relevant square-root arguments lie in a strictly monotone region.

## 6. Computational procedure

For a finite joint table, every quantity can be computed directly.

**Algorithm 1 (Information-to-description certificate).** Given an $m\times k$ positive probability table $p$, a safety margin $\eta\ge0$, sample size $n>0$, and confidence level $\delta\in(0,1)$:

1. Verify positivity and normalization of $p$.
2. Compute row marginals $p_S(s)$ and column marginals $p_H(h)$.
3. Compute each information density $i(s;h)$.
4. Assign
   $$
   \ell(h)=\max_{s\in\mathcal S}i(s;h)+\eta.
   $$
5. Compute $I=\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)i(s;h)$.
6. Compute $L=\sum_h p_H(h)\ell(h)$ and $M=\max_h\ell(h)$.
7. Set $c=\log(1/\delta)$ and report $R_n(I+c)$, $R_n(L+c)$, and $R_n(M+c)$.
8. Check the ordered chain $I\le L\le M$ and the corresponding radius chain.

The construction in step 4 guarantees pointwise domination by definition. Computing marginals and all densities takes $O(mk)$ arithmetic operations. The remaining reductions also take $O(mk)$ time, and storage can be $O(m+k)$ beyond the input if densities are streamed rather than retained.

### Numerical example

Consider

$$
p=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.24&0.10&0.06\\
0.08&0.20&0.32
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entries are positive and sum to one. Applying the procedure with $\eta=0.10$, $n=200$, and $\delta=0.05$ gives approximately

$$
I(S;H)=0.136368,
\qquad
L_\ell=0.461574,
\qquad
M=0.728609.
$$

With $c=\log 20$, the radii are

$$
R_{200}(I+c)=0.088489,
$$

$$
R_{200}(L_\ell+c)=0.092969,
$$

and

$$
R_{200}(M+c)=0.096493.
$$

The numbers illustrate the theoretical order. They also quantify the cost of replacing exact information first by expected length and then by a uniform maximum.

Holding complexity fixed, the radius scales as $n^{-1/2}$. In the example, the expected-length radius is approximately $0.185938$ at $n=50$, $0.092969$ at $n=200$, and $0.046485$ at $n=800$. Quadrupling sample size halves the radius.

## 7. Interpretation and applications

### 7.1 Data dependence rather than raw parameter count

A model may have many parameters yet depend weakly on a particular sample, or have few parameters yet encode sample-specific choices precisely. Mutual information targets dependence, not merely dimension. Description length offers an operational surrogate when a representation can be justified.

Potential descriptions include sparse supports and values, pruned tree structures, quantized weight vectors, low-rank factors, symbolic expressions, or indices into finite dictionaries. The transfer theorems are representation-agnostic. Their requirement is the pointwise inequality, not a particular compressor.

### 7.2 Expected versus worst-case compression

The expected bound $L_\ell$ can exploit the fact that long hypotheses are rarely selected. The uniform bound $M$ ignores this distributional advantage but is easier to certify. Thus the chain

$$
I\le L_\ell\le M
$$

is also a ladder of available information: exact joint dependence, output-weighted code cost, and worst-case code budget.

### 7.3 Confidence and sample size

For the common choice $c=\log(1/\delta)$, demanding greater confidence decreases $\delta$ and increases the radius only logarithmically inside the square root. Sample size appears linearly in the denominator inside the root. Description savings and confidence changes therefore interact additively in complexity but nonlinearly in the final radius.

### 7.4 What the result does not establish

The transfer theorem assumes the information-based gap inequality. It does not derive concentration, define a particular loss, or identify $g$ with a specific empirical-population risk difference. Those tasks belong to the probabilistic PAC-Bayes layer.

It also does not derive pointwise domination from prefix-free coding. A physical coding theorem must account for the decoder, shared side information, and units. Finally, strict positivity excludes zero-probability events. These boundaries are features of the present clean finite model, not claims about the limits of the broader method.

## 8. Discussion

The analysis isolates a reusable deterministic core. Once a statistical argument provides

$$
g\le\sqrt{\frac{I(S;H)+c}{2n}},
$$

and a compression argument provides

$$
i(s;h)\le\ell(h),
$$

the rest follows by weighted summation, normalization, and monotonicity. No additional probabilistic approximation is introduced during transfer.

The pointwise condition also explains why compression-based reasoning can be conservative. For each $h$, the length must dominate the worst information density across samples, even if that worst pair is rare. More refined methods might use sample-dependent codes, expected coding inequalities, or tail conditions. Such refinements could tighten $L_\ell$ while requiring more elaborate hypotheses.

The result nevertheless captures a robust principle: reducing a valid complexity certificate cannot worsen a monotone bound. Compression is useful not because file size is mystical, but because a justified description budget upper-bounds the information a learner's output can carry about its input.

## 9. Future work

Several extensions would deepen the theory.

First, the information-based premise should be derived from exponential-moment and change-of-measure arguments for explicit losses and confidence levels. Second, zero-probability events should be admitted using absolute continuity and extended-real relative entropy. Third, the finite joint table should be connected to random variables and Markov kernels on general probability spaces.

Fourth, binary prefix-free codes and Kraft's inequality should be introduced to derive information-dominating lengths from concrete coding assumptions. Fifth, randomized and deterministic learners should be constructed explicitly from data distributions and kernels. Sixth, expected and high-probability information bounds should be compared. Seventh, nondegeneracy conditions should be developed for strict rather than weak improvement. Finally, countable and standard Borel spaces would permit Radon--Nikodym derivatives and measure-theoretic mutual information.

## 10. Conclusion

For finite strictly positive learning systems, the compression-to-generalization chain is exact and transparent. Pointwise domination of information density implies

$$
I(S;H)\le\mathbb E[\ell(H)].
$$

Consequently, every information-based square-root PAC-Bayes premise transfers to an expected-description guarantee. A uniform length budget yields a simpler worst-case guarantee, and ordered budgets yield ordered radii. The complete hierarchy is

$$
g
\le R_n(I+c)
\le R_n(\mathbb E[\ell(H)]+c)
\le R_n(M+c).
$$

Thus a shorter valid description is not merely aesthetic: it supplies a no-worse generalization certificate, provided it honestly dominates the dependence between the training sample and the learned hypothesis.

## Appendix A. Structural consequences and edge cases

This appendix records several consequences that help interpret the main results without adding assumptions.

### A.1 Independence

If $S$ and $H$ are independent, then $p(s,h)=p_S(s)p_H(h)$ for every pair. Hence

$$
i(s;h)=\log 1=0
$$

pointwise and $I(S;H)=0$. Any nonnegative length function dominates information density. The information radius becomes

$$
R_n(c)=\sqrt{\frac{c}{2n}}.
$$

This is the baseline in which model selection carries no information about the realized sample. Description bounds can still be larger because they need not be zero; that gap reflects looseness in the chosen certificate rather than genuine dependence.

### A.2 Deterministic learners and positivity

A truly deterministic learner maps each sample to one hypothesis. Its induced joint law has zeros away from the graph of the map and therefore falls outside the strict-positivity model. One can approximate such a law by adding arbitrarily small randomization, but a complete exact treatment should permit zeros and formulate information density only where the joint law is absolutely continuous with respect to the product marginal. This observation explains why strict positivity is mathematically convenient but substantively restrictive.

### A.3 Units

Because the logarithm is natural, $i$, $I$, $L_\ell$, $M$, and $c$ are expressed in nats. If code lengths $b(h)$ are measured in bits, the compatible nat length is

$$
\ell(h)=b(h)\log 2.
$$

Mixing bit lengths directly with natural-log confidence penalties would be dimensionally inconsistent. Conversely, using base-two logarithms throughout expresses all information terms in bits and changes the constants in formulas derived with natural exponentials. A concrete application should choose one convention and retain it throughout.

### A.4 Tightness of the averaging step

The Compression Inequality becomes an equality exactly when the weighted slack sums to zero:

$$
\sum_{s,h}p(s,h)\bigl(\ell(h)-i(s;h)\bigr)=0.
$$

Under strict positivity and pointwise nonnegative slack, this occurs precisely when $\ell(h)=i(s;h)$ for every pair. Since $\ell(h)$ cannot vary with $s$, equality requires the information density associated with each fixed $h$ to be constant across samples. This is a demanding condition. Consequently, a hypothesis-only pointwise code can be substantially looser than mutual information.

### A.5 Sensitivity of the radius

For positive $C$, the derivative of $R_n(C)$ with respect to complexity is

$$
\frac{d}{dC}R_n(C)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2nC}}.
$$

The marginal benefit of reducing complexity is therefore larger when total complexity is small. With respect to sample size, treating $n$ continuously for interpretation,

$$
R_n(C)=\sqrt{\frac{C}{2}}n^{-1/2}.
$$

These formulas explain diminishing returns in both description reduction and data accumulation, while preserving monotonic improvement.

## Appendix B. An audit checklist for applications

To apply the transfer principle responsibly, the following items should be made explicit.

1. **Finite objects.** Specify the sample and hypothesis spaces and explain any discretization.
2. **Joint law.** State how the learner and data distribution induce $p(s,h)$.
3. **Support.** Verify strict positivity, or use a theory that handles zero probabilities.
4. **Units.** Use a common logarithm base for information, lengths, and confidence penalties.
5. **Description mechanism.** Account for the decoder and all shared side information.
6. **Pointwise certificate.** Establish $i(s;h)\le\ell(h)$ for every relevant pair, not merely on average.
7. **PAC-Bayes premise.** State the loss, gap, probability level, and theorem supplying $g\le R_n(I+c)$.
8. **Choice of summary.** Decide whether exact expected length $L_\ell$ or a uniform budget $M$ is appropriate.
9. **Numerical domain.** Ensure the square-root complexity is nonnegative in any numerical implementation.
10. **Claim strength.** Report weak monotonicity unless strictness assumptions are actually verified.

Following this checklist prevents a compression metaphor from being mistaken for a bound. It also makes clear where improvements can enter: a tighter probabilistic premise, a more faithful code, a smaller expected length, or a less conservative uniform budget.

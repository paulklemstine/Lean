## Abstract

We study a degree-one model of the connection between the Jacobian and Dixmier settings. Let

$$
F(X,Y)=(aX+bY+c,\ dX+eY+f)
$$

be an affine polynomial map with rational coefficients. Its polynomial Jacobian determinant is the constant $ae-bd$. If $x$ and $y$ belong to an arbitrary associative rational algebra, not assumed commutative, define

$$
X'=ax+by+c1,
\qquad
Y'=dx+ey+f1.
$$

We prove the universal identity

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)[y,x],
$$

where $[u,v]=uv-vu$. Consequently, every affine map with Jacobian determinant one carries each pair satisfying the Weyl relation $[y,x]=1$ to another pair satisfying the same relation. The proof rests only on bilinearity, alternation, and centrality of rational scalars. We also give the explicit affine inverse when $ae-bd\ne0$, formulate certification algorithms, discuss operator examples and computational demonstrations, and delineate the precise boundary between this affine theorem and the open nonlinear Jacobian and Dixmier conjectures.

## 1. Introduction

The Jacobian conjecture asks whether a polynomial map $F:\mathbb C^n\to\mathbb C^n$ whose Jacobian determinant is the constant $1$ must have a polynomial inverse. The condition implies local analytic invertibility everywhere, but the conjecture asks for a global algebraic conclusion. Despite the elementary formulation, the general problem remains open.

A second family of questions concerns endomorphisms of Weyl algebras, the noncommutative algebras generated by coordinate and differentiation operators. Their canonical generators satisfy a commutator relation. The expectation that suitable endomorphisms are automorphisms is known as the Dixmier conjecture. Classical research relates the Jacobian and Dixmier problems, suggesting that commutative derivative data and noncommutative commutator data are two manifestations of a common structure.

This paper isolates that structure in the affine plane. The result is deliberately limited but completely general within its scope: coefficients are rational, while the target algebra may be any associative unital rational algebra and may be highly noncommutative. The same scalar $ae-bd$ appears as the determinant of the derivative matrix and as the multiplier of the canonical commutator.

The principal statements are as follows.

1. The Jacobian determinant of $F(X,Y)=(aX+bY+c,dX+eY+f)$ equals $ae-bd$.
2. For affine substitutions $X'=ax+by+c1$ and $Y'=dx+ey+f1$ in any associative rational algebra,

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)[y,x].
$$

3. If $ae-bd=1$ and $[y,x]=1$, then $[Y',X']=1$.
4. If $ae-bd\ne0$, the affine map has an explicit affine polynomial inverse.

The first and fourth assertions are standard linear algebra. The second is the bridge: it identifies the commutative determinant with the scale factor governing a noncommutative relation. The third follows at once and is the affine Jacobian–Weyl preservation theorem.

The paper is self-contained. Section 2 introduces the algebraic objects. Sections 3 and 4 establish the commutative and noncommutative identities. Section 5 combines them and treats inversion. Section 6 explains algorithms and examples. Sections 7–9 discuss geometry, applications, limitations, and directions for extending the bridge beyond degree one.

## 2. Definitions and algebraic setting

### 2.1. Affine polynomial maps

Let $\mathbb Q[X,Y]$ be the commutative polynomial ring in two indeterminates. Fix coefficients $a,b,c,d,e,f\in\mathbb Q$ and define

$$
F_0(X,Y)=aX+bY+c,
\qquad
F_1(X,Y)=dX+eY+f.
$$

The associated affine polynomial map is

$$
F=(F_0,F_1):\mathbb A^2_{\mathbb Q}\longrightarrow\mathbb A^2_{\mathbb Q}.
$$

Its linear coefficient matrix and translation vector are

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\d&e\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
t=\begin{pmatrix}c\\f\end{pmatrix},
$$

so that $F(z)=Mz+t$ for a column vector $z=(X,Y)^{\mathsf T}$.

### 2.2. Jacobian matrix and determinant

For a polynomial map $G=(G_0,G_1)$, its Jacobian matrix is

$$
JG=
\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial G_0}{\partial X} & \dfrac{\partial G_0}{\partial Y}\\[6pt]
\dfrac{\partial G_1}{\partial X} & \dfrac{\partial G_1}{\partial Y}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The polynomial $\det JG$ is its Jacobian determinant. For an affine map, all entries of the Jacobian matrix are constant.

### 2.3. Associative rational algebras

An associative unital rational algebra $A$ is a ring with identity $1$ equipped with scalar multiplication by $\mathbb Q$, compatible with addition and multiplication. Elements of $A$ need not commute. Rational scalar multiples of $1$ are central, so for $q\in\mathbb Q$ and $u\in A$,

$$
(q1)u=u(q1)=qu.
$$

This centrality is the only coefficient property required in the commutator calculation.

### 2.4. Commutators and Weyl pairs

For $u,v\in A$, define the additive commutator

$$
[u,v]=uv-vu.
$$

It satisfies four elementary identities:

$$
[u+v,w]=[u,w]+[v,w],
$$

$$
[u,v+w]=[u,v]+[u,w],
$$

$$
[u,u]=0,
\qquad
[u,v]=-[v,u].
$$

It is also rational-bilinear. We adopt the orientation in which a **Weyl pair** is a pair $(x,y)\in A^2$ satisfying

$$
[y,x]=1.
$$

This orientation agrees with taking $x$ as multiplication by a variable and $y$ as differentiation.

### 2.5. Affine substitution in a noncommutative algebra

For $x,y\in A$, define

$$
X'=ax+by+c1,
\qquad
Y'=dx+ey+f1.
$$

No ordering convention is needed because these expressions have degree at most one. The coefficients are central, and each summand contains at most one of $x$ and $y$.

## 3. The commutative determinant calculation

We begin with the geometric side of the bridge.

**Theorem 3.1 (Affine Jacobian determinant).** Let

$$
F(X,Y)=(aX+bY+c,\ dX+eY+f)
$$

with rational coefficients. Then

$$
JF=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\d&e\end{pmatrix}
$$

and

$$
\det JF=ae-bd.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Formal differentiation gives

$$
\frac{\partial F_0}{\partial X}=a,
\quad
\frac{\partial F_0}{\partial Y}=b,
\quad
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial X}=d,
\quad
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial Y}=e.
$$

The constants $c$ and $f$ disappear. Taking the determinant of the resulting $2\times2$ matrix yields $ae-bd$. $\square$

The determinant has the usual geometric interpretation. Over $\mathbb R$, the linear part sends the standard basis vectors to $(a,d)^{\mathsf T}$ and $(b,e)^{\mathsf T}$, whose signed parallelogram area is $ae-bd$. Translation does not affect area. Thus the map scales oriented area by $ae-bd$.

**Corollary 3.2 (Unit-Jacobian criterion).** The affine map $F$ has Jacobian determinant one if and only if

$$
ae-bd=1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** This is immediate from Theorem 3.1. $\square$

The theorem is valid over any commutative coefficient ring in which the displayed derivatives and determinant are defined. Rational coefficients are retained here to match the noncommutative algebra setting used below.

## 4. The noncommutative commutator calculation

The noncommutative side has exactly the same coefficient determinant.

**Lemma 4.1 (Central translations do not affect commutators).** If $r,s$ are central elements of an associative ring and $u,v$ are arbitrary elements, then

$$
[u+r,v+s]=[u,v].
$$

**Proof sketch.** Expand by bilinearity:

$$
[u+r,v+s]=[u,v]+[u,s]+[r,v]+[r,s].
$$

Every term except $[u,v]$ vanishes because $r$ and $s$ commute with all elements. $\square$

**Lemma 4.2 (Alternating two-term expansion).** For rational scalars $a,b,d,e$ and elements $x,y$ of an associative rational algebra,

$$
[dx+ey,ax+by]=(ae-bd)[y,x].
$$

**Proof sketch.** Rational bilinearity gives

$$
[dx+ey,ax+by]
=da[x,x]+db[x,y]+ea[y,x]+eb[y,y].
$$

The diagonal terms vanish. Antisymmetry gives $[x,y]=-[y,x]$. Therefore

$$
[dx+ey,ax+by]
=-db[y,x]+ea[y,x]
=(ae-bd)[y,x].
$$

No commutativity between $x$ and $y$ is assumed. $\square$

Combining these lemmas yields the universal identity.

**Theorem 4.3 (Affine commutator-scaling theorem).** Let $A$ be any associative unital rational algebra, let $x,y\in A$, and let $a,b,c,d,e,f\in\mathbb Q$. Set

$$
X'=ax+by+c1,
\qquad
Y'=dx+ey+f1.
$$

Then

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)[y,x].
$$

**Proof sketch.** By Lemma 4.1, the central translations $c1$ and $f1$ can be removed from the commutator. Lemma 4.2 then computes the remaining expression. $\square$

Several features deserve emphasis. First, the theorem is an identity, not merely an implication under the Weyl relation. Second, it applies to every pair $x,y$, regardless of the value of $[y,x]$. Third, the target algebra is arbitrary apart from associativity, an identity, and its rational algebra structure. Finally, the translation coefficients do not enter the scale factor.

**Corollary 4.4 (General scalar relation).** If $[y,x]=\lambda1$ for some $\lambda\in\mathbb Q$, then

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)\lambda1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Substitute the assumed value of $[y,x]$ into Theorem 4.3. $\square$

Thus the transformation law applies not only to the normalized Weyl relation but to every central scalar commutator.

## 5. The affine Jacobian–Weyl bridge and inversion

We now identify the scalar in Theorem 4.3 with the commutative Jacobian determinant from Theorem 3.1.

**Theorem 5.1 (Affine Jacobian–Weyl bridge).** Let

$$
F(X,Y)=(aX+bY+c,\ dX+eY+f)
$$

be an affine polynomial map over $\mathbb Q$. Suppose $\det JF=1$. In any associative unital rational algebra, if $(x,y)$ is a Weyl pair satisfying $[y,x]=1$ and

$$
X'=ax+by+c1,
\qquad
Y'=dx+ey+f1,
$$

then $(X',Y')$ is again a Weyl pair:

$$
[Y',X']=1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Theorem 3.1 and the hypothesis imply $ae-bd=1$. Theorem 4.3 then gives

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)[y,x]=1\cdot1=1.
$$

$\square$

This is the principal bridge theorem. Its two halves use different algebraic environments. The Jacobian is computed in a commutative polynomial ring, while the commutator is computed in an arbitrary noncommutative algebra. The coefficient determinant transfers unchanged between them.

Unit determinant also gives an explicit inverse in the commutative affine setting.

**Theorem 5.2 (Explicit affine inverse).** Let $\Delta=ae-bd$. If $\Delta\ne0$, then $F$ is invertible and

$$
F^{-1}(U,V)=
\left(
\frac{e(U-c)-b(V-f)}{\Delta},
\frac{-d(U-c)+a(V-f)}{\Delta}
\right).
$$

In particular, if $\Delta=1$, the inverse is an affine polynomial map over $\mathbb Q$.

**Proof sketch.** The inverse matrix is

$$
M^{-1}=\frac1\Delta
\begin{pmatrix}e&-b\\-d&a\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Solving $(U,V)^{\mathsf T}=M(X,Y)^{\mathsf T}+t$ gives $(X,Y)^{\mathsf T}=M^{-1}((U,V)^{\mathsf T}-t)$, which is the displayed formula. Direct substitution in either order yields the identity map. $\square$

**Corollary 5.3 (Degree-one Jacobian conclusion).** Every affine polynomial map of the plane with Jacobian determinant one has an affine polynomial inverse.

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 5.2 with $\Delta=1$. $\square$

The inverse coefficient matrix also has determinant $1$. Accordingly, the reverse affine substitution preserves the Weyl relation as well. This shows that in degree one, unit-determinant affine transformations act as reversible changes of Weyl generators.

## 6. Algorithms and computational demonstrations

### 6.1. Determinant and relation certification

Given rational coefficients $a,b,c,d,e,f$, compute

$$
\Delta=ae-bd.
$$

The value $\Delta$ simultaneously answers two questions:

- the commutative map scales oriented area by $\Delta$;
- the noncommutative substitution scales $[y,x]$ by $\Delta$.

If $\Delta=1$, the procedure certifies both unit Jacobian and preservation of the Weyl relation. The computation uses two scalar multiplications and one subtraction, hence constant arithmetic complexity. If rational numbers are represented by arbitrary-precision integers, bit complexity depends on numerator and denominator lengths, but the number of rational operations remains constant.

### 6.2. Inverse construction

If $\Delta\ne0$, construct the inverse by the formula in Theorem 5.2. Evaluation of the forward or inverse map on a point requires a constant number of rational operations. Exact rational arithmetic avoids floating-point ambiguity in determinant-one tests.

### 6.3. Symbolic commutator coefficient

A compact symbolic computation can represent a linear expression by the coefficient triple $(p,q,r)$ corresponding to $px+qy+r1$. For two such expressions $L_1=(p,q,r)$ and $L_2=(s,t,u)$, bilinearity gives

$$
[L_1,L_2]=(qt-ps)[y,x],
$$

with orientation checked by expansion. Applied to $Y'=(d,e,f)$ and $X'=(a,b,c)$, the coefficient is $ea-db=ae-bd$. This representation computes the exact commutator scale without requiring a concrete noncommutative model.

### 6.4. Example with determinant one

Take

$$
a=2,\quad b=1,\quad c=5,\quad d=3,\quad e=2,\quad f=-4.
$$

Then

$$
\Delta=2\cdot2-1\cdot3=1.
$$

The map

$$
F(X,Y)=(2X+Y+5,\ 3X+2Y-4)
$$

has inverse

$$
F^{-1}(U,V)=(2(U-5)-(V+4),\ -3(U-5)+2(V+4)).
$$

For every Weyl pair,

$$
[3x+2y-4,\ 2x+y+5]=[y,x]=1.
$$

### 6.5. Example with nonunit determinant

Take

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Then $\Delta=6$. The map scales oriented area by $6$, and

$$
[3y,2x]=6[y,x].
$$

A Weyl pair is therefore sent to a pair with commutator $6$, not to a normalized Weyl pair. The map remains invertible because $\Delta\ne0$, but it does not preserve the chosen normalization.

### 6.6. Singular example

If the rows of $M$ are proportional, then $\Delta=0$. For example,

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The affine map collapses the plane onto a line and has no inverse. Simultaneously,

$$
[2x+4y,\ x+2y]=0
$$

for all $x,y$, even if the original pair is noncommutative. The same degeneracy destroys area and annihilates the two-dimensional commutator.

## 7. Geometric and operator interpretations

### 7.1. Alternating forms

The identity is best understood through alternation. In two dimensions, the determinant is the factor by which a linear map scales an alternating area form. The commutator is likewise alternating because $[u,u]=0$ and $[u,v]=-[v,u]$. Restricting the commutator to the rational span of $x$ and $y$ yields an alternating bilinear map. Therefore a change of basis with matrix $M$ must scale it by $\det M$.

The explicit calculation in Lemma 4.2 is precisely this general exterior-algebra principle written without additional machinery. The theorem is not an accidental cancellation: it is forced by the one-dimensional nature of alternating two-forms on a two-dimensional space.

### 7.2. Differential operators

Let $A$ be the algebra of rational-linear operators on $\mathbb Q[t]$. Define

$$
(xp)(t)=t p(t),
\qquad
(yp)(t)=p'(t).
$$

For every polynomial $p$,

$$
((yx-xy)p)(t)
=\frac{d}{dt}(tp(t))-t p'(t)
=p(t).
$$

Hence $[y,x]=1$. Theorem 5.1 says that every unit-determinant affine combination of these two operators is another Weyl pair. For example,

$$
X'=2x+y+5,
\qquad
Y'=3x+2y-4
$$

still satisfies $[Y',X']=1$.

Finite-dimensional matrices cannot realize this exact characteristic-zero relation: the trace of a commutator is zero, whereas the trace of the identity is the positive dimension. Infinite-dimensional operator algebras are therefore the natural concrete setting.

### 7.3. Classical and quantum transformation laws

A unit-determinant linear transformation preserves oriented area in a two-dimensional phase plane. Canonical commutation relations are a noncommutative counterpart of canonical coordinates. The affine bridge shows that the same matrix condition preserves both structures. This observation helps motivate why commutative Jacobian questions and noncommutative Weyl-algebra questions can be related, while making no claim that their full nonlinear theories are identical.

## 8. Structural reformulation via alternating bilinear maps

The bridge identity admits a coordinate-free formulation that explains both its rigidity and its two-dimensional character. Let $V$ be a two-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb Q$ with ordered basis $(v_1,v_2)$, and let $B:V\times V\to W$ be any alternating bilinear map into a rational vector space $W$. For a matrix

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\d&e\end{pmatrix},
$$

define

$$
v_1'=av_1+bv_2,
\qquad
v_2'=dv_1+ev_2.
$$

**Theorem 8.1 (Determinant law for alternating bilinear maps).** In this setting,

$$
B(v_2',v_1')=(ae-bd)B(v_2,v_1).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Bilinearity expands the left side into four terms. Alternation removes $B(v_1,v_1)$ and $B(v_2,v_2)$, while $B(v_1,v_2)=-B(v_2,v_1)$. The surviving coefficient is $ae-bd$. $\square$

The commutator theorem is obtained by taking $V$ to be the rational span of $x$ and $y$, taking $W$ to be the underlying rational vector space of the ambient algebra, and setting $B(u,v)=[u,v]$. Although the ambient algebra can be enormous, the relevant transformation occurs in a two-dimensional coefficient space.

The geometric determinant law is another instance: take $W=\mathbb Q$ and let $B$ be the oriented area form normalized by $B(v_2,v_1)$. Thus the two branches of the bridge are not merely parallel computations. They are specializations of one transformation law.

This viewpoint also predicts the higher-dimensional linear analogue. An alternating $n$-linear form transforms by the determinant of an $n\times n$ coefficient matrix. Pairwise commutators, however, naturally organize into an alternating matrix or two-form rather than an $n$-form. In higher-dimensional Weyl settings, preservation is therefore governed by a symplectic matrix equation, not solely by determinant one. In dimension two, the symplectic condition collapses to the determinant-one condition, which is why the present identity is exceptionally compact.

Translations fit naturally into this account. Affine space has no preferred origin, but its difference vectors form a vector space. Derivatives discard translation on the geometric side. Central constants disappear from commutators on the algebraic side. Both operations extract the same linear transformation $M$ from the affine data.

## 9. Scope, applications, and limitations

### 9.1. Composition and group structure

Affine substitutions compose according to matrix multiplication. If transformations with linear parts $M$ and $N$ have commutator scales $\det M$ and $\det N$, their composite has linear part $MN$ and scale

$$
\det(MN)=(\det M)(\det N).
$$

The commutator law reproduces this multiplicativity directly: the first substitution multiplies $[y,x]$ by $\det N$, and the second multiplies the result by $\det M$. In particular, determinant-one affine maps are closed under composition. Their inverses also have determinant one, so they form a group acting on Weyl pairs.

Translations form a normal subgroup within this affine group. They change the chosen generators by central constants but act trivially on the commutator. The linear determinant-one matrices carry the nontrivial mixing data. This decomposition separates the location-changing part of an affine transformation from the part that changes a two-dimensional frame.

For determinant $\Delta\ne0$, normalization can be restored after a nonunit transformation by an additional diagonal rescaling of determinant $\Delta^{-1}$. This does not mean the original map preserved the relation; rather, it identifies precisely the correction required. When $\Delta=0$, no such correction exists because both invertibility and the alternating component have been lost.

### 9.2. Applications and limitations

The theorem supplies a reliable base case for any attempt to extend Jacobian–Weyl correspondences to polynomial substitutions. It can be used as:

- a normalization test for affine changes of Weyl generators;
- a symbolic certificate that translations are irrelevant to the canonical relation;
- a consistency condition for higher-degree formulas, which must reduce to determinant scaling in degree one;
- an educational model of the connection between area forms and commutators;
- an exact computational routine for generating determinant-one examples and their inverses.

The result is universal over associative rational algebras, but narrow in polynomial degree and dimension. It does **not** prove the Jacobian conjecture beyond affine maps. It does **not** prove the Dixmier conjecture. It does **not** establish degree-three sufficiency, cubic-homogeneous reduction, or a general Jacobian–Dixmier implication. Those claims require substantial theory absent from the elementary identity proved here.

There is also a characteristic issue. Rational scalars ensure characteristic zero and central coefficient action. Analogues over other fields may hold at the affine identity level, but broader conjectural connections can change in positive characteristic. Over finite fields one must distinguish a polynomial as a formal expression from the function it induces on a finite set; injectivity, surjectivity, nonvanishing Jacobian, and existence of a polynomial inverse interact differently there.

## 10. Future research

Several extensions arise naturally.

First, one may define the first Weyl algebra directly by generators and the relation $[Y,X]=1$, then package a unit-determinant affine substitution as an algebra endomorphism. Combining the forward substitution with the explicit inverse matrix should prove that it is an automorphism.

Second, polynomial substitutions require normal-ordering formulas. For multiplication and differentiation operators, commutation with a generator behaves like formal differentiation. Establishing these identities systematically would permit finite-degree analogues of Theorem 4.3.

Third, stable equivalence operations should be studied: adjoining variables and composing with elementary automorphisms are central to reductions of polynomial maps. A precise account must track degree bounds and preservation of constant Jacobian conditions.

Fourth, cubic and Drużkowski-type reductions could be developed for finite-dimensional polynomial maps. Such work must state exactly which transformations preserve invertibility and which assumptions on the coefficient field are necessary.

Fifth, finite-field analogues deserve separate treatment. The affine determinant identity survives algebraically, but global statements about polynomial functions require careful reformulation.

## 11. Conclusion

For the affine map

$$
F(X,Y)=(aX+bY+c,\ dX+eY+f),
$$

the commutative Jacobian determinant is $ae-bd$. For the corresponding substitution in any associative rational algebra,

$$
X'=ax+by+c1,
\qquad
Y'=dx+ey+f1,
$$

the noncommutative commutator obeys

$$
[Y',X']=(ae-bd)[y,x].
$$

Therefore Jacobian determinant one preserves the Weyl relation, while nonzero determinant gives an explicit affine inverse. Both facts are governed by the transformation law for alternating two-dimensional structure. This complete degree-one calculation provides a precise, transparent bridge between commutative Jacobian geometry and noncommutative Weyl algebra, while leaving the nonlinear conjectures clearly outside its asserted scope.

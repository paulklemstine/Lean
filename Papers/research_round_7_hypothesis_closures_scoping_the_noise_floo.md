## Abstract

We give a rigorous, self-contained treatment of four structural probes of a semiprime $N = pq$ ($p \ne q$ prime), each of which was proposed as a route to a factor-revealing witness lying outside the known classification of numeric witnesses, and we close all four.

First, we study the **agreement set** $A(N) = \{a \in (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times : (a\mid p) = (a\mid q)\}$, an aggregate invariant under both the swap $p \leftrightarrow q$ and conjugation. We prove that $2|A(N)| = \varphi(N)$ exactly (for odd $p$), and that $A(N)$ coincides with the level set $\{a : J(a\mid N) = 1\}$ of the Jacobi symbol, an $N$-computable function. The aggregate therefore collapses onto the quadratic character modulo $N$ and carries no information beyond $\varphi(N)$.

Second, we determine the **zero-divisor graph** $\Gamma(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})$ completely: its vertex set splits into two wings of sizes $q-1$ and $p-1$, adjacency holds exactly across wings, so $\Gamma \cong K_{q-1,\,p-1}$; consequently $|V| = p + q - 2$ and $\deg(x) + 1$ is a prime factor of $N$ for every vertex $x$. We show that this structural witness collapses onto the numeric trace witness ($p$ and $q$ are the roots of $X^2 - (|V|+2)X + N$) and that it is circular: exhibiting one vertex already exhibits a factor, since $\gcd(x, N) \in \{p,q\}$.

Third, we prove a **noise-floor bound for atomic uniform primitives**: a single uniform query on $[1,N)$ reveals a factor with probability $(p+q-2)/N \le 2/p$, and $(p+q)^2 \ge 4N$ with equality iff $p = q$, so the floor is $\approx 2/\sqrt N$ for balanced semiprimes. We contrast this with the *correlated* sample set generated by the static rho map $x \mapsto x^2+1$, for which we prove unconditionally — by reduction-compatibility plus pigeonhole — that a factor-bearing pair exists among the first $p+1$ iterates. The density gain is real; we prove it is paid for by the aggregation cost $\binom{p+1}{2} \ge p$. The noise-floor principle is thereby scoped: it bounds atomic uniform primitives, not derived or correlated samples.

Fourth, we analyse the **digit-convolution relaxation**. Linearizing the base-$b$ digit equations by $w_{ij} = p_iq_j$ makes factorizations exactly the rank-one solutions; the determinant, the constraint distinguishing them, is quadratic and hence discarded. We exhibit an explicit constant-norm kernel vector — the carry commutator $C = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, with $\Phi_b(C) = 0$ for all $b$ and $\|C\|^2 = 2$ — and deduce that every target has a non-rank-one companion solution at squared distance at most $8$, uniformly in $N$. Short-vector search cannot isolate the factorization.

We close with a tropical reformulation: in min-plus coordinates the divisor hyperbola degenerates to a line whose corner sits at $\sqrt N$, and every divisor pair straddles the corner. All four closures are stated and proved for all semiprimes, not as heuristics.

**Keywords:** semiprime, Legendre symbol, Jacobi symbol, character orthogonality, zero-divisor graph, complete bipartite graph, Pollard rho, pigeonhole, lattice relaxation, Gaussian heuristic, tropical geometry.

## 1. Introduction

Let $N = pq$ with $p \ne q$ prime. A **witness** for $N$ is any datum from which $p$ and $q$ can be recovered in time polynomial in $\log N$. Classical examples are numeric: the trace $p+q$; the larger factor $\max(p,q)$; the value $\varphi(N) = (p-1)(q-1)$; the order of the unit group; a nontrivial square root of $1$ modulo $N$. All of these are equivalent to the factorization, in both directions, by elementary algebra: from $s = p + q$ and $N = pq$ one solves $X^2 - sX + N = 0$; from $\varphi(N)$ one recovers $s = N + 1 - \varphi(N)$; and so on.

The interesting question is not whether other witnesses exist but whether there exist witnesses of a *genuinely different type* — objects that are not numbers of this classified kind, and whose evaluation might therefore evade the barriers that block the numeric ones. Four candidates, each with a plausible case for evading the classification, are the subject of this paper.

**(A) Symmetric character aggregates.** The known obstructions include a symmetry barrier: a quantity computable from $N$ alone is necessarily invariant under $p \leftrightarrow q$; conversely, many natural probes fail because they are *not* invariant and hence cannot be evaluated. This suggests searching for a probe that *is* invariant under all available symmetries and nevertheless separates semiprimes of equal size. The agreement set of Legendre symbols is the canonical candidate.

**(B) Structural (non-numeric) witnesses.** The classification of numeric witnesses says nothing, on its face, about *combinatorial* objects. A graph attached to $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$ is not a number; if its isomorphism class determines $\{p,q\}$, then it is a witness outside the letter of the numeric classification.

**(C) Correlated samplers versus the noise floor.** A heuristic "noise-floor principle" asserts that factor-bearing events have density at most $\approx N^{-1/2}$. The sample set produced by the static rho iteration measurably violates the density form of this statement. Either the principle is false, or it must be scoped.

**(D) Geometry of numbers on the digits.** Digit-level multiplication is a convolution; linearizing it produces a lattice built from the digits of $N$ alone. If the factorization were the unique short vector of that lattice, lattice reduction would factor $N$.

We resolve all four. Each resolution is negative for factoring but positive as mathematics: each is an exact structural theorem about $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$, valid for all semiprimes.

## 2. Notation and standing conventions

Throughout, $p$ and $q$ denote distinct primes and $N = pq$. We write:

- $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for the unit group, of order $\varphi(N) = (p-1)(q-1)$;
- $\pi_p : \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and $\pi_q : \mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}$ for the reduction ring homomorphisms; by the Chinese Remainder Theorem $(\pi_p,\pi_q)$ is an isomorphism onto the product;
- $(a\mid p) \in \{0,\pm 1\}$ for the Legendre symbol, i.e. the unique quadratic character of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ extended by $0$; for $a$ a unit mod $N$, $(a\mid p)$ abbreviates the Legendre symbol of $\pi_p(a)$, and similarly for $q$;
- $J(a \mid N)$ for the Jacobi symbol, which for $N = pq$ satisfies $J(a\mid N) = (a\mid p)\,(a\mid q)$ by definition/multiplicativity in the lower argument, and which is computable in time $O(\log^2 N)$ from $a$ and $N$ alone, with no knowledge of $p,q$.

Since $\pi_p$ is a ring homomorphism and $a$ is a unit, $\pi_p(a)$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$; in particular $\pi_p(a) \ne 0$ and $(a\mid p) \in \{\pm1\}$. The same holds for $q$. We use this dichotomy repeatedly without comment.

## 3. Closure A: symmetric character aggregates collapse

### 3.1 The agreement set

**Definition 3.1 (Agreement set).** For $N = pq$,
$$A(N) \;=\; \bigl\{\, a \in (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times \;:\; (a\mid p) = (a\mid q) \,\bigr\}.$$

$A(N)$ is invariant under the swap $p \leftrightarrow q$ (the defining condition is symmetric) and under conjugation. It is therefore a legitimate candidate for a *both-symmetries-invariant* aggregate. The question is whether $|A(N)|$, or the set itself, separates factorizations.

### 3.2 The flipping unit

The engine of the collapse is a single element.

**Lemma 3.2 (Existence of a flipping unit).** Let $p$ be an odd prime and $q \ne p$ prime. Then there exists $u \in (\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ with
$$(u \mid p) = -1, \qquad (u\mid q) = +1 .$$

*Proof.* Since $p$ is odd, the quadratic character of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is nontrivial, so there exists $y \in \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ with $(y\mid p) = -1$; in particular $y \ne 0$. Since $p \ne q$ are primes they are coprime, so by the Chinese Remainder Theorem there is an integer $k$ with $k \equiv y \pmod p$ and $k \equiv 1 \pmod q$. Then $p \nmid k$ (else $y = 0$) and $q \nmid k$ (else $1 \equiv 0 \bmod q$), so $\gcd(k, N) = 1$ and $k$ defines a unit $u$ of $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$. By construction $\pi_p(u) = y$, giving $(u\mid p) = -1$, and $\pi_q(u) = 1$, giving $(u \mid q) = +1$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.3 (Translation exchanges agreement and disagreement).** With $u$ as in Lemma 3.2, for every unit $a$:
$$a u \in A(N) \iff a \notin A(N).$$

*Proof.* Both Legendre symbols are multiplicative: $(au\mid p) = (a\mid p)(u\mid p) = -(a\mid p)$ and $(au\mid q) = (a\mid q)(u\mid q) = (a\mid q)$. Since $(a\mid p),(a\mid q) \in \{\pm1\}$, the equality $-(a\mid p) = (a\mid q)$ holds precisely when $(a\mid p) \neq (a\mid q)$. $\square$

### 3.3 The count

**Theorem 3.4 (Agreement collapse).** Let $p$ be an odd prime, $q \ne p$ prime, $N = pq$. Then
$$2\,|A(N)| \;=\; \varphi(N) \;=\; (p-1)(q-1),$$
that is, $|A(N)| = \varphi(N)/2$ exactly.

*Proof.* Right multiplication by the flipping unit $u$ is a bijection of $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$ (its inverse is multiplication by $u^{-1}$). By Lemma 3.3 it carries $A(N)$ bijectively onto the complement $A(N)^c$ inside the unit group. Hence $|A(N)| = |A(N)^c|$. Since $|A(N)| + |A(N)^c| = |(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times| = \varphi(N)$, we get $2|A(N)| = \varphi(N)$. The explicit value follows from multiplicativity of $\varphi$ on coprime arguments and $\varphi(\text{prime } r) = r - 1$. $\square$

The count is a function of $\varphi(N)$ alone. Since $\varphi(N)$ is itself equivalent to the factorization ($p + q = N + 1 - \varphi(N)$), the count is not a *new* witness: it is the old one, and computing it requires already knowing the answer.

### 3.4 The collapse onto the Jacobi symbol

The set-level statement is stronger and more damning.

**Theorem 3.5 (Agreement is a Jacobi level set).** For every unit $a$ of $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$, with $\tilde a \in \{0,\dots,N-1\}$ its representative,
$$a \in A(N) \iff J(\tilde a \mid N) = 1 .$$

*Proof.* The Legendre symbol of $\pi_p(a)$ equals the Legendre symbol of the integer $\tilde a$ at $p$, since $\tilde a \equiv \pi_p(a) \bmod p$; likewise at $q$. By multiplicativity of the Jacobi symbol in its lower argument, $J(\tilde a \mid pq) = (\tilde a\mid p)(\tilde a \mid q)$. Both factors lie in $\{\pm 1\}$, so their product is $+1$ exactly when they are equal, which is the definition of membership in $A(N)$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.6 (Barrier 2 in sharpest form).** The agreement aggregate is $N$-computable: membership in $A(N)$ is decidable in time $O(\log^2 N)$ from $N$ and $a$ alone. Hence it carries no information about $\{p,q\}$ beyond what $N$ already determines.

This is character orthogonality in action. The two Legendre characters $\chi_p = (\cdot \mid p)$ and $\chi_q = (\cdot\mid q)$, pulled back to $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^\times$, are distinct nontrivial quadratic characters. Any *aggregate* built from them that is invariant under the group $\{\mathrm{id},\ \chi_p \leftrightarrow \chi_q\}$ of available symmetries can only be a function of the product $\chi_p\chi_q$ — and the product is the Jacobi character mod $N$, which is public. Concretely, $\mathbf{1}[\chi_p(a) = \chi_q(a)] = \tfrac12(1 + \chi_p(a)\chi_q(a))$, an identity that displays the collapse in one line and, summed over $a$, reproves Theorem 3.4 since $\sum_a \chi_p(a)\chi_q(a) = 0$ by orthogonality of the nontrivial character $\chi_p\chi_q$.

## 4. Closure B: the zero-divisor graph is $K_{q-1,\,p-1}$, and it is circular

### 4.1 Vertices and wings

**Definition 4.1.** The **zero-divisor graph** $\Gamma(N)$ has vertex set
$$V(N) \;=\; \{\, x : 0 < x < N,\ \gcd(x, N) \ne 1 \,\},$$
and $x \sim y$ iff $x \ne y$ and $N \mid xy$.

**Definition 4.2.** For $d \mid N$, the **wing** of $d$ is $W_d = \{x : 0 < x < N,\ d \mid x\}$.

**Proposition 4.3 (Wing decomposition).** For distinct primes $p, q$ and $N = pq$:
$$V(N) = W_p \,\cup\, W_q, \qquad W_p \cap W_q = \emptyset .$$

*Proof.* If $x \in V(N)$ then $\gcd(x,N) > 1$, so some prime $r$ divides both $x$ and $N = pq$; as $r$ is prime, $r \in \{p, q\}$, placing $x$ in $W_p$ or $W_q$. Conversely if $p \mid x$ and $0<x<N$ then $p \mid \gcd(x,N)$, so $\gcd(x,N) \ne 1$; similarly for $q$. For disjointness: if $p \mid x$ and $q \mid x$ then, $p$ and $q$ being coprime, $N = pq \mid x$, forcing $x \ge N$ (as $x>0$), contradicting $x < N$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.4 (Wing sizes).** $|W_p| = q - 1$ and $|W_q| = p - 1$; hence
$$|V(N)| \;=\; p + q - 2 .$$

*Proof.* The map $k \mapsto pk$ is an injection from $\{1, \dots, q-1\}$ onto $W_p$: it lands in $W_p$ since $0 < pk < pq$, and every multiple of $p$ in $(0,N)$ has this form with $0 < k < q$. Injectivity is cancellation by $p > 0$. Symmetrically for $W_q$. The wings are disjoint, so the counts add, and $(q-1) + (p-1) = p+q-2$. $\square$

### 4.2 Exact adjacency: the graph is complete bipartite

**Theorem 4.5 (Bipartite structure).** Let $p \ne q$ be primes, $N = pq$. For $x, y \in V(N)$:
$$x \sim y \iff \bigl(x \in W_p \text{ and } y \in W_q\bigr) \ \text{or}\ \bigl(x \in W_q \text{ and } y \in W_p\bigr).$$
Equivalently, $\Gamma(N) \cong K_{q-1,\,p-1}$: it is the complete bipartite graph on parts of sizes $q-1$ and $p-1$.

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) If $x = pa$ and $y = qb$ then $xy = pq\,ab$, so $N \mid xy$; and $x \ne y$ because the wings are disjoint.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $x, y$ lie in the *same* wing, say both in $W_p$, and $N \mid xy$. Then $q \mid xy$, and $q$ prime gives $q \mid x$ or $q \mid y$. Either way that element lies in $W_p \cap W_q = \emptyset$, a contradiction. The argument for $W_q$ is symmetric (swap the roles of $p$ and $q$). Since by Proposition 4.3 every vertex lies in one of the two wings, the only remaining possibility for an edge is a cross-wing pair. $\square$

**Corollary 4.6 (Degrees).** Every $x \in W_p$ has $\deg(x) = |W_q| = p-1$, and every $x \in W_q$ has $\deg(x) = |W_p| = q-1$. Consequently, for every vertex $x$,
$$\deg(x) + 1 \in \{p, q\} \quad\text{and}\quad (\deg(x)+1) \mid N .$$

Thus the degree sequence of $\Gamma(N)$ — indeed the degree of a *single* vertex — recovers the factorization. This is precisely the sense in which a structural witness exists: $\Gamma(N)$ is a combinatorial object, not a number in the classified list, and its isomorphism class determines $\{p, q\}$.

### 4.3 The structural witness is the trace witness

**Theorem 4.7 (Collapse onto the trace).** Set $s = |V(N)| + 2$. Then $s = p + q$, and both primes are roots of
$$X^2 - sX + N = 0 .$$

*Proof.* Proposition 4.4 gives $|V(N)| = p+q-2$, so $s = p+q$. Then $p^2 - (p+q)p + pq = 0$ and $q^2 - (p+q)q + pq = 0$ identically. $\square$

So whatever novelty the graph possesses as an *object*, the information it delivers is exactly the classical trace $p+q$. The structural witness does not escape the numeric classification; it re-encodes it.

### 4.4 The circularity

**Theorem 4.8 (Circularity of the structural witness).** For every $x \in V(N)$,
$$\gcd(x, N) \in \{p, q\} .$$
In particular, exhibiting a single vertex of $\Gamma(N)$ already exhibits a nontrivial factor of $N$, at the cost of one gcd.

*Proof.* Let $x \in W_p$ (the case $x \in W_q$ is symmetric). Then $p \mid x$ and $p \mid N$, so $p \mid g := \gcd(x,N)$; write $g = pk$. From $g \mid N = pq$ and cancellation, $k \mid q$, so $k = 1$ or $k = q$ by primality. If $k = q$ then $q \mid g \mid x$, placing $x \in W_p \cap W_q = \emptyset$ — impossible. Hence $k=1$ and $g = p$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.9 (Cost of construction).** The vertex set $V(N)$ has density $(p+q-2)/N$ inside $[1,N)$. Locating a vertex by search over residues is exactly the trial-division / uniform-sampling problem of Section 5, and by Theorem 5.2 succeeds with probability at most $2/\min(p,q)$ per query. Hence *building* $\Gamma(N)$ costs $\Omega(\min(p,q))$ queries in the worst case, whereas *reading* it (via Corollary 4.6) is free.

This is the precise verdict: the structural witness is real, lies outside the letter of the numeric classification, and is nonetheless sealed — not by the classification but by the conjunction of aggregation cost (Corollary 4.9) and circularity (Theorem 4.8).

## 5. Closure C: scoping the noise-floor principle

### 5.1 The atomic uniform primitive

**Definition 5.1.** The **hit density** of $N = pq$ is
$$\delta(N) \;=\; \frac{|V(N)|}{N} \;=\; \frac{p+q-2}{pq},$$
the probability that a single uniform draw $a \leftarrow [1, N)$ satisfies $\gcd(a,N) > 1$.

**Theorem 5.2 (Atomic uniform noise-floor bound).** If $p \le q$ then
$$\delta(N) \;\le\; \frac{2}{p} \;=\; \frac{2}{\min(p,q)} .$$

*Proof.* $\delta(N) = (p+q-2)/(pq)$. Since $p \le q$ we have $p + q - 2 < 2q$, hence $\delta(N) < 2q/(pq) = 2/p$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (The floor and its extremal case).** For all naturals $p,q$: $(p+q)^2 \ge 4pq$, with equality iff $p = q$. Consequently, writing $\delta = \delta(N)$,
$$\bigl(\delta N + 2\bigr)^2 \;\ge\; 4N,$$
so $\delta \ge (2\sqrt N - 2)/N$: the hit density is bounded below by roughly $2/\sqrt N$, with the minimum approached exactly at balanced semiprimes.

*Proof.* $(p+q)^2 - 4pq = (p-q)^2 \ge 0$, vanishing iff $p=q$. For the second statement, $\delta N = p+q-2$, so $(\delta N + 2)^2 = (p+q)^2 \ge 4pq = 4N$. $\square$

Theorems 5.2 and 5.3 pin the atomic uniform primitive between $2/\sqrt N$ (balanced case, the hard case, and the cryptographic case) and $2/\min(p,q)$ (unbalanced case, where trial division is cheap anyway). This is the **noise-floor principle**, and in this form it is a theorem.

### 5.2 The static rho sampler: correlation beats density

Now consider the *derived* sample set generated by iterating a polynomial map. Let
$$\rho(x) = x^2 + 1 ,$$
taken over $\mathbb{Z}$ so that differences of iterates make sense, and let $x_t = \rho^{[t]}(x_0)$.

**Lemma 5.4 (Reduction-compatibility).** For any modulus $m$ and any $a \equiv b \pmod m$, we have $\rho(a) \equiv \rho(b) \pmod m$, and hence by induction $\rho^{[t]}(a) \equiv \rho^{[t]}(b) \pmod m$ for all $t \ge 0$.

*Proof.* Congruence is preserved by multiplication and addition: $a\equiv b$ gives $a^2 \equiv b^2$ and then $a^2+1 \equiv b^2 + 1$. Induction on $t$, applying the one-step statement to the congruent pair $\rho^{[t]}(a) \equiv \rho^{[t]}(b)$. $\square$

The lemma is the source of the correlation: the walk modulo $N$ *covers* the walk modulo $p$. The reduced walk lives in a state space of size $p$, not $N$, and this alone forces structure.

**Theorem 5.5 (Unconditional collision).** Let $p \ge 1$ and let $x_0 \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary. There exist $i < j \le p$ with
$$p \mid x_j - x_i .$$
No probabilistic hypothesis is used.

*Proof.* Consider the map $t \mapsto x_t \bmod p$ on $\{0,1,\dots,p\}$, a set of $p+1$ indices, taking values in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, a set of $p$ values. By pigeonhole it is not injective: there are indices $i \ne j$ in $\{0,\dots,p\}$ with $x_i \equiv x_j \pmod p$. Order them so that $i<j$; then $j \le p$ and $p \mid x_j - x_i$. $\square$

**Lemma 5.6 (Extraction).** Let $p,q$ be primes and $d \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $p \mid d$ and $q \nmid d$. Then
$$\gcd(d, pq) = p .$$

*Proof.* Let $g = \gcd(d, pq)$. From $p \mid d$ and $p \mid pq$ we get $p \mid g$; write $g = pk$. From $g \mid pq$ and cancellation, $k \mid q$, so $k \in \{1,q\}$. If $k = q$ then $q \mid g \mid d$, contradicting $q \nmid d$. Hence $g = p$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.7 (Correctness core of the rho method).** For any $x_0$ and any distinct primes $p,q$, there exist $i<j\le p$ with $p \mid x_j - x_i$, and for any such pair, if additionally $q \nmid x_j - x_i$, then
$$\gcd(x_j - x_i,\ N) = p .$$

*Proof.* Combine Theorem 5.5 and Lemma 5.6. $\square$

Thus the factor-bearing density of the rho sample *set* is not $N^{-1/2}$: among $T = p+1$ samples a factor-bearing pair is guaranteed, i.e. the pair-density is at least $1/\binom{p+1}{2}$ and the *set*-level success probability is $1$. The density form of the noise-floor principle is therefore false for correlated samples — as experiment measured directly.

### 5.3 The aggregation price

**Theorem 5.8 (Aggregation cost).** For every $p \ge 0$,
$$\binom{p+1}{2} \;=\; \frac{p(p+1)}{2} \;\ge\; p .$$

*Proof.* For $p = 0$ both sides are $0$. For $p \ge 1$, $p(p+1)/2 \ge p \iff p+1 \ge 2$, true. $\square$

**Corollary 5.9 (Scoping of the noise-floor principle).** Extracting a factor from the guaranteed collision of Theorem 5.7 by unstructured pairwise gcds requires $\binom{T}{2}$ operations with $T = p+1$, hence at least $p$ operations — no fewer than trial division by all primes up to $p$. The density gain of the correlated sample set is therefore not a complexity gain.

The correct statement of the principle is consequently:

> **Noise-floor principle (scoped).** The bound $\Pr[\text{success}] \le 2/\min(p,q)$ applies to an *atomic uniform primitive*: a single query drawn from the uniform distribution on $[1,N)$ (or a distribution close to it). It is *not* a density theorem about derived or correlated sample sets. Correlated sets may have far higher factor-bearing density; what they do not evade is the cost of the aggregation needed to convert density into a factor.

The genuine $\tilde O(\sqrt p)$ running time of Pollard's rho comes from replacing exhaustive pairwise comparison by cycle detection together with a birthday heuristic on the reduced functional graph — a *known method*, and the recognized exception to the principle, not a new escape from it.

## 6. Closure D: the digit relaxation is degenerate

### 6.1 The relaxation

Fix a base $b \ge 2$ and consider two-digit factors $p = u_0 + u_1 b$, $q = v_0 + v_1 b$ (the smallest nontrivial case; the phenomenon below is dimension-independent, since the kernel vector we construct is supported on two coordinates).

Multiplication is a digit convolution. Introducing the relaxation variables $w_{ij} = u_iv_j$ makes the digit equations linear, with the number recovered by the **digit functional**
$$\Phi_b(w) \;=\; \sum_{i,j\in\{0,1\}} w_{ij}\, b^{\,i+j} .$$
Write $\langle u\rangle_b = u_0 + u_1b$ and let $u \otimes v$ denote the rank-one matrix $(u_iv_j)_{i,j}$. Let $\|w\|^2 = \sum_{i,j} w_{ij}^2$ be the squared Frobenius norm.

**Proposition 6.1 (Faithfulness).** $\Phi_b(u\otimes v) = \langle u\rangle_b\,\langle v\rangle_b$.

*Proof.* $\sum_{i,j} u_iv_j b^{i+j} = \bigl(\sum_i u_ib^i\bigr)\bigl(\sum_j v_jb^j\bigr)$. $\square$

So the solutions of $\Phi_b(w) = N$ that are *rank one with the right digit ranges* are exactly the factorizations of $N$. The relaxation drops the rank condition.

**Proposition 6.2 (The discarded constraint).** $\det(u\otimes v) = (u_0v_0)(u_1v_1) - (u_0v_1)(u_1v_0) = 0$. Rank-one matrices are exactly those killed by the determinant, a *quadratic* function; the linear relaxation cannot express it.

### 6.2 The carry commutator

**Definition 6.3.** The **carry commutator** is $C = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$.

**Theorem 6.4 (Kernel vector of constant norm).** For every base $b$,
$$\Phi_b(C) = b^{0+1} - b^{1+0} = 0, \qquad \|C\|^2 = 2 .$$

The reason is structural: the coefficient $b^{i+j}$ depends only on $i+j$, so the functional cannot distinguish the transposed positions $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$. The information lost in that identification is exactly the carry information of the original digit multiplication.

**Theorem 6.5 (Non-isolation of the factorization target).** For every base $b$ and all digit vectors $u,v$, there exists an integer matrix $w$ with
$$\Phi_b(w) = \Phi_b(u\otimes v), \qquad \det w \ne 0, \qquad \|w - u\otimes v\|^2 \le 8 .$$
In particular $w$ solves the relaxed digit system for the same $N$ but is *not* a factorization, and its distance from the target is bounded by an absolute constant independent of $N$.

*Proof.* Take $w = u\otimes v + cC$ for $c \in \{1,2\}$. Then $\Phi_b(w) = \Phi_b(u\otimes v) + c\,\Phi_b(C) = \Phi_b(u\otimes v)$ by Theorem 6.4, and $\|w - u\otimes v\|^2 = c^2\|C\|^2 = 2c^2 \le 8$. For the determinant, a direct $2\times2$ computation with $D = u_0v_1 - u_1v_0$ gives
$$\det(u\otimes v + cC) = c\,D + c^2 .$$
If $D + 1 \ne 0$, take $c = 1$: the determinant is $D + 1 \ne 0$. If $D + 1 = 0$, i.e. $D = -1$, take $c = 2$: the determinant is $2(-1) + 4 = 2 \ne 0$. $\square$

**Proposition 6.6 (Target norm grows).** $\|u\otimes v\|^2 = (u_0^2+u_1^2)(v_0^2+v_1^2)$; in particular if all four digits are nonzero then $\|u \otimes v\|^2 \ge 4$, and the norm grows multiplicatively with the digit norms of $p$ and $q$.

**Corollary 6.7 (Failure of short-vector search).** The relaxed solution set contains, within an $O(1)$-radius ball around every factorization target, spurious solutions of the same digit value with nonzero determinant. Since the target's own norm grows with the size of $N$ while the perturbation stays of constant size, the factorization is never an isolated shortest vector; a reduction algorithm returning a shortest or nearly-shortest vector has no mechanism to prefer the rank-one point. This is the exact structural content of the observation that the target sits *at* the Gaussian heuristic $\sqrt{\dim}$ rather than well below it.

Re-imposing rank one is not a repair: rank one plus carry integrality is the $\Theta(N)$-state de-carrying dynamic program, which is not a relaxation at all but the original problem restated.

## 7. The tropical picture: the corner at $\sqrt N$

All four closures share a geometric shadow. The divisor locus of $N$ is the hyperbola $xy = N$. Passing to logarithms turns it into the line
$$X + Y = \log N .$$
In the min-plus (tropical) semiring, where $a \oplus b = \min(a,b)$ and $a \odot b = a+b$, the tropical polynomial $X \odot Y$ is ordinary addition, and the tropical line $X\odot Y = \log N$ has its corner — the point of symmetry, where the two coordinates are equal — at $X = Y = \tfrac12 \log N$, i.e. at $\sqrt N$.

**Proposition 7.1 (Corner straddle).** If $p \le q$ and $N = pq$ then $p^2 \le N \le q^2$. In logarithmic coordinates, $\log p \le \tfrac12\log N \le \log q$: every divisor pair straddles the corner.

*Proof.* Multiply $p \le q$ by $p > 0$ and by $q>0$ respectively. $\square$

**Proposition 7.2 (Tropical submultiplicativity of the integer logarithm).** For primes $p,q$, in min-plus coordinates with $\lfloor \log_2 \cdot \rfloor$,
$$\lfloor \log_2 p\rfloor \odot \lfloor \log_2 q \rfloor \;\le\; \lfloor \log_2 (pq)\rfloor,$$
i.e. $\lfloor\log_2 p\rfloor + \lfloor \log_2 q\rfloor \le \lfloor \log_2 N\rfloor$; and the tropical *sum* (the minimum) of the two coordinates is at most the corner coordinate $\tfrac12\log_2 N$.

*Proof.* $2^{\lfloor\log_2 p\rfloor}\cdot 2^{\lfloor\log_2 q\rfloor} \le pq$, so the exponent sum is at most $\lfloor\log_2(pq)\rfloor$ by the defining property of the integer logarithm. The second statement is the straddle of Proposition 7.1 in logarithmic form. $\square$

The corner is the geometric form of the noise floor: the balanced case $p \approx q \approx \sqrt N$ is where the divisor locus is furthest from the coordinate axes and where the hit density of Theorem 5.3 attains its minimum $\approx 2/\sqrt N$. Each of the four probes above is, in this language, an attempt to reach the corner along a path that does not cross the $\sqrt N$ scale. None succeeds.

## 8. Algorithms and their costs

We record the algorithmic content of the results, with complexity in bit operations, $n = \log_2 N$.

**Algorithm 1 (Agreement test).** Given $N$ and $a$: return $[\,J(a\mid N) = 1\,]$, computed by the quadratic-reciprocity Euclidean algorithm. Cost $O(n^2)$. By Theorem 3.5 this decides membership in $A(N)$ *without* knowing $p,q$; by Theorem 3.4 the aggregate count is $\varphi(N)/2$, so no query strategy against $A(N)$ can separate two semiprimes of equal $\varphi$-value.

**Algorithm 2 (Zero-divisor graph reader).** Given a vertex $x$: output $\gcd(x,N)$, which by Theorem 4.8 is $p$ or $q$; or output $\deg(x)+1$, which by Corollary 4.6 is $p$ or $q$. Cost $O(n^2)$ *given* the vertex. Producing the vertex costs $\Omega(\min(p,q))$ expected queries by Theorem 5.2 — the entire cost of the pipeline is in the input.

**Algorithm 3 (Unconditional rho collision).** Iterate $x \mapsto x^2 + 1 \bmod N$ for $T$ steps, storing all iterates; for each pair $(i,j)$ compute $g=\gcd(x_j - x_i, N)$ and return $g$ if $1 < g < N$. By Theorem 5.7 the algorithm succeeds with $T = p + 1$; the pairwise loop costs $\binom{T}{2} = \Theta(p^2)$ gcds. Floyd cycle detection reduces the loop to $O(T)$ gcds but reintroduces a heuristic (birthday) bound $T \approx \sqrt p$; that is the known-method exception.

**Algorithm 4 (Digit-lattice perturbation certificate).** Given a base $b$ and a candidate rank-one target $u\otimes v$, output $w = u\otimes v + cC$ with $c \in \{1,2\}$ chosen so that $cD + c^2 \ne 0$, where $D = u_0v_1 - u_1v_0$. By Theorem 6.5 this is a same-value, non-rank-one lattice point at squared distance $\le 8$. Cost $O(1)$ arithmetic operations. The certificate demonstrates non-isolation *for the specific target*, hence rules out short-vector search as an isolation mechanism.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Four distinct failure modes

It is worth emphasizing that the four closures fail for four genuinely different reasons.

- **Symmetry saturation (Section 3).** An aggregate invariant under all symmetries available to a $p,q$-agnostic observer becomes computable by that observer. This is a statement about characters and orthogonality; nothing about computation enters except the observation that $J(\cdot\mid N)$ is fast.
- **Access, not information (Section 4).** The zero-divisor graph *does* contain the factorization, redundantly and in a form outside the numeric classification. The obstruction is that its vertex set coincides with the divisor structure, so it cannot be constructed without solving the problem. This is the cleanest available example of a witness that is real, non-numeric, and useless.
- **Scope of a probabilistic bound (Section 5).** A bound proved for one sampling model was applied to another. The repair is a scoping statement, and the resulting picture is *sharper* than the original: the atomic bound is now a theorem with explicit constant $2/\min(p,q)$, and the correlated regime is characterized by the aggregation cost.
- **Relaxation degeneracy (Section 6).** Convexifying/linearizing a rank constraint creates a kernel; if the kernel contains a constant-norm lattice vector, the relaxed problem cannot isolate the true point. This is a general lesson about lattice attacks on multiplicative structure, not specific to base $b$ or to two digits.

### 9.2 What the closures do *not* show

None of these results is a lower bound on the complexity of integer factorization, and none should be read as evidence that no polynomial algorithm exists. They are exact statements about four specific probes. In particular:

- Theorem 3.4 does not preclude a *non*-symmetric aggregate being useful; it shows that symmetry buys nothing.
- Theorem 4.5 does not preclude *other* structural witnesses; it shows that this one is circular. A structural witness whose construction does not require a nontrivial divisor is not ruled out by anything here.
- Theorem 5.2 bounds a single query; it says nothing about adaptive multi-query strategies except through the aggregation accounting.
- Theorem 6.5 rules out short-vector isolation for the specific two-digit relaxation with the digit functional $\Phi_b$; a relaxation with a genuinely different linear functional, or with a lattice in which the transpose degeneracy is broken, is not addressed.

### 9.3 The unifying accounting

A common ledger runs through all four. Every probe has an *evaluation cost* and an *information content*. The classified numeric witnesses have low evaluation cost only when the answer is known. What the four closures show is that each of the new candidates satisfies one of:

$$\text{(i) information content } = 0, \qquad \text{(ii) evaluation cost } \ge \min(p,q), \qquad \text{(iii) target not isolated.}$$

The agreement set falls in (i); the zero-divisor graph in (ii); the rho sample set in (ii) via aggregation; the digit lattice in (iii). There is no candidate here in the desired regime of positive information at polylogarithmic cost.

## 10. Future directions

The exact structural theorems proved above suggest sharper, falsifiable successors.

**Conjecture A (Structural-witness dichotomy).** For every pair of distinct primes $p \ne q$, any graph invariant of the zero-divisor graph of $\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$ that is computable in time $\mathrm{polylog}\,N$ from $N$ alone is a function of $N$ alone; equivalently, every invariant that separates two semiprimes of equal size requires $\Omega(\min(p,q))$ vertex queries. The motivation is Theorem 4.8: a *single* vertex already yields a factor, so an invariant is either blind (a function of $N$) or its evaluation embeds trial division — there is no middle regime. With the graph now characterized exactly as $K_{q-1,p-1}$ with a proven degree sequence, query-complexity statements about it can be attacked directly.

**Conjecture B (Atomic-uniform noise floor as a theorem, not a principle).** Let $D$ be any distribution on $[1,N)$ whose density is computable from $N$ in $\mathrm{polylog}\,N$ time and which is $\varepsilon$-close to uniform in total variation. Then a single sample from $D$ reveals a nontrivial factor with probability at most $2/\min(p,q) + \varepsilon$. Theorem 5.2 is the uniform case with exact constant $2/p$, and the perturbation term is forced by the total-variation bound; the content is that polylog-computability prevents any density from concentrating on the $p+q-2$ zero divisors.

**Conjecture C (Aggregation lower bound for correlated samplers).** Any algorithm that factors $N = pq$ using only pairwise gcds of iterates of a degree-$d$ polynomial map mod $N$ must perform $\Omega(\sqrt p)$ gcd operations, and the $p+1$ pigeonhole bound of Theorem 5.5 is tight up to the birthday square root. The collision structure of Section 5 is purely a statement about the reduced walk on $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, so the birthday bound should be provable as a counting statement about functional graphs, independently of any randomness assumption.

**Conjecture D (Lattice non-isolation in all dimensions).** The two-coordinate carry commutator of Theorem 6.4 generalizes: for digit vectors of any length $m$, the kernel of $\Phi_b$ on $m\times m$ matrices contains the $\binom{m}{2}$ antisymmetric "transposition" matrices $E_{ij} - E_{ji}$ with $i+j$ fixed, each of squared norm $2$. Hence the factorization target is non-isolated in every dimension, with a kernel of dimension growing quadratically in $m$ while the perturbation norm stays constant. The conjecture is that consequently no linear relaxation of the digit convolution can isolate the factorization, for any base and any digit length.

## 11. Conclusion

We have closed four structural hypotheses about semiprimes, each with an exact theorem rather than a heuristic. The agreement set of Legendre symbols has exactly $\varphi(N)/2$ elements and is the Jacobi level set, so symmetric character aggregates collapse. The zero-divisor graph of $\mathbb{Z}/pq\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly $K_{q-1,p-1}$, its vertex count is $p+q-2$, its degrees give the factors, and yet a single vertex already gives a factor by gcd — a structural witness that is real and circular. The atomic uniform sampling primitive succeeds with probability $(p+q-2)/N \le 2/\min(p,q)$, minimized at $\approx 2/\sqrt N$ for balanced semiprimes, while the correlated rho sample set provably contains a factor-bearing pair among its first $p+1$ elements — a genuine density escape paid for exactly by the $\binom{p+1}{2} \ge p$ aggregation cost. And the linearized digit convolution admits a constant-norm kernel vector, placing a non-factorization solution within squared distance $8$ of every target, so short-vector search cannot isolate the answer.

Taken together the results give a precise map of the boundary: information about the factorization is everywhere in the structure of $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$, and it is everywhere either symmetry-saturated, circular, aggregation-bound, or non-isolated. Knowing where the cliffs are is the prerequisite for finding the path.

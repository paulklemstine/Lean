## Abstract

We study the multiplicative arithmetic of ideals in the semiring $\mathbb N$ of natural numbers. Unlike ideals in a ring, these ideals retain genuinely additive information that is lost upon passage to $\mathbb Z$, and their multiplication need not satisfy cancellation. We introduce multiplicative similarity, under which two ideals are equivalent when multiplication by a common nonzero ideal makes them equal. We prove that similarity is an equivalence relation compatible with multiplication and that every multiplicative invariant taking values in a cancellative commutative monoid is constant on similarity classes. We then define an element $r\in\mathbb N$ to be integral over an ideal $A$ when $\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD$ for some nonzero ideal $D$. The resulting integral closure contains the original ideal, is monotone, and is invariant under similarity. Our central adjunction criterion states that $A+\langle r\rangle$ is similar to $A$ exactly when $r$ is integral over $A$. Finally, we give a completely explicit failure of cancellation:

$$
A=\langle5,17\rangle,\qquad
B=\langle5,17,43\rangle,\qquad
C=\langle5,11,19,23\rangle
$$

satisfy $A\ne B$ and $AC=BC$. Explicit additive certificates account for every product involving the extra generator $43$. These results establish a self-contained foundation for studying stabilized arithmetic, closure, and multiplicative invariants of numerical-semigroup ideals.

## 1. Introduction

An ideal of the natural-number semiring is at once an algebraic object and an additive reachability system. Given generators $a_1,\ldots,a_m$, the ideal they generate contains precisely the nonnegative integer combinations of those generators. This elementary description links ideals of $\mathbb N$ to numerical semigroups, coin-exchange problems, affine semigroups, and discrete optimization.

The multiplicative structure of these ideals is less familiar. If $I$ and $J$ are ideals, their product is generated by products $xy$ with $x\in I$ and $y\in J$. One might expect this multiplication to inherit the robust cancellation behavior of positive integers. It does not. Distinct ideals can become equal after multiplication by the same nonzero ideal.

This failure suggests two complementary responses. First, one may define two objects to be similar if a common multiplier makes them equal. Similarity deliberately identifies information that multiplication can erase. Second, one may ask which individual elements can be adjoined to an ideal without changing its similarity class. This leads naturally to an integrality condition and an associated closure.

The development below has four goals. Section 2 gives the semiring and ideal background needed for a self-contained treatment. Section 3 develops similarity abstractly and proves its universal invariance property. Section 4 specializes to ideals of $\mathbb N$ and establishes the fundamental properties of integral closure. Section 5 proves the adjunction criterion connecting closure to similarity. Section 6 supplies a detailed noncancellation example, including explicit arithmetic certificates. Sections 7 and 8 discuss algorithms and applications.

The central theme is stabilization. Ordinary equality compares ideals immediately. Similarity compares them after a common multiplicative environment has been attached. Integral closure detects the individual generators that become redundant under such stabilization.

## 2. Ideals and their arithmetic

### 2.1. The ambient semiring

The natural numbers form a commutative semiring under ordinary addition and multiplication. They have additive identity $0$ and multiplicative identity $1$, but additive inverses are absent. This absence matters: extending an ideal of $\mathbb N$ to an ideal of $\mathbb Z$ can discard fine additive information.

**Definition 2.1 (Ideal of $\mathbb N$).** An ideal is a subset $I\subseteq\mathbb N$ satisfying:

1. $0\in I$;
2. if $x,y\in I$, then $x+y\in I$;
3. if $x\in I$ and $n\in\mathbb N$, then $nx\in I$.

For a set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, its generated ideal is

$$
\langle S\rangle
=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{t}n_is_i:
t\ge0,\ n_i\in\mathbb N,\ s_i\in S\right\}.
$$

When $S=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\}$, we write $\langle a_1,\ldots,a_m\rangle$. In particular,

$$
\langle r\rangle=\{nr:n\in\mathbb N\}
$$

is the principal ideal generated by $r$.

The zero ideal is $\{0\}$, while the unit ideal is $\mathbb N=\langle1\rangle$.

### 2.2. Sum, order, and product

Ideals are ordered by inclusion. Their sum is

$$
I+J=\{x+y:x\in I,\ y\in J\}.
$$

It is the smallest ideal containing both $I$ and $J$. In particular,

$$
\langle S\rangle+\langle T\rangle=\langle S\cup T\rangle.
$$

**Definition 2.2 (Product of ideals).** The product $IJ$ is the ideal generated by all products $xy$ with $x\in I$ and $y\in J$.

For finite generating sets, distributivity gives the useful formula

$$
\langle a_1,\ldots,a_m\rangle
\langle b_1,\ldots,b_n\rangle
=
\langle a_ib_j:1\le i\le m,\ 1\le j\le n\rangle.
$$

Ideal multiplication is associative and commutative, has unit $\mathbb N$, and distributes over ideal sum:

$$
(I+J)K=IK+JK.
$$

It is also monotone: if $I\subseteq I'$ and $J\subseteq J'$, then $IJ\subseteq I'J'$.

**Lemma 2.3 (Products of nonzero ideals are nonzero).** If $I$ and $J$ are nonzero ideals of $\mathbb N$, then $IJ$ is nonzero.

**Proof sketch.** Choose nonzero $x\in I$ and nonzero $y\in J$. The product $xy$ belongs to $IJ$. Since the natural numbers have no zero divisors, $xy\ne0$. Thus $IJ$ contains a nonzero element. $\square$

This elementary fact is essential whenever products of witnesses are used: it guarantees that stabilization does not collapse through the zero ideal.

## 3. Multiplicative similarity

We first work in an arbitrary commutative monoid $M$.

**Definition 3.1 (Multiplicative similarity).** Elements $a,b\in M$ are similar, written here as $a\sim b$, if there exists $c\in M$ such that

$$
ac=bc.
$$

**Theorem 3.2 (Similarity is an equivalence relation).** Multiplicative similarity on a commutative monoid is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.

**Proof sketch.** Reflexivity follows from $a\cdot1=a\cdot1$. Symmetry follows by reversing the witnessing equality. For transitivity, suppose $ad=bd$ and $be=ce$. Then

$$
a(de)=(ad)e=(bd)e=(be)d=(ce)d=c(de),
$$

so $a\sim c$. $\square$

The transitivity argument explains why commutativity is useful: it permits the two witnesses to be rearranged into a common witness $de$.

**Theorem 3.3 (Multiplicative compatibility).** If $a_1\sim a_2$ and $b_1\sim b_2$, then

$$
a_1b_1\sim a_2b_2.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Choose witnesses $c_1,c_2$ satisfying $a_1c_1=a_2c_1$ and $b_1c_2=b_2c_2$. Then

$$
(a_1b_1)(c_1c_2)
=(a_1c_1)(b_1c_2)
=(a_2c_1)(b_2c_2)
=(a_2b_2)(c_1c_2).
$$

Thus $c_1c_2$ witnesses the desired similarity. $\square$

Consequently, multiplication descends to similarity classes.

### 3.1. A universal invariance principle

A commutative monoid $N$ is cancellative if $xz=yz$ implies $x=y$. A multiplicative map $f:M\to N$ satisfies $f(xy)=f(x)f(y)$ and $f(1)=1$.

**Theorem 3.4 (Cancellative invariants are similarity invariants).** Let $f:M\to N$ be a multiplicative map from a commutative monoid to a cancellative commutative monoid. If $a\sim b$, then

$$
f(a)=f(b).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Choose $c$ with $ac=bc$. Applying $f$ yields

$$
f(a)f(c)=f(ac)=f(bc)=f(b)f(c).
$$

Cancellation of $f(c)$ proves the claim. $\square$

This theorem characterizes a broad family of measurements that cannot distinguish similar objects. Whenever an invariant is both multiplicative and cancellative in its target, similarity invariance is automatic rather than an additional property requiring separate proof.

### 3.2. Similarity for natural-number ideals

For ideals, the zero ideal must be excluded as a witness.

**Definition 3.5 (Similarity of ideals).** Ideals $A,B\subseteq\mathbb N$ are similar if there exists a nonzero ideal $C$ such that

$$
AC=BC.
$$

The nonzero condition prevents the trivial equality $A\{0\}=B\{0\}=\{0\}$ from identifying every pair. By Lemma 2.3, products of nonzero witnesses remain nonzero, so the equivalence and compatibility arguments above apply unchanged to nonzero ideal witnesses.

## 4. Integrality and integral closure

Similarity compares whole ideals. Integrality asks whether one element is already present from the stabilized multiplicative viewpoint.

**Definition 4.1 (Integral element).** Let $A$ be an ideal of $\mathbb N$ and $r\in\mathbb N$. The element $r$ is integral over $A$ if there exists a nonzero ideal $D$ such that

$$
\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD.
$$

The ideal $D$ is called an integrality witness for $r$ over $A$.

**Definition 4.2 (Integral closure).** The integral closure of $A$ is

$$
\overline A
=\{r\in\mathbb N:r\text{ is integral over }A\}.
$$

This definition is set-theoretic: the results below concern the membership behavior of $\overline A$. The condition says that after multiplication by a suitable nonzero ideal, every multiple generated from $r$ is absorbed by the corresponding product with $A$.

### 4.1. Extensivity

**Theorem 4.3 (Every ideal is contained in its integral closure).** For every ideal $A$,

$$
A\subseteq\overline A.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Let $r\in A$. Use the unit ideal $D=\mathbb N$, which is nonzero. Since $A$ is closed under multiplication by arbitrary natural numbers, $\langle r\rangle\subseteq A$. Therefore

$$
\langle r\rangle D=\langle r\rangle
\subseteq A=AD.
$$

Thus $r$ is integral over $A$. $\square$

### 4.2. Monotonicity

**Theorem 4.4 (Monotonicity of integral closure).** If $A\subseteq B$, then

$$
\overline A\subseteq\overline B.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Take $r\in\overline A$ and choose a nonzero witness $D$ with $\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD$. Monotonicity of ideal multiplication gives $AD\subseteq BD$, hence

$$
\langle r\rangle D\subseteq BD.
$$

The same $D$ witnesses $r\in\overline B$. $\square$

### 4.3. Similarity invariance

**Theorem 4.5 (Integral closure is invariant under similarity).** If $A$ and $B$ are similar ideals, then

$$
\overline A=\overline B.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Choose a nonzero ideal $C$ with $AC=BC$. Suppose $r\in\overline A$, witnessed by a nonzero ideal $D$:

$$
\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD.
$$

Multiply this inclusion by $C$. Associativity and commutativity give

$$
\langle r\rangle(DC)
=(\langle r\rangle D)C
\subseteq(AD)C
=A(DC).
$$

To replace $A$ by $B$, rearrange the factors:

$$
A(DC)=D(AC)=D(BC)=B(DC).
$$

Hence

$$
\langle r\rangle(DC)\subseteq B(DC).
$$

By Lemma 2.3, $DC$ is nonzero, so it is a valid witness and $r\in\overline B$. The same argument with $A$ and $B$ interchanged proves the reverse inclusion. $\square$

This result shows that integral closure lives naturally on similarity classes. It retains stable multiplicative information while ignoring distinctions that a common factor can erase.

## 5. The adjunction criterion

Let $A+\langle r\rangle$ denote the ideal obtained by adjoining $r$ to $A$. The following theorem gives the exact relation between adjunction, integrality, and similarity.

**Theorem 5.1 (Adjunction Criterion).** Let $A$ be an ideal of $\mathbb N$ and let $r\in\mathbb N$. Then the following are equivalent:

1. $A+\langle r\rangle$ is similar to $A$;
2. $r$ is integral over $A$.

Equivalently,

$$
A+\langle r\rangle\sim A
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
r\in\overline A.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Suppose first that $A+\langle r\rangle$ is similar to $A$. There is a nonzero ideal $D$ such that

$$
(A+\langle r\rangle)D=AD.
$$

By distributivity,

$$
(A+\langle r\rangle)D=AD+\langle r\rangle D.
$$

Since the left side equals $AD$, the second summand is contained in $AD$:

$$
\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD.
$$

Thus $D$ witnesses that $r$ is integral over $A$.

Conversely, suppose $r$ is integral over $A$. Choose nonzero $D$ such that $\langle r\rangle D\subseteq AD$. Again using distributivity,

$$
(A+\langle r\rangle)D
=AD+\langle r\rangle D
=AD.
$$

Hence $D$ witnesses $A+\langle r\rangle\sim A$. $\square$

The theorem supplies an operational characterization of closure:

$$
\overline A
=\{r\in\mathbb N:A+\langle r\rangle\sim A\}.
$$

Thus an integral element is precisely a generator whose adjunction does not change the stabilized multiplicative type of the ideal.

**Corollary 5.2 (Finite adjunction of integral elements).** If $r_1,\ldots,r_t\in\overline A$, then adjoining them one at a time produces ideals linked to $A$ by a chain of similarities, provided integrality is re-established at each enlarged stage.

**Proof sketch.** The Adjunction Criterion applies at each step. Monotonicity guarantees that an element integral over $A$ remains integral over any larger intermediate ideal. Transitivity of similarity then connects the final ideal to $A$. $\square$

In particular, finite collections of elements already in $\overline A$ do not alter the similarity class when adjoined.

## 6. Explicit failure of cancellation

We now establish noncancellation with concrete ideals:

$$
A=\langle5,17\rangle,
\qquad
B=\langle5,17,43\rangle,
\qquad
C=\langle5,11,19,23\rangle.
$$

### 6.1. Distinguishing $A$ and $B$

**Lemma 6.1.** The ideals $A$ and $B$ are distinct.

**Proof sketch.** Clearly $43\in B$. If $43\in A$, there would be $u,v\in\mathbb N$ with

$$
43=5u+17v.
$$

Because $17v\le43$, only $v=0,1,2$ are possible. These give $5u=43,26,9$, none divisible by $5$. Hence $43\notin A$ and $A\ne B$. $\square$

### 6.2. Computing the products

Since $A\subseteq B$, monotonicity immediately gives

$$
AC\subseteq BC.
$$

The ideal $B$ is $A+\langle43\rangle$, so distributivity yields

$$
BC=AC+\langle43\rangle C.
$$

It therefore suffices to prove

$$
\langle43\rangle C\subseteq AC.
$$

The product ideal $\langle43\rangle C$ is generated by

$$
215=43\cdot5,
\quad473=43\cdot11,
\quad817=43\cdot19,
\quad989=43\cdot23.
$$

The product $AC$ is generated by the eight pairwise products

$$
25,55,95,115,85,187,323,391.
$$

Each extra generator has an explicit expression in $AC$:

$$
215=10\cdot5+15\cdot11=2\cdot25+3\cdot55,
$$

$$
473=17\cdot19+30\cdot5=323+6\cdot25,
$$

$$
817=34\cdot11+17\cdot19+5\cdot19+5\cdot5
=2\cdot187+323+95+25,
$$

and

$$
989=17\cdot23+17\cdot19+25\cdot11
=391+323+5\cdot55.
$$

For the first factors, $10,15,30,34,25\in A$ because each is a nonnegative combination of $5$ and $17$. Every second factor belongs to $C$. Consequently each summand is in $AC$, and closure under addition places all four numbers in $AC$.

**Theorem 6.2 (Concrete Noncancellation Theorem).** The ideals

$$
A=\langle5,17\rangle,
\quad B=\langle5,17,43\rangle,
\quad C=\langle5,11,19,23\rangle
$$

satisfy

$$
A\ne B
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
AC=BC.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 6.1 gives $A\ne B$. The inclusion $AC\subseteq BC$ follows from $A\subseteq B$. The four displayed certificates show that every generator of $\langle43\rangle C$ lies in $AC$, so

$$
BC=AC+\langle43\rangle C\subseteq AC.
$$

Thus equality holds. $\square$

**Corollary 6.3.** Multiplication in the commutative semiring of ideals of $\mathbb N$ is not cancellative.

**Proof sketch.** Theorem 6.2 exhibits $A\ne B$ and a common nonzero factor $C$ with $AC=BC$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.4.** The element $43$ is integral over $A=\langle5,17\rangle$, although $43\notin A$.

**Proof sketch.** Since $B=A+\langle43\rangle$ and $BC=AC$ with $C\ne0$, the Adjunction Criterion implies $43\in\overline A$. Lemma 6.1 proves nonmembership in $A$. $\square$

This corollary sharply separates ordinary membership from stabilized membership.

## 7. Algorithms and computational certificates

The preceding theorems suggest finite procedures for finitely generated ideals.

### 7.1. Bounded membership by dynamic programming

Given generators $g_1,\ldots,g_m$ and a bound $N$, one can compute all elements of $\langle g_1,\ldots,g_m\rangle$ up to $N$. Initialize a Boolean array with only $0$ reachable. Scan integers from $0$ to $N$; whenever $n$ is reachable, mark $n+g_i$ reachable for every generator within the bound.

The running time is $O(mN)$ and storage is $O(N)$. This computes exact bounded membership, not merely a heuristic. It directly confirms, for example, that $43\notin\langle5,17\rangle$.

### 7.2. Product-generator reduction

For finite generator lists $G$ and $H$, form all pairwise products

$$
P=\{gh:g\in G,\ h\in H\}.
$$

The product ideal is $\langle P\rangle$. Redundant product generators may be removed by testing whether each lies in the ideal generated by the others. A simple dynamic program bounded by the largest candidate gives a practical reduction procedure. With $m=|G|$, $n=|H|$, and maximum candidate $M$, pair generation costs $O(mn)$ and naive redundancy testing costs $O((mn)^2M)$; incremental implementations are usually faster.

### 7.3. Verifying an adjunction witness

To test a proposed witness $D=\langle d_1,\ldots,d_s\rangle$ for integrality of $r$ over $A=\langle a_1,\ldots,a_m\rangle$, it suffices to check each generator $rd_j$ of $\langle r\rangle D$ for membership in $AD$, generated by all $a_i d_j$. Because ideal containment between finitely generated ideals reduces to membership of generators, these finitely many tests prove the whole inclusion.

For the example, the checks are exactly the four certificates for $215$, $473$, $817$, and $989$. This algorithm mirrors the mathematical proof: a potentially infinite inclusion is reduced to finitely many additive decompositions.

## 8. Applications and interpretation

### 8.1. Numerical semigroups

When a finitely generated ideal has generators with greatest common divisor $1$, its positive part is eventually cofinite: all sufficiently large natural numbers occur. Such systems are numerical semigroups or closely related semigroup ideals. Classical invariants record gaps, conductors, and minimal generators. Ideal multiplication adds a second operation whose effect on these additive invariants is subtle.

Similarity isolates the information stable under multiplication by a common ideal. Integral closure then detects generators that are absent additively but redundant multiplicatively. The example $43\notin A$ yet $43\in\overline A$ is the smallest conceptual unit of this distinction.

### 8.2. Coarse-graining and indistinguishability

The equation $AC=BC$ can be read as a loss of resolution. Before the environment $C$ is introduced, $A$ and $B$ differ; afterward, their attainable products coincide. Analogous patterns occur whenever systems are combined with a fixed interface or context. Similarity records contextual indistinguishability, while Theorem 3.4 says that every cancellative multiplicative observable must assign identical values to contextually indistinguishable systems.

### 8.3. Factorization theory

Cancellation is often an invisible assumption behind clean factorization theories. Once it fails, uniqueness can break at several levels: factors may not be recoverable from a product, and irreducible decompositions may have different structures or lengths. Passing to similarity classes repairs exactly the distinctions erased by common multiplication, but it does not automatically guarantee unique factorization. Instead, it provides the appropriate arena in which multiplicative invariants can be sought.

## 9. Discussion

The results form a compact structural chain.

1. Similarity is the equivalence relation generated by equality after common multiplication.
2. Its compatibility with multiplication makes similarity classes into a meaningful multiplicative quotient.
3. Cancellative multiplicative invariants necessarily factor through this quotient.
4. Integrality detects when a single principal contribution is absorbed after multiplication by a nonzero witness.
5. Integral closure is extensive, monotone, and constant on similarity classes.
6. The Adjunction Criterion identifies integral elements exactly as generators whose addition preserves similarity.
7. The ideals $\langle5,17\rangle$ and $\langle5,17,43\rangle$ prove that cancellation genuinely fails, while simultaneously illustrating the adjunction criterion.

The explicit example also clarifies why ideal arithmetic cannot be read solely from generating lists. A generator may be indispensable before multiplication and entirely redundant afterward. The four identities for $215$, $473$, $817$, and $989$ are local certificates of a global equality between infinite additive sets.

Several boundaries should be emphasized. The present results do not assert that the integral closure is finitely generated by a specified algorithm, nor do they identify it with a Newton-polygon construction. They establish the witness-based closure and its fundamental structural properties. Likewise, the noncancellation example proves existence rather than classifying all failures of cancellation.

## 10. Future work

A first direction is a finite-power criterion: determine whether $r$ is integral over $I$ exactly when $r^k\in I^k$ for some $k\ge1$. Such a theorem would convert an existential ideal witness into a single finite-power certificate.

A second direction concerns eventual symmetry. If powers $S^k$ of a numerical-semigroup ideal are symmetric for every sufficiently large $k$, one may ask whether $S$ must be integrally equivalent to a two-generator numerical semigroup.

A third direction seeks complete geometric invariants. Primewise lower Newton polygons may characterize similarity of nonzero ideals, connecting additive generators to tropical convex geometry.

A fourth direction concerns nonunique factorization. One may ask whether, for every $n\ge2$, some similarity class admits two irreducible factorizations whose lengths differ by exactly $n$.

Finally, the genus of powers may exhibit asymptotic curvature: for every nonprincipal numerical-semigroup ideal $S$, the sequence $k\mapsto\operatorname{genus}(S^k)$ may eventually be strictly log-convex.

## 11. Conclusion

The ideal arithmetic of $\mathbb N$ differs decisively from ordinary integer arithmetic. Multiplication can erase distinctions: there are explicit nonzero ideals $A$, $B$, and $C$ with $A\ne B$ but $AC=BC$. Similarity turns this failure into structure by identifying ideals that become equal under a common nonzero multiplier. Integral closure then describes the elements that are already present from this stabilized viewpoint.

The Adjunction Criterion provides the conceptual synthesis:

$$
r\in\overline A
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
A+\langle r\rangle\sim A.
$$

Thus closure measures redundancy under multiplicative stabilization. The concrete behavior of $43$ relative to $\langle5,17\rangle$ demonstrates that stable membership can be strictly broader than ordinary membership. This distinction supplies a foundation for further study of factorization, tropical invariants, Newton data, and asymptotic properties of numerical-semigroup ideals.

## Abstract

Let $(F_n)_{n\ge 0}$ be the Fibonacci sequence, defined by $F_0=0$, $F_1=1$, and $F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n$. This paper develops a finite-family synchronization principle from three ingredients: the strong divisibility identity $\gcd(F_a,F_b)=F_{\gcd(a,b)}$, its transport to arbitrary finite sets, and the existence of primitive prime divisors at prime indices $q\ge 13$. We prove that finite gcds commute with the Fibonacci map:

$$
\gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}=F_{\gcd(S)}
$$

for every finite set $S$ of nonnegative integers, including the empty set under the convention $\gcd(\varnothing)=0$. We then show that every prime $q\ge 13$ admits a prime $p$ with exact apparition law

$$
p\mid F_m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\mid m
$$

for all $m\ge 0$. Consequently, the same $p$ synchronizes every finite family:

$$
p\mid \gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\mid \gcd(S).
$$

We also describe a certified primitive-part method establishing that every $F_n$ with $13\le n\le 10{,}000$ has a primitive prime divisor. The finite upper bound is explicit: no unbounded composite-index assertion is inferred from this certificate. Algorithms, examples, applications, limitations, and extensions to other strong divisibility sequences are discussed.

## 1. Introduction

Recurrence sequences connect additive generation rules with multiplicative arithmetic. The Fibonacci sequence is the standard example: its terms arise from addition, yet their divisibility is organized by the divisibility of their indices. The identity

$$
\gcd(F_a,F_b)=F_{\gcd(a,b)}
$$

expresses this organization in its strongest elementary form. It says not merely that divisibility of indices implies divisibility of values, but that the Fibonacci map preserves greatest common divisors exactly.

This paper studies what happens when this preservation law is combined with a prime that appears for the first time at a prime index. The combination produces a global and uniform conclusion. A primitive prime divisor at a prime index $q$ does not merely witness novelty in $F_q$; it divides precisely those Fibonacci numbers whose indices are multiples of $q$. The same prime also tests arbitrary finite families: it divides their common gcd exactly when $q$ divides the gcd of their indices.

The main contribution is therefore a bridge among three levels of structure:

1. **Binary arithmetic:** gcds of two Fibonacci values.
2. **Finite aggregation:** gcds of arbitrary finite families.
3. **Global apparition:** the complete divisibility locus of one prime.

The bridge is useful both conceptually and computationally. Conceptually, it identifies finite gcd as a meet operation preserved by the Fibonacci map. Computationally, it replaces gcd calculations on potentially enormous Fibonacci values by gcd calculations on small indices.

A second theme is the careful separation of finite certification from unbounded theory. A primitive-part computation proves primitive divisor existence for every index in the interval $13\le n\le 10{,}000$. The prime-index synchronization theorem is unbounded because its primitive-divisor input is unbounded over prime indices. By contrast, the finite composite computation does not establish the tail beyond $10{,}000$; that tail requires independent cyclotomic growth estimates.

## 2. Definitions and conventions

### 2.1. Fibonacci numbers

The Fibonacci sequence $(F_n)_{n\ge 0}$ is defined by

$$
F_0=0,\qquad F_1=1,\qquad F_{n+2}=F_{n+1}+F_n.
$$

All indices in this paper are nonnegative integers. We use $a\mid b$ to mean that $a$ divides $b$.

### 2.2. Finite gcd

For a nonempty finite set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$, $\gcd(S)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of its elements. We adopt the standard neutral convention

$$
\gcd(\varnothing)=0.
$$

Likewise, for a finite family of nonnegative integers $(x_n)_{n\in S}$, the gcd of the empty family is $0$. This convention is natural because $\gcd(0,a)=a$, so $0$ is the identity element for finite gcd aggregation.

### 2.3. Primitive prime divisors and apparition

A prime $p$ is a **primitive prime divisor** of $F_n$ if

$$
p\mid F_n
$$

and

$$
p\nmid F_k\qquad\text{for every }k\text{ with }0<k<n.
$$

The **rank of apparition** of a prime $p$ that divides at least one positive-index Fibonacci number is the least positive integer $r$ such that $p\mid F_r$. Thus $p$ is primitive at index $n$ exactly when its rank of apparition is $n$.

### 2.4. Synchronizing primes

Fix a positive integer $q$. A prime $p$ is an **exact synchronizing prime for $q$** if

$$
p\mid F_m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\mid m
$$

for every $m\ge 0$. Such a prime detects the principal divisibility filter generated by $q$ through Fibonacci divisibility.

## 3. Foundational divisibility laws

We begin with two standard structural facts.

### Theorem 3.1 (Strong divisibility identity)

For all nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$,

$$
\gcd(F_a,F_b)=F_{\gcd(a,b)}.
$$

#### Proof sketch

Apply the Euclidean algorithm to the indices and the Fibonacci addition identities to the values. If $a\ge b$, suitable addition formulas imply that the common divisors of $F_a$ and $F_b$ are exactly the common divisors of $F_{a-b}$ and $F_b$. Repeating this reduction follows the Euclidean algorithm until the index pair becomes $(\gcd(a,b),0)$. Since $F_0=0$ and $\gcd(F_d,0)=F_d$, the result follows.

### Corollary 3.2 (Index divisibility transports to values)

If $a\mid b$, then $F_a\mid F_b$.

#### Proof sketch

If $a\mid b$, then $\gcd(a,b)=a$. Theorem 3.1 gives $\gcd(F_a,F_b)=F_a$, which is equivalent to $F_a\mid F_b$.

These statements explain why the index arithmetic controls the recurrence arithmetic. The next section upgrades the binary law to arbitrary finite families.

## 4. Finite gcd transport

### Theorem 4.1 (Finite GCD Transport Theorem)

For every finite set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$,

$$
\gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}=F_{\gcd(S)}.
$$

The statement includes $S=\varnothing$, for which both sides equal $0$.

#### Proof sketch

Proceed by induction on the cardinality of $S$. For the empty set,

$$
\gcd\varnothing=0=F_0=F_{\gcd(\varnothing)}.
$$

Assume the result for a finite set $S$ and let $a\notin S$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\gcd\{F_n:n\in S\cup\{a\}\}
&=\gcd\!\left(F_a,\gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}\right)\\
&=\gcd\!\left(F_a,F_{\gcd(S)}\right)\\
&=F_{\gcd(a,\gcd(S))}\\
&=F_{\gcd(S\cup\{a\})}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The induction step uses the binary strong divisibility identity exactly once.

### Corollary 4.2 (Common-divisor compression)

For every positive integer $d$ and finite set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$,

$$
d\mid F_{\gcd(S)}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d\mid F_n\text{ for every }n\in S.
$$

#### Proof sketch

By Theorem 4.1, $F_{\gcd(S)}$ is the gcd of the finite family $(F_n)_{n\in S}$. An integer divides a finite gcd exactly when it divides every member of the family. The empty case is consistent: every positive $d$ divides $0$.

### Example 4.3

Let $S=\{18,30,42\}$. Since $\gcd(S)=6$,

$$
\gcd(F_{18},F_{30},F_{42})=F_6=8.
$$

The index-first calculation avoids taking a gcd of the much larger numbers $2584$, $832040$, and $267914296$.

### Structural interpretation

Finite gcd is the meet operation in the divisibility order. Theorem 4.1 says that the Fibonacci map preserves all finite meets. This observation is sequence-independent in form: any sequence satisfying a binary strong divisibility identity and taking value $0$ at index $0$ admits the same induction.

## 5. Primitive divisors on a certified finite range

We now describe a finite primitive-divisor result and the mechanism supporting it.

### Theorem 5.1 (Primitive divisors through index $10{,}000$)

For every integer $n$ satisfying

$$
13\le n\le 10{,}000,
$$

there exists a prime $p$ such that

$$
p\mid F_n
$$

and

$$
p\nmid F_k\qquad(0<k<n).
$$

Thus every Fibonacci number in this index range has a primitive prime divisor.

The argument divides into prime and composite indices. Prime indices are covered by a general primitive-divisor theorem valid for every prime $q\ge 13$. Composite indices in the stated range are handled by a finite primitive-part certificate.

### 5.1. Proper-divisor reduction

The following lemma converts non-primitivity at an arbitrary earlier index into divisibility at a proper divisor of the target index.

### Lemma 5.2 (Reduction to a proper divisor)

Let $n>0$, and suppose $p\mid F_n$. If $p\mid F_k$ for some $0<k<n$, then there exists a positive proper divisor $d$ of $n$ such that $p\mid F_d$.

#### Proof sketch

Set $d=\gcd(n,k)$. The strong divisibility identity gives

$$
p\mid \gcd(F_n,F_k)=F_d.
$$

Moreover, $d\mid n$, $d>0$, and $d\le k<n$, so $d$ is a positive proper divisor of $n$.

This lemma is the key compression step. To prove that a factor of $F_n$ is primitive, it is enough to show that it divides no $F_d$ indexed by a proper divisor $d$ of $n$; one need not compare against every earlier index separately.

### 5.2. Primitive-part construction

For fixed $n$, begin with $R=F_n$. Enumerate the positive proper divisors $d$ of $n$. For each such $d$, repeatedly replace

$$
R\leftarrow \frac{R}{\gcd(R,F_d)}
$$

while $\gcd(R,F_d)>1$. Denote the final remainder by $P_n$ and call it the **primitive part** produced by the stripping procedure.

The construction has two elementary invariants:

1. At every stage, $R\mid F_n$.
2. After processing $d$, the resulting $R$ is coprime to $F_d$.

Because later stages only divide $R$, coprimality with previously processed values is preserved. Hence the final $P_n$ divides $F_n$ and is coprime to $F_d$ for every positive proper divisor $d$ of $n$.

### Lemma 5.3 (Primitive-part criterion)

If $P_n>1$, then $F_n$ has a primitive prime divisor.

#### Proof sketch

Choose any prime $p$ dividing $P_n$. Since $P_n\mid F_n$, one has $p\mid F_n$. By construction, $p$ divides no $F_d$ for any positive proper divisor $d$ of $n$. If $p$ divided some earlier $F_k$ with $0<k<n$, Lemma 5.2 would force $p\mid F_{\gcd(n,k)}$, where $\gcd(n,k)$ is a positive proper divisor of $n$. This is impossible. Therefore $p$ is primitive at $n$.

### 5.3. Scope of the finite certificate

For each composite $n$ with $14\le n\le 10{,}000$, direct evaluation of the deterministic stripping procedure yields $P_n>1$. Combining Lemma 5.3 with the prime-index argument gives Theorem 5.1.

The bound $10{,}000$ is part of the theorem, not an incidental implementation detail. A finite certificate over this interval cannot establish an unbounded statement for composite indices. Extending the result past the certified range requires independent lower bounds for suitable cyclotomic primitive parts of Fibonacci numbers. This distinction prevents a finite verification from being mistaken for an infinite proof.

## 6. Exact apparition at prime indices

The synchronization theorem rests on primitive divisors at prime indices.

### Theorem 6.1 (Prime-index primitive divisor existence)

For every prime $q\ge 13$, there exists a prime $p$ that is a primitive prime divisor of $F_q$.

This theorem supplies a prime whose first positive appearance occurs at $q$. Primality of $q$ then sharpens first appearance into an exact description of all appearances.

### Theorem 6.2 (Exact Apparition Theorem)

Let $q\ge 13$ be prime. Then there exists a prime $p$ such that, for every $m\ge 0$,

$$
p\mid F_m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad q\mid m.
$$

#### Proof sketch

Choose a primitive prime divisor $p$ of $F_q$ using Theorem 6.1.

Suppose first that $p\mid F_m$. Since $p\mid F_q$, Theorem 3.1 implies

$$
p\mid \gcd(F_q,F_m)=F_{\gcd(q,m)}.
$$

Because $q$ is prime and $\gcd(q,m)\mid q$, either $\gcd(q,m)=1$ or $\gcd(q,m)=q$. The first alternative would imply $p\mid F_1=1$, impossible for a prime. Hence $\gcd(q,m)=q$, so $q\mid m$.

Conversely, if $q\mid m$, Corollary 3.2 gives $F_q\mid F_m$. Since $p\mid F_q$, transitivity yields $p\mid F_m$.

### Remark 6.3 (Why prime indices are special)

For composite $q$, a divisor of $q$ may lie strictly between $1$ and $q$. The gcd $\gcd(q,m)$ therefore need not collapse to the two cases used above. Primitive divisor existence at a composite index still implies that its rank of apparition is $q$, and rank-of-apparition theory can often recover the same multiples law, but the elementary two-case argument in Theorem 6.2 uses primality essentially.

### Example 6.4

Take $q=13$. Since

$$
F_{13}=233
$$

and $233$ is prime, $p=233$ is primitive at index $13$. Hence

$$
233\mid F_m\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 13\mid m.
$$

For example, $F_{26}=121393=233\cdot 521$, whereas $233\nmid F_{27}$.

## 7. Finite-family synchronization

We now combine exact apparition with finite gcd transport.

### Theorem 7.1 (Finite-Family Synchronization Theorem)

Let $q\ge 13$ be prime. There exists a prime $p$ such that, for every finite set $S\subseteq\mathbb N$,

$$
p\mid \gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\mid \gcd(S).
$$

The same prime $p$ works simultaneously for every finite set $S$.

#### Proof sketch

Choose $p$ from Theorem 6.2. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
p\mid \gcd\{F_n:n\in S\}
&\Longleftrightarrow p\mid F_n\text{ for every }n\in S\\
&\Longleftrightarrow q\mid n\text{ for every }n\in S\\
&\Longleftrightarrow q\mid \gcd(S).
\end{aligned}
$$

The first and last equivalences are the universal property of finite gcd; the middle equivalence is exact apparition applied elementwise. For $S=\varnothing$, both divisibility statements are true because the two gcds are $0$.

### Corollary 7.2 (Pair synchronization)

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 7.1, for all $a,b\ge 0$,

$$
p\mid \gcd(F_a,F_b)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\mid \gcd(a,b).
$$

### Corollary 7.3 (Family-wide divisibility test)

Under the same hypotheses,

$$
p\mid F_n\text{ for every }n\in S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
q\mid n\text{ for every }n\in S.
$$

### Example 7.4

Let $q=13$, $p=233$, and $S=\{26,39,65\}$. Since $\gcd(S)=13$, Theorem 7.1 gives

$$
233\mid \gcd(F_{26},F_{39},F_{65}).
$$

Indeed, every index is divisible by $13$. If $65$ is replaced by $66$, the gcd of the indices becomes $1$, so $233$ no longer divides the gcd of the corresponding Fibonacci values.

### Example 7.5

Let $S=\{0,26,52\}$. Its gcd is $26$, and $F_0=0$. The presence of index $0$ does not cause an exception:

$$
\gcd(F_0,F_{26},F_{52})=\gcd(0,F_{26},F_{52})=F_{26}.
$$

Thus $233$ divides the family gcd, exactly as $13\mid 26$ predicts.

## 8. Algorithms

### 8.1. Fast finite-family gcd evaluation

**Input:** A finite list of nonnegative indices $n_1,\ldots,n_r$.

**Output:** The gcd $\gcd(F_{n_1},\ldots,F_{n_r})$.

**Method:** Compute $g=\gcd(n_1,\ldots,n_r)$ and return $F_g$ using fast doubling.

The gcd fold requires $O(r\log M)$ bit operations up to multiplication-model factors, where $M=\max n_i$. Fast doubling computes $F_g$ with $O(\log g)$ big-integer multiplication steps. The method can be exponentially more economical in output size than generating every $F_{n_i}$, since $F_n$ has $\Theta(n)$ bits.

### 8.2. Synchronization decision

Once a synchronizing pair $(q,p)$ is known, deciding whether $p$ divides the gcd of a finite Fibonacci family requires only the index gcd:

1. Compute $g=\gcd(S)$.
2. Return whether $q\mid g$.

No Fibonacci number and no large prime-factor computation is needed. The arithmetic cost is dominated by the gcd fold on the indices.

### 8.3. Primitive-part certification

For a target index $n$:

1. Compute $F_n$.
2. Enumerate all positive proper divisors $d$ of $n$.
3. Initialize $R=F_n$.
4. For each $d$, repeatedly divide $R$ by $\gcd(R,F_d)$ until the gcd is $1$.
5. If $R>1$, choose a prime factor $p$ of $R$.
6. Conclude from Lemma 5.3 that $p$ is primitive at $n$.

This direct method is mathematically transparent but not intended as the asymptotically fastest way to study large indices. Enumerating divisors by trial division costs $O(\sqrt n)$ integer divisions; computing Fibonacci values by fast doubling is logarithmic in the index in terms of big-integer multiplications; factoring the final remainder may dominate. Its role is certification over a finite range, not replacement of cyclotomic theory.

## 9. Applications

### 9.1. Index-first exact arithmetic

Theorem 4.1 provides an exact simplification rule for symbolic and numerical systems. Given a family of Fibonacci expressions, a gcd engine can reduce the indices before constructing large values. This is especially effective when the original indices are large but share a small gcd.

### 9.2. Modular event schedules

Theorem 6.2 realizes the multiples of a prime $q$ as the exact set of times at which a fixed prime $p$ divides the Fibonacci state. This can model periodic trigger schedules: the recurrence provides the state evolution, while reduction modulo $p$ reveals a clock of period $q$ at zero.

### 9.3. Distributed synchronization

For multiple agents labeled by indices in $S$, Theorem 7.1 says that a common modular event occurs precisely when all labels lie in the multiples of $q$, equivalently when $q$ divides their gcd. The result is a mathematical synchronization primitive: a global common-factor condition is reduced to a meet computation on labels.

### 9.4. Structural transfer to other sequences

The finite transport proof uses only a binary identity of the form

$$
\gcd(U_a,U_b)=U_{\gcd(a,b)}
$$

and the convention $U_0=0$. Consequently, the finite-family theorem transfers immediately to every strong divisibility sequence with those properties. What remains sequence-specific is primitive-divisor existence and the resulting apparition law.

## 10. Limitations and discussion

The results have three distinct scopes.

First, finite gcd transport is completely general over all finite index sets and all nonnegative indices. It has no lower or upper bound.

Second, exact synchronization is unbounded over prime indices $q\ge 13$. Its proof depends on the availability of a primitive divisor at each such prime index and on the two-element divisor structure of a prime.

Third, the all-index primitive-divisor theorem presented here is restricted to $13\le n\le 10{,}000$. Composite indices in that interval are covered by a finite primitive-part certificate. The unbounded composite tail is not a consequence of the certificate and is not asserted.

This separation illustrates a useful methodological point. The synchronization mechanism itself is qualitative and inexpensive: once exact apparition is known, finite-family synchronization follows immediately. Quantitative growth estimates enter only in guaranteeing primitive divisors across broader classes of indices. One may therefore develop cyclotomic bounds independently and attach them to the synchronization framework at a single interface.

The empty family and index zero deserve explicit mention. With the convention $\gcd(\varnothing)=0$, finite transport remains exact because $F_0=0$. Divisibility statements remain valid because every positive integer divides $0$. Families containing zero also behave naturally because $\gcd(0,n)=n$.

## 11. Future work

A first objective is composite-index synchronization through cyclotomic primitive parts. For every nonexceptional composite index, one expects a primitive prime whose divisibility locus is exactly the multiples of that index. Finite gcd transport would then lift the single-index law to every finite family without additional quantitative work.

A second direction concerns nondegenerate Lucas sequences. Whenever such a sequence satisfies strong divisibility, the finite-meet argument applies unchanged. The remaining tasks are to classify exceptional indices and establish primitive-divisor and apparition theorems appropriate to the sequence.

A third direction is a dual least-common-multiple law for ranks of apparition. For suitably independent moduli, the rank of their product should be controlled by the least common multiple of the individual ranks, with local corrections at primes dividing the discriminant.

A fourth direction is elliptic divisibility. Denominator sequences associated with nontorsion rational points often exhibit divisibility behavior reminiscent of Fibonacci numbers. Primitive prime divisors may induce finite-family synchronization outside an exceptional set governed by bad reduction.

Finally, effective bounds are needed. Since synchronization has no additional quantitative cost after primitive-divisor existence, any explicit bound for primitive divisors immediately becomes an explicit bound for synchronizing primes. Establishing bounds polynomial in suitable logarithmic heights would turn the structural theory into an effective general-purpose tool.

## 12. Conclusion

The Fibonacci sequence preserves finite gcds exactly. At every prime index $q\ge 13$, a primitive prime divisor upgrades this meet-preserving behavior into an exact periodic signal: one prime $p$ divides $F_m$ precisely at multiples of $q$. The same $p$ then synchronizes every finite family, converting a common-divisor question among Fibonacci values into a gcd question among indices.

The resulting chain is concise:

$$
\text{strong divisibility}
\Longrightarrow
\text{finite gcd transport}
\Longrightarrow
\text{exact apparition}
\Longrightarrow
\text{finite-family synchronization}.
$$

Alongside this unbounded prime-index result, the primitive-part method certifies primitive prime divisors for every index from $13$ through $10{,}000$. The explicit boundary keeps finite computation and infinite theory properly separated. Together, these results reveal a robust principle: in strong divisibility sequences, first appearances of prime factors can govern the simultaneous arithmetic of entire finite families.

## Abstract

The tree of primitive Pythagorean triples is generated by three integer matrices $M_1, M_2, M_3 \in \mathrm{O}(2,1;\mathbb{Z})$ acting on column vectors $(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$. We determine the exact spectral data of these generators and the exact behaviour of their reductions modulo an integer, and we show that the resulting *modular resonance frequencies* separate the prime factors of a semiprime.

Two of the generators, $M_1$ and $M_3$, are unipotent with characteristic polynomial $(X-1)^3$ and nilpotency index exactly $3$; their powers admit closed polynomial forms, from which we derive that their multiplicative order modulo any odd $m$ is exactly $m$, and a *factoring barrier*: every greatest common divisor extracted from a unipotent branch divides $\gcd(k^2,m)$, so the branch carries no factoring information at all.

The middle generator $M_2$ is hyperbolic, with characteristic polynomial $(X+1)(X^2-6X+1)$ and eigenvalues $-1$ and $3 \pm 2\sqrt 2 = (1\pm\sqrt2)^{\pm2}$, the square of the silver ratio and a norm-one unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. We prove, by an explicit matrix Frobenius computation, that modulo an odd prime $p$ the order of $M_2$ divides $p-1$ when $p \equiv \pm1 \pmod 8$ and divides $p+1$ when $p\equiv\pm3\pmod 8$, and always divides $p^2-1$; exactly, $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2) = \mathrm{lcm}(2,\mathrm{ord}_p(U))$ where $U = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&2\\4&3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the hyperbolic block. We prove further that $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $M_2$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ if and only if $(\lambda+1)(\lambda^2-6\lambda+1)=0$, that the quadratic factor splits over $\mathbb{F}_p$ if and only if $2$ is a quadratic residue mod $p$, and hence that the **split/inert dichotomy of the spectrum is literally the $p-1$ versus $p+1$ frequency dichotomy**.

Combining these we obtain a provably correct factoring criterion: if an exponent $k$ is a resonance of $p$ but not of $q$, then some entry $x$ of the integer matrix $M_2^{\,k}-I$ satisfies $\gcd(x,pq)=p$ exactly. We also prove the converse barrier: if $k$ is a resonance of the whole modulus, every attainable gcd equals $N$. Finally we extract the *Berggren–Lucas trace sequence* $t(k)=\mathrm{tr}(M_2^k)$, obeying $t(k+3)=5t(k+2)+5t(k+1)-t(k)$ with $t(0..3)=3,5,35,197$, and prove the Fermat-type congruence $t(p)\equiv 5 \pmod p$ for every odd prime $p$, yielding a linear-time compositeness test.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, Lorentz group $\mathrm{O}(2,1)$, silver ratio, quadratic reciprocity, Frobenius endomorphism, Lucas sequences, integer factorization, Pollard $p-1$, Williams $p+1$.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The tree of right triangles

A *primitive Pythagorean triple* is a triple of positive integers $(a,b,c)$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ and $\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. It is a classical observation, associated with the names Barning, Hall and Berggren, that the set of all such triples forms an infinite ternary tree rooted at $(3,4,5)$, whose edges are given by three fixed integer matrices. In the normalization used throughout this paper these are

$$
M_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M_3 = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 3\end{pmatrix},
$$

acting on column vectors. Applied to $(3,4,5)$ they yield $(5,12,13)$, $(21,20,29)$ and $(15,8,17)$ respectively; iterating produces every primitive triple exactly once.

The structural reason for the tree's existence is that these matrices are integral isometries of the Lorentz form
$$Q(a,b,c) = a^2 + b^2 - c^2, \qquad Q = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1),$$
so that Pythagorean triples are exactly the integral null vectors of a $(2{+}1)$-dimensional Minkowski space and the tree is an orbit of a discrete group of Lorentz transformations.

### 1.2 What this paper does

This paper studies the *reductions modulo $N$* of the three generators, and asks: after how many steps does the Berggren dynamics return to the identity? We call this exponent the **resonant frequency** of the generator at the modulus. The results below determine it completely, tie it to the eigenvalue structure of $M_2$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$, and convert the resulting arithmetic into a factoring criterion for semiprimes $N=pq$ and a compositeness test.

The three main conceptual findings are:

1. **Only the hyperbolic branch carries arithmetic information.** The unipotent generators have order exactly equal to the modulus and provably yield no factoring advantage.
2. **The resonant frequency of the hyperbolic branch is $p-1$ or $p+1$ according to $p \bmod 8$**, and this dichotomy is exactly the dichotomy of whether the eigenvalues of $M_2$ are visible in $\mathbb{F}_p$ or hidden in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.
3. **Frequency misalignment at the two primes of $N=pq$ is necessary and sufficient** for the resonance test to split $N$.

### 1.3 Relation to known factoring methods

The factoring criterion obtained here belongs to the family of Pollard's $p-1$ and Williams' $p+1$ methods, and, like them, it runs quickly precisely when $p-1$ or $p+1$ is smooth. It is not a break of RSA, and primes generated for cryptographic use are chosen so that neither $p\pm1$ is smooth. What is distinctive is (i) the provenance — the criterion is derived from the symmetry group of Pythagorean triples rather than postulated — and (ii) that a single fixed matrix realises both the $p-1$ and the $p+1$ mechanism simultaneously, with the residue class of $p$ modulo $8$ automatically selecting the operative one.

## 2. Notation and preliminaries

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring, $I$ is the identity matrix of the appropriate size, and $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ for a prime $p$. For an integer matrix $A$ and $m \in \mathbb{N}$ we write $A \bmod m$ for the entrywise reduction, a matrix over $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$; reduction is a ring homomorphism, so $(A \bmod m)^k = A^k \bmod m$.

We record the elementary but constantly used translation between matrix congruences and divisibility.

**Lemma 2.1 (Congruence criterion).** *For any $N \in \mathbb{N}$ and any integer matrix $A$,*
$$A \equiv I \pmod N \iff N \mid (A - I)_{ij} \text{ for all } i,j.$$

*Proof.* Reduction mod $N$ is entrywise, and an element of $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$ is zero exactly when the integer representing it is divisible by $N$. $\square$

We also use the second supplement to quadratic reciprocity: for an odd prime $p$,
$$2 \text{ is a square in } \mathbb{F}_p \iff p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8,$$
and Euler's criterion, $a^{(p-1)/2} = \pm1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ according to whether $a$ is a square.

## 3. Exact spectral data of the generators

### 3.1 Determinants, traces, characteristic polynomials

**Proposition 3.1.** $\det M_1 = \det M_3 = 1$, $\det M_2 = -1$; $\mathrm{tr}\,M_1 = \mathrm{tr}\,M_3 = 3$, $\mathrm{tr}\,M_2 = 5$.

**Theorem 3.2 (Spectral classification).**
$$\chi_{M_1}(X) = \chi_{M_3}(X) = (X-1)^3, \qquad \chi_{M_2}(X) = (X+1)(X^2-6X+1).$$

*Proof sketch.* Direct expansion of $\det(X I - M_i)$ for $3\times 3$ matrices, followed by factorisation. For $M_1$: $X^3 - 3X^2 + 3X - 1$. For $M_2$: $X^3-5X^2-5X+1$, which has the obvious root $X=-1$ (as $\mathrm{tr} M_2 = 5$ and $\det M_2 = -1$ force the cofactor structure) and quotient $X^2-6X+1$. $\square$

Thus $M_1$ and $M_3$ are **unipotent** and $M_2$ is **hyperbolic**.

**Proposition 3.3 (Exact nilpotency index).** $(M_1-I)^3 = (M_3-I)^3 = 0$, while $(M_1-I)^2 \neq 0$ and $(M_3-I)^2\neq 0$. Hence the nilpotency index of $M_i - I$ is exactly $3$ for $i \in \{1,3\}$.

### 3.2 The hyperbolic eigenvalues are silver-ratio units

**Theorem 3.4 (Silver-ratio spectrum).** The roots of $X^2 - 6X + 1$ are $3 \pm 2\sqrt2$. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ one has
$$3 + 2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2, \qquad \mathrm{N}(3+2\sqrt2) = 3^2 - 2\cdot 2^2 = 1, \qquad (3+2\sqrt2)(3-2\sqrt2)=1.$$
Thus the two hyperbolic eigenvalues of $M_2$ are mutually inverse units of norm $1$, namely the square of the fundamental unit (the *silver ratio*) $1+\sqrt2$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$.

Numerically $3+2\sqrt2 \approx 5.8284$: iterating $M_2$ multiplies the hypotenuse asymptotically by this factor at each step, which is why the $M_2$-branch of the tree $(3,4,5) \to (21,20,29)\to(119,120,169)\to\cdots$ grows geometrically.

**Proposition 3.5 (Minimal polynomial and eigenline).** $(M_2+I)(M_2^2 - 6M_2 + I) = 0$, and neither factor vanishes; hence $X+1$ and $X^2-6X+1$ are precisely the irreducible constituents of the minimal polynomial of $M_2$. The vector $(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}$ spans the $(-1)$-eigenline: $M_2 (1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T} = -(1,-1,0)^{\mathsf T}$. Moreover $M_2^{\mathsf T} = M_2$, so $M_2$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$.

### 3.3 Lorentz structure

**Theorem 3.6 (Integral isometries).** Over every commutative ring $R$,
$$M_i^{\mathsf T}\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1)\,M_i = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,-1), \qquad i = 1,2,3.$$
Consequently, writing $\Phi(v) = v_0^2+v_1^2-v_2^2$, one has $\Phi(M_i v) = \Phi(v)$ for all $v \in R^3$.

*Proof sketch.* A finite verification of nine bilinear identities per generator; because the statement is a polynomial identity with integer coefficients it holds over $\mathbb{Z}$ and hence over every commutative ring by base change. $\square$

**Corollary 3.7 (Triples to triples, over $\mathbb{Z}$ and mod $N$).** If $a^2+b^2=c^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, then the image $(a',b',c') = M_i(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}$ again satisfies $a'^2+b'^2=c'^2$. The same holds over $\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z}$: the Berggren dynamics preserves the conic $a^2+b^2=c^2$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^3$.

This is the group-theoretic explanation of the tree: the tree exists because the generators lie in the orthogonal group of the Pythagorean form, not because of an accidental algebraic identity.

## 4. The unipotent branches: closed forms, exact order, and a factoring barrier

### 4.1 Closed-form powers

**Theorem 4.1 (Unipotent closed forms).** For all $k \ge 0$,
$$
M_1^{\,k} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2k & 2k \\ 2k & 1-2k^2 & 2k^2 \\ 2k & -2k^2 & 1+2k^2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
M_3^{\,k} = \begin{pmatrix} 1-2k^2 & 2k & 2k^2 \\ -2k & 1 & 2k \\ -2k^2 & 2k & 1+2k^2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $k$: the base case is the identity, and the inductive step is a $3\times3$ product whose entries simplify by $\left(2(k+1)\right)$- and $\left(2(k+1)^2\right)$-identities. Conceptually the forms come from $M_i = I + \eta$ with $\eta^3 = 0$, giving $M_i^k = I + k\eta + \binom{k}{2}\eta^2$, a matrix polynomial of degree $2$ in $k$. $\square$

### 4.2 Exact resonance

**Theorem 4.2 (Exact unipotent resonance).** Let $m$ be odd. Then for every $k \ge 0$,
$$M_1^{\,k}\equiv I \pmod m \iff m \mid k,$$
and likewise for $M_3$. Consequently, for odd $m > 0$,
$$\mathrm{ord}_m(M_1) = \mathrm{ord}_m(M_3) = m.$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) The $(0,1)$ entry of $M_1^k - I$ is $-2k$; if the congruence holds then $m \mid 2k$, and $m$ odd forces $m \mid k$. ($\Leftarrow$) If $m \mid k$ then $m \mid 2k$ and $m \mid 2k^2$, so by Theorem 4.1 every off-diagonal entry vanishes and every diagonal entry is $1$ modulo $m$. The order statement follows because the set of resonant exponents is exactly $m\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.3.** For a semiprime $N = pq$ with $p,q$ odd, $\mathrm{ord}_N(M_1) = pq$. The two prime frequencies are *not separated* by the unipotent flow: there is no exponent in resonance with $p$ but not with $q$ other than the multiples of $p$ themselves.

### 4.3 The factoring barrier

**Lemma 4.4 (Entry classification).** Every entry of $M_1^{\,k} - I$ is one of $0,\ \pm2k,\ \pm2k^2$.

**Theorem 4.5 (Unipotent factoring barrier).** Let $N$ be odd and $k \ge 0$. For every entry $x$ of $M_1^{\,k}-I$ with $x \neq 0$,
$$\gcd(x, N) \ \big|\ \gcd(k^2, N).$$

*Proof sketch.* Put $g=\gcd(x,N)$. By Lemma 4.4, $x \mid$-divides one of $\pm 2k, \pm 2k^2$, so $g \mid 2k^2$; as $g \mid N$ and $N$ is odd, $g$ is odd and coprime to $2$, whence $g \mid k^2$. Together with $g \mid N$ this gives $g \mid \gcd(k^2,N)$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.6 (No factoring advantage).** If a prime $r$ divides $\gcd(x,N)$ for some nonzero entry $x$ of $M_1^k - I$ with $N$ odd, then $r \mid k$ and $r \mid N$. In other words, the unipotent branch can only reveal primes already present in the exponent: one must know a factor of $N$ before the method can display it.

This is a genuine impossibility result, and it is what focuses all subsequent attention on $M_2$: **resonance requires oscillation**, and a unipotent (polynomially drifting) flow has no phase to be in or out of.

## 5. Hyperbolic resonance: the frequencies $p \mp 1$

### 5.1 Block normal form

Let
$$
W = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\1&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
U = \begin{pmatrix}3&2\\4&3\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
V = \begin{pmatrix}3&2&0\\4&3&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The columns of $W$ are the vectors $(1,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$, which span the plane on which $M_2$ acts hyperbolically, and the $(-1)$-eigenvector $(1,-1,0)$.

**Theorem 5.1 (Integral block diagonalisation).** Over every commutative ring, $M_2 W = W V$, hence $M_2^{\,k} W = W V^{\,k}$ for all $k \geq 0$. Moreover $\det W = 2$ and $\det U = 1$, and
$$V^{\,k} = \begin{pmatrix} U^{\,k} & 0 \\ 0 & (-1)^k\end{pmatrix}.$$

Since $\det W = 2$, the matrix $W$ is invertible over $\mathbb{F}_p$ for every odd prime $p$, so over $\mathbb{F}_p$ the conjugation is a genuine change of basis. This reduces every question about $M_2$ mod $p$ to a question about the $2\times2$ block $U$ together with the sign $(-1)^k$.

### 5.2 The block $U$ *is* the number $3+2\sqrt 2$

Write $S = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&2\\4&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

**Lemma 5.2.** $U = 3I + S$ and $S^2 = 8I$. Moreover $U\,(6I - U) = I$, so $U^{-1} = 6I - U$ and $U$ is a unit of determinant $1$.

Thus the subring $\mathbb{Z}[S] \subseteq M_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is a faithful matrix model of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt 8] = \mathbb{Z}[2\sqrt2]$, with $S$ playing the role of $2\sqrt2$ and $U$ the role of $3+2\sqrt2$.

### 5.3 The Frobenius computation

**Theorem 5.3 (Matrix Frobenius).** Let $p$ be an odd prime. Over $\mathbb{F}_p$,
$$U^{\,p} = 3I + 8^{(p-1)/2}\, S.$$

*Proof sketch.* Since $3I$ is central, $(3I+S)^p = (3I)^p + S^p$ by the freshman's-dream identity in characteristic $p$ for commuting elements. Then $(3I)^p = 3^p I = 3I$ by Fermat, and, writing $p = 2\cdot\frac{p-1}{2}+1$,
$$S^p = (S^2)^{(p-1)/2} S = (8I)^{(p-1)/2} S = 8^{(p-1)/2} S. \qquad\square$$

Because $8 = 2^3$ and $\left(2^{(p-1)/2}\right)^2 = 1$ (Euler), we have $8^{(p-1)/2} = \left(2^{(p-1)/2}\right)^3 = 2^{(p-1)/2} \in \{+1,-1\}$: the Legendre symbol of $2$.

**Theorem 5.4 (Block resonance).** Let $p$ be an odd prime.
1. If $2^{(p-1)/2} = 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ then $U^{\,p} = U$, hence $U^{\,p-1} = I$.
2. If $2^{(p-1)/2} = -1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ then $U^{\,p} = 6I - U = U^{-1}$, hence $U^{\,p+1} = I$.

*Proof sketch.* Case 1 is immediate from Theorem 5.3 and $U = 3I+S$; multiply $U^{p-1}\cdot U = U^p = U$ on the right by $U^{-1} = 6I-U$. Case 2: with $8^{(p-1)/2} = -1$, Theorem 5.3 gives $U^p = 3I - S = 6I - (3I+S) = 6I - U$, and $U(6I-U) = I$ by Lemma 5.2, so $U^{p+1} = U^p U = I$. $\square$

Conceptually: in case 1 the eigenvalues $3\pm2\sqrt2$ lie in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$, a cyclic group of order $p-1$; in case 2 they lie in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ and are conjugate, so the Frobenius inverts them, placing them on the norm-one torus of order $p+1$.

### 5.4 Transport back to $M_2$

**Lemma 5.5 (Transport).** Let $p$ be an odd prime and suppose $U^{\,k}=I$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ with $k$ even. Then $M_2^{\,k} \equiv I \pmod p$.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 5.1, $V^k = \mathrm{diag}(U^k, (-1)^k) = I$ and $M_2^k W = W V^k = W$; as $\det W = 2 \neq 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$, $W$ is invertible and $M_2^k = I$. $\square$

The parity condition is exactly the contribution of the $(-1)$-eigenvalue. It is automatically satisfied: $p\mp1$ are even for odd $p$.

**Theorem 5.6 (Hyperbolic Resonance Theorem).** Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then
$$
p \equiv \pm 1 \!\!\pmod 8 \ \Longrightarrow\ M_2^{\,p-1}\equiv I \!\!\pmod p,
\qquad
p \equiv \pm 3 \!\!\pmod 8 \ \Longrightarrow\ M_2^{\,p+1}\equiv I \!\!\pmod p,
$$
and in all cases
$$M_2^{\,p^2-1}\equiv I \pmod p .$$

*Proof sketch.* Combine Euler's criterion with the second supplement to quadratic reciprocity to convert the residue class of $p$ mod $8$ into the value of $2^{(p-1)/2}$; apply Theorem 5.4 and Lemma 5.5. The uniform statement follows from $p^2-1 = (p-1)(p+1)$ and the fact that a resonance divides all its multiples. $\square$

### 5.5 The exact resonance spectrum and the order formula

The theorems above give sufficient exponents. The next result determines the resonance set exactly.

**Theorem 5.7 (Exact resonance spectrum).** Let $p$ be an odd prime and $k \ge 0$. Then
$$M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \pmod p \iff \bigl(U^{\,k} = I \text{ in } \mathbb{F}_p \text{ and } k \text{ is even}\bigr).$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Leftarrow$) is Lemma 5.5. ($\Rightarrow$): conjugating by the invertible $W$ turns $M_2^k=I$ into $V^k = I$, i.e. $U^k = I$ and $(-1)^k = 1$; the latter forces $k$ even, since $-1 \ne 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ for odd $p$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.8 (Order formula).** For every odd prime $p$,
$$\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2) = \mathrm{lcm}\bigl(2,\ \mathrm{ord}_p(U)\bigr).$$

*Proof sketch.* The resonance set of $M_2$ is the intersection of $2\mathbb{Z}$ with $\mathrm{ord}_p(U)\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $\mathrm{lcm}(2,\mathrm{ord}_p(U))\mathbb{Z}$; the order is its positive generator. $\square$

This is the sharp form of the resonance theorem: the frequency bound $p\mp1$ of Theorem 5.6 is attained exactly when $U$ generates the full cyclic group ($\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ or the norm-one torus of $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$), which is a primitive-root condition. Small examples: the bound is attained for $p = 3,5,7,11,13,19,23,31,37,43,47,53,61,67,71$, and fails for $p = 17$ (order $8$, not $16$), $p=29$ (order $10$, not $30$), $p=41$ (order $10$, not $40$), $p=59$ (order $20$, not $60$), $p=73$ (order $36$), $p=79$ (order $26$).

## 6. The eigenvalue distribution modulo $p$

The resonance results were about orders. We now show that the same dichotomy is visible directly in the *spectrum*, giving the sense in which the resonant frequency is "read off the eigenvalues".

**Lemma 6.1 (Determinant in closed form).** Over any commutative ring $R$ and for every $\lambda \in R$,
$$\det(M_2 - \lambda I) = -(\lambda+1)(\lambda^2-6\lambda+1).$$

**Theorem 6.2 (Eigenvalue criterion).** Let $p$ be a prime and $\lambda \in \mathbb{F}_p$. There exists a nonzero $v \in \mathbb{F}_p^3$ with $M_2 v = \lambda v$ if and only if
$$(\lambda+1)(\lambda^2-6\lambda+1) = 0 \quad\text{in } \mathbb{F}_p.$$

*Proof sketch.* Over a field, $(M_2-\lambda I)v=0$ has a nonzero solution iff $\det(M_2-\lambda I)=0$; apply Lemma 6.1 and cancel the sign. $\square$

**Proposition 6.3.** $\lambda = -1$ is an eigenvalue of $M_2$ modulo every prime, with eigenvector $(1,-1,0)$.

**Theorem 6.4 (Splitting criterion).** Let $p$ be an odd prime. Then
$$\exists \lambda \in \mathbb{F}_p:\ \lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 1 = 0 \iff 2 \text{ is a square in } \mathbb{F}_p .$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) If $\lambda^2-6\lambda+1=0$ then $\left((\lambda-3)/2\right)^2 = (\lambda^2-6\lambda+9)/4 = 8/4 = 2$; the division by $2$ is legitimate since $p$ is odd. ($\Leftarrow$) If $2 = s^2$ then $\lambda = 3+2s$ satisfies $\lambda^2-6\lambda+1 = 4s^2-8 = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5 (Split case).** Let $p$ be an odd prime with $p \equiv \pm1\pmod 8$. Then $M_2$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda \neq -1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$; concretely both roots $3\pm 2s$ (where $s^2=2$) exist in $\mathbb{F}_p$, they are distinct, and neither equals $-1$. The spectrum of $M_2$ over $\mathbb{F}_p$ therefore consists of three eigenvalues.

*Proof sketch.* Existence is Theorem 6.4 plus the second supplement. That no root equals $-1$: substituting $\lambda=-1$ into $\lambda^2-6\lambda+1$ gives $8 = 2^3$, and $2^3 = 0$ in the field $\mathbb{F}_p$ would force $2 = 0$, contradicting $p$ odd. Distinctness: the discriminant $36-4 = 32 = 2^5 \neq 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.6 (Inert case).** Let $p$ be an odd prime with $p \equiv \pm3\pmod 8$. Then the only eigenvalue of $M_2$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ is $-1$: any $\lambda$ with $(\lambda+1)(\lambda^2-6\lambda+1)=0$ satisfies $\lambda=-1$. The hyperbolic pair is *inert*, living in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$.

*Proof sketch.* If the quadratic factor vanished at some $\lambda$, Theorem 6.4 would make $2$ a square mod $p$, forcing $p\equiv\pm1\pmod 8$, contrary to hypothesis. $\square$

**Theorem 6.7 (Spectrum $\Rightarrow$ frequency).** Let $p$ be an odd prime.
1. If $M_2$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda\ne-1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ (split spectrum), then $M_2^{\,p-1}\equiv I\pmod p$.
2. If $-1$ is the only eigenvalue of $M_2$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$ (inert spectrum), then $M_2^{\,p+1}\equiv I\pmod p$.

*Proof sketch.* In case 1, the existence of a root of the quadratic makes $2$ a square by Theorem 6.4, hence $2^{(p-1)/2}=1$ by Euler, hence Theorem 5.4(1) and Lemma 5.5 apply. In case 2 one argues contrapositively: if $2$ were a square then by Theorem 6.4 the quadratic would have a root $\lambda$, which by hypothesis must equal $-1$, giving $2^3 = 0$ and hence $2=0$ — impossible. Therefore $2^{(p-1)/2}=-1$ and Theorem 5.4(2) applies. $\square$

This is the precise sense in which **the resonant frequency is a spectral invariant**: split spectrum $\Leftrightarrow$ frequency divides $p-1$; inert spectrum $\Leftrightarrow$ frequency divides $p+1$.

**Theorem 6.8 (Spectral misalignment at a semiprime).** Let $p,q$ be odd primes with $p \equiv \pm1 \pmod 8$ and $q\equiv\pm3\pmod8$. Then
$$M_2^{\,p-1}\equiv I \!\!\pmod p, \qquad M_2^{\,q+1}\equiv I \!\!\pmod q,$$
and the hyperbolic spectrum of $M_2$ is split at $p$ and inert at $q$. The two primes of $N=pq$ therefore carry *different spectral types*.

This structural mismatch is the source of the resonance misalignment exploited in the next section.

## 7. Resonance and factorization

### 7.1 Chinese remainder decomposition of resonance

**Theorem 7.1 (CRT decomposition of resonance).** Let $m,n$ be coprime and $k \ge 0$. Then
$$M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \!\!\pmod{mn} \iff M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \!\!\pmod{m} \text{ and } M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \!\!\pmod{n}.$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 2.1 the statement is entrywise divisibility. Divisibility by $mn$ implies divisibility by each; conversely, if $m$ and $n$ are coprime and both divide an integer, so does $mn$. $\square$

Hence **factoring $N=pq$ is exactly the problem of separating two resonant frequencies inside one modulus.**

### 7.2 The extraction lemma

**Lemma 7.2 (Factor extraction).** Let $p \ge 1$ and let $q$ be prime. If $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ satisfies $p \mid x$ and $q \nmid x$, then $\gcd(x, pq) = p$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $g=\gcd(x,pq)$. Since $p\mid x$ and $p \mid pq$, $p\mid g$; write $g=pt$. From $g \mid pq$ we get $t\mid q$, so $t \in\{1,q\}$ as $q$ is prime. If $t=q$ then $g=pq$, so $q \mid g \mid x$, contradicting $q\nmid x$. Hence $t=1$ and $g=p$. $\square$

### 7.3 The factoring theorem

**Theorem 7.3 (Resonance extracts a prime factor).** Let $N = pq$ with $p,q$ prime, and let $k$ be an exponent such that
$$M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \pmod p \qquad\text{but}\qquad M_2^{\,k}\not\equiv I \pmod q .$$
Then there exist indices $i,j$ such that the integer entry $x = (M_2^{\,k}-I)_{ij}$ satisfies
$$\gcd(x, N) = p .$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 2.1 the first hypothesis says $p$ divides every entry of $M_2^k - I$; the second says some entry $x$ is not divisible by $q$. Apply Lemma 7.2 to that entry. $\square$

**Corollary 7.4 (Canonical frequency).** If $p$ is an odd prime, $q$ a prime, and $M_2^{\,p^2-1}\not\equiv I \pmod q$, then the exponent $k = p^2-1$ splits $N=pq$ in the sense of Theorem 7.3.

**Corollary 7.5 (Sharp frequencies).** Let $p$ be an odd prime and $q$ prime.
- If $p\equiv\pm1\pmod 8$ and $M_2^{\,p-1}\not\equiv I \pmod q$, then $k=p-1$ splits $N=pq$.
- If $p\equiv\pm3\pmod 8$ and $M_2^{\,p+1}\not\equiv I \pmod q$, then $k=p+1$ splits $N=pq$.

**Theorem 7.6 (Alignment barrier).** If $M_2^{\,k}\equiv I \pmod N$, then for every $i,j$,
$$\gcd\bigl((M_2^{\,k}-I)_{ij},\, N\bigr) = N .$$
Hence resonance *misalignment* is not merely convenient for the method — it is necessary.

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 2.1, $N$ divides every entry, so $N$ divides the gcd; and the gcd divides $N$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.7 (Semiprime period bound).** Let $p\ne q$ be odd primes. Then modulo $N=pq$,
$$M_2^{\,\mathrm{lcm}(p^2-1,\;q^2-1)} \equiv I \pmod{N},$$
a Carmichael-type bound. The method of Theorem 7.3 works precisely at those exponents where this least common multiple has *not yet* been reached at both primes simultaneously.

### 7.4 Worked instances

**Example 7.8 ($N=15$).** $3 \equiv 3\pmod 8$, so the frequency of $3$ is $4$; $5\equiv5\pmod8$ gives $5$ the frequency $6$. Indeed $M_2^4 \equiv I \pmod 3$ while $M_2^4 \not\equiv I \pmod 5$, and
$$M_2^{4}-I = \begin{pmatrix}288&288&408\\288&288&408\\408&408&576\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gcd(288,15)=3.$$

**Example 7.9 (textbook RSA modulus $N=3233=53\cdot61$).** $53 \equiv 5 \pmod 8$, so the frequency of $53$ divides $54$; $61\equiv5\pmod 8$ gives $61$ the frequency dividing $62$. One verifies $M_2^{54}\equiv I \pmod{53}$ and $M_2^{54}\not\equiv I\pmod{61}$, so every nonzero-mod-$61$ entry of $M_2^{54}-I$ has gcd exactly $53$ with $3233$. The method returns the factor $53$.

**Example 7.10 (alignment).** Modulo $15$ the frequencies $4$ and $6$ align at $12$: $M_2^{12}\equiv I \pmod{15}$, and every gcd at $k=12$ equals $15$. This is Theorem 7.6 in action, and it explains why the practical algorithm must accumulate the exponent one prime power at a time and test after each step.

### 7.5 The contrast with the unipotent branch

Theorem 4.5 and Theorem 7.3 together constitute the main structural finding of this development:

> Among the three generators of the Pythagorean tree, **exactly one — the hyperbolic generator $M_2$ — carries prime-separating spectral information.** The unipotent generators $M_1$ and $M_3$ provably carry none.

## 8. The Berggren–Lucas trace sequence

### 8.1 Definition and recurrence

**Definition 8.1.** The *Berggren–Lucas sequence* is
$$t(k) = \mathrm{tr}\bigl(M_2^{\,k}\bigr) = (3+2\sqrt2)^k + (3-2\sqrt2)^k + (-1)^k, \qquad k \ge 0.$$

**Theorem 8.2 (Cayley–Hamilton and the recurrence).** $M_2^3 = 5M_2^2 + 5M_2 - I$, and consequently
$$t(k+3) = 5\,t(k+2) + 5\,t(k+1) - t(k), \qquad t(0)=3,\ t(1)=5,\ t(2)=35,\ t(3)=197 .$$

*Proof sketch.* The first identity is Cayley–Hamilton for $\chi_{M_2}(X)=X^3-5X^2-5X+1$ (verified directly). Multiplying by $M_2^k$ and taking traces, which is linear, gives the recurrence. $\square$

The first terms are
$$3,\ 5,\ 35,\ 197,\ 1155,\ 6725,\ 39203,\ 228485,\ 1331715,\ 7761797,\ \dots$$

### 8.2 The Fermat-type congruence

**Theorem 8.3 (Trace splitting).** For an odd prime $p$ and all $k\ge0$, over $\mathbb{F}_p$,
$$\mathrm{tr}\bigl(M_2^{\,k}\bigr) = \mathrm{tr}\bigl(U^{\,k}\bigr) + (-1)^k .$$

*Proof sketch.* Conjugation by the invertible $W$ preserves traces, and $V^k = \mathrm{diag}(U^k,(-1)^k)$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.4 (Berggren–Lucas congruence).** For every odd prime $p$,
$$t(p) \equiv 5 \pmod p .$$

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 5.3, $U^p = 3I + 8^{(p-1)/2}S$, and $S$ is traceless, so $\mathrm{tr}(U^p) = 6$ regardless of the sign. By Theorem 8.3, $t(p) \equiv 6 + (-1)^p = 6 - 1 = 5 \pmod p$, using that $p$ is odd. $\square$

Note $5 = t(1) = \mathrm{tr}(M_2)$: the congruence says the Frobenius fixes the trace, as it must, since the Frobenius permutes the eigenvalues.

### 8.3 A compositeness test

**Corollary 8.5 (Compositeness witness).** If $n$ is odd and $t(n) \not\equiv 5 \pmod n$, then $n$ is composite.

The test is *linear time* with the recurrence and *logarithmic time* via modular matrix exponentiation of $M_2$, and it requires no factorisation of $n$. It fails only on *Berggren pseudoprimes*.

**Example 8.6.** $t(9) = 7\,761\,797 \equiv 8 \pmod 9$, so $9$ is composite.

**Example 8.7 (empirical strength).** Of the $122$ odd composites $n$ with $9\le n<400$, the test certifies $118$; the exceptions — the first Berggren pseudoprimes — are $35$, $119$, $169$ and $385$.

## 9. Algorithms

### 9.1 Resonance search (Berggren $p\pm1$ method)

Given $N$ and a smoothness bound $B$, accumulate the exponent $E = \mathrm{lcm}(1,\dots,B)$ into $M_2$ modulo $N$ one prime power at a time, taking a gcd after each step:

```
Input:  odd composite N, smoothness bound B
Output: a nontrivial factor of N, or FAIL

A <- M2 mod N
for each prime r <= B:
    e <- largest power of r that is <= B
    A <- A^e mod N
    g <- gcd of all entries of (A - I) mod N, together with N
    if 1 < g < N: return g
    if g = N: return FAIL          # frequencies aligned; back off the exponent
return FAIL
```

**Correctness.** If at some stage the accumulated exponent $E$ is a multiple of $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2)$ but not of $\mathrm{ord}_q(M_2)$, Theorem 7.3 guarantees $g = p$. If $E$ is a multiple of both, Theorem 7.6 guarantees $g=N$ and the algorithm reports failure (in practice one restarts with a finer exponent ladder).

**Complexity.** $O(\pi(B))$ matrix exponentiations, each $O(\log B)$ modular $3\times3$ multiplications, so $\tilde O(B)$ modular multiplications of $O(\log N)$-bit numbers, plus one gcd per prime. The algorithm succeeds whenever $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2)$ is $B$-smooth for exactly one of the two primes; by Corollary 5.8 and Theorem 5.6, this order divides $p-1$ or $p+1$, so the method's success condition is **$B$-smoothness of $p-1$ or $p+1$** — the union of the conditions for Pollard's and Williams' methods.

### 9.2 Exact order computation

To compute $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2)$ for a known prime $p$: by Theorem 5.6, the order divides $p\mp1$ according to $p \bmod 8$; factor that number and remove prime factors greedily from the exponent while the power remains the identity. By Corollary 5.8 the answer equals $\mathrm{lcm}(2,\mathrm{ord}_p(U))$, so it suffices to work with the $2\times2$ block. Complexity: one factorisation of $p\mp1$ plus $O(\log p)$ modular exponentiations.

### 9.3 Trace compositeness test

Compute $t(n) \bmod n$ either by the linear recurrence in $O(n)$ steps or by modular exponentiation $M_2^n \bmod n$ in $O(\log n)$ matrix multiplications, and compare with $5$.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What the resonance mechanism is

Every result above is a consequence of one picture. The generator $M_2$ acts on a $3$-dimensional space that splits, over any ring where $2$ is invertible, into a hyperbolic plane and a reflected line. On the hyperbolic plane the action is multiplication by the unit $3+2\sqrt2$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. Reducing mod $p$, that unit lands either in $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ (a group of order $p-1$) or on the norm-one torus of $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ (a group of order $p+1$), and *which* is decided by whether $2$ is a square mod $p$ — that is, by $p \bmod 8$. The order of the unit in that group is the resonant frequency, up to the factor of $2$ contributed by the reflection.

Two distinct primes generically place their units in groups of different orders, so a single exponent can be a multiple of one order and not of the other; the gcd then separates them. Everything else is bookkeeping.

### 10.2 Honest limits

The factoring criterion is rigorous and unconditional, but its *efficiency* is exactly that of the $p\pm1$ family: it is fast only when $p-1$ or $p+1$ is smooth. RSA key generation standards specifically require strong primes or, more commonly, rely on the overwhelming probability that random large $p\pm1$ have a large prime factor. So Theorem 7.3 does not threaten deployed cryptography. Its value is threefold:

1. It shows that a classical geometric object — the tree of right triangles — contains, in its middle branch, a complete $p\pm1$ factoring engine, with the case selection performed automatically by quadratic reciprocity.
2. It gives an exact, not merely asymptotic, description of the resonance set (Theorem 5.7, Corollary 5.8), which makes the failure mode (Theorem 7.6) fully explicit.
3. It proves a matching *negative* result (Theorem 4.5) about the other two branches, which is rare: most factoring folklore says what works, not what provably cannot.

### 10.3 Applications beyond factoring

- **Primality testing.** The Berggren–Lucas congruence (Theorem 8.4) supplies a Lucas-type test whose underlying object is a $3$-dimensional representation with a $2$-dimensional torus part; combining it with a Fermat base-$2$ test is the natural Baillie–PSW analogue.
- **Pseudorandomness.** Because the orbit of $M_2$ on $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^3$ is confined to the conic $a^2+b^2=c^2$ (Corollary 3.7) and has period $p\mp1$, it gives a cheap, structured generator of points on that conic — of interest as a test object for equidistribution questions.
- **Pythagorean triples mod $p$.** Theorem 5.6 says the tree of triples, read modulo $p$, is a finite dynamical system with period dividing $p^2-1$; the root $(3,4,5)$ returns to itself after $p^2-1$ steps for every odd prime $p$. This gives an arithmetic-dynamics view of the tree that seems not to have been exploited.

## 11. Future directions

The following conjectures are each falsifiable by a single explicit prime or semiprime.

### C1. Sharpness of the resonance: $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2) = p\mp1$ for a positive-density set of primes

For $p\equiv\pm3\pmod8$ write $d(p) = \mathrm{ord}_p(M_2)$. We conjecture that $d(p) = p+1$ for a set of primes of positive natural density (in the range examined the bound is attained for $13$ of the $17$ primes tested), and dually $d(p)=p-1$ for a positive density of $p\equiv\pm1\pmod8$. Equivalently, $3+2\sqrt2$ is a primitive root of the norm-one torus of $\mathbb{F}_p(\sqrt2)$ infinitely often.

*Key insight.* The order formula $\mathrm{ord}_p(M_2) = \mathrm{lcm}(2,\mathrm{ord}_p(U))$ reduces the question to the multiplicative order of the fixed unit $3+2\sqrt2 = (1+\sqrt2)^2$ in a one-dimensional torus — precisely the setting of Artin's primitive-root conjecture for the norm-one subgroup, where Hooley-type conditional results (under GRH) are available.

*Why now.* The reduction to the torus is complete, so a conditional proof needs only the torus version of Artin's conjecture; the observed failures at $p = 17, 29, 41, 59$ give a ready-made test set.

### C2. The Berggren–Lucas primality test has no Carmichael numbers below its Pell modulus

Let $t(k)=\mathrm{tr}(M_2^k)$, with recurrence $t(k+3)=5t(k+2)+5t(k+1)-t(k)$ and $t(0..3) = 3,5,35,197$. We conjecture that there is **no odd composite** $n$ satisfying simultaneously
$$t(n) \equiv 5 \pmod n \qquad\text{and}\qquad t(n+1)\equiv t(n-1)\pmod n .$$
Dropping the second condition, pseudoprimes certainly exist (the smallest being $35$, $119$, $169$, $385$); the conjecture is that the two-sided test is Carmichael-free, as is believed for the Baillie–PSW combination.

*Key insight.* The condition $t(n)\equiv 5$ tests the Frobenius on the *trace* of a $3$-dimensional representation whose semisimple part is the norm-one torus of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, whereas the second condition tests the torus itself; a counterexample would have to be simultaneously Fermat-like for the split and the non-split torus at every prime divisor.

*Why now.* The congruence is established and the recurrence is linear-time computable, so the conjecture is directly testable to $10^9$, and a counterexample would immediately falsify it.

### C3. Resonance misalignment is generically achievable: a $p\pm1$-smoothness dichotomy

For a semiprime $N=pq$, define $B_{\mathrm{smooth}}(N)$ as the least $B$ such that some $k \le B!$ is a resonance of $p$ but not of $q$. We conjecture
$$B_{\mathrm{smooth}}(N) = \min\bigl(B(p-1),\,B(p+1),\,B(q-1),\,B(q+1)\bigr),$$
where $B(m)$ denotes the largest prime factor of $m$; i.e. the Berggren generator factors $N$ in time $\tilde O(B_{\mathrm{smooth}}(N))$, matching Pollard $p-1$ and Williams $p+1$ *simultaneously* with a single fixed matrix and no auxiliary parameter choice.

### Further avenues

- **Higher-rank analogues.** The Berggren tree generalises to trees of solutions of other quadratic forms; each such tree has its own hyperbolic generator and hence its own resonance frequency governed by a different splitting condition. A systematic catalogue would produce a family of $p\pm1$ engines indexed by real quadratic fields.
- **Distribution of Berggren pseudoprimes.** Are there infinitely many? Is their counting function $O(x^{1-\epsilon})$?
- **Equidistribution on the modular conic.** How well does the $M_2$-orbit of $(3,4,5)$ equidistribute among the $\approx p$ points of the conic $a^2+b^2=c^2$ in $\mathbb{P}^2(\mathbb{F}_p)$?

## 12. Conclusion

Starting from three integer matrices that generate the tree of primitive Pythagorean triples, we have determined their exact spectra ($(X-1)^3$ twice, $(X+1)(X^2-6X+1)$ once), identified the hyperbolic eigenvalues as the square of the silver ratio, and proved that these matrices are Lorentz isometries over every commutative ring. Reducing modulo a prime, we showed that the two unipotent generators have order exactly the modulus and provably carry no factoring information, while the hyperbolic generator has order $\mathrm{lcm}(2,\mathrm{ord}_p(U))$, dividing $p-1$ or $p+1$ according to $p \bmod 8$ — a dichotomy that coincides exactly with whether the hyperbolic eigenvalues are visible in $\mathbb{F}_p$ or hidden in $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$. Misaligned frequencies at the two primes of a semiprime yield a provably correct factor-extraction criterion, aligned frequencies provably yield nothing, and the trace sequence of the hyperbolic generator supplies a Fermat–Lucas compositeness test.

The unifying slogan is that **the spectrum of an arithmetic operator is a frequency, and different primes tune it differently**. In the Pythagorean tree, exactly one of the three branches is tuned at all.

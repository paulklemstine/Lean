## Abstract

We study function classes generated from real constants, coordinate functions, exponentiation, and field operations. On every compact space admitting a continuous injective map into a finite-dimensional Euclidean space, the algebra generated by exponentials of the embedding coordinates is uniformly dense in the space of continuous real-valued functions. In particular, finite exponential–field expressions are dense in $C([0,1]^n)$ for every $n$. The proof is an application of the real Stone–Weierstrass theorem: injectivity of the coordinate map and of the exponential function gives point separation. We then establish a dual uniqueness principle. Two continuous linear functionals on $C(X)$ that agree on the exponential-coordinate algebra agree on every continuous function. This connects universal approximation with the determination of stable linear observables. Finally, we give a quantitative shallow construction for the square function. The one-exponential expression

$$
S_h(x)=\frac{2}{h^2}\bigl(e^{hx}-1-hx\bigr)
$$

satisfies $\|S_h-x^2\|_\infty\le 4h/9$ on $[0,1]$ whenever $0<h\le1$. At $h=1/N$, this yields the explicit rate $4/(9N)$. We discuss constructive algorithms, stable numerical evaluation, applications, limitations, and the open problem of depth-sensitive approximation rates.

## 1. Introduction

Compositions and algebraic combinations of elementary transcendental functions form a natural language for modeling. Exponentials describe growth and decay, logarithms encode scale, and field operations combine elementary responses into richer expressions. A basic expressive-power question is whether this language can approximate arbitrary continuous functions on compact domains.

This paper gives a positive qualitative answer for a particularly small fragment. Logarithms and nested exponentials are unnecessary for density: constants, algebra operations, and exponentials of finitely many separating coordinates already suffice. The result is not tied specifically to a cube. It holds on any compact topological space that admits a continuous injective coordinate representation in $\mathbb R^n$.

The underlying mechanism is geometric. An algebra of real continuous functions is uniformly dense if it contains constants and separates points. Given distinct points, an injective Euclidean coordinate map distinguishes them in at least one coordinate. Applying the exponential preserves that distinction because $e^t$ is injective. The real Stone–Weierstrass theorem then supplies density.

Density has an immediate dual consequence. A continuous linear functional is determined by its values on any dense linear subspace. Since the exponential-coordinate algebra is dense, agreement of two stable linear observables on all finite exponential–field expressions forces agreement on all continuous functions. This principle is relevant to moment problems, integration, inverse problems, and any setting where a function is accessed through continuous linear measurements.

The Stone–Weierstrass argument is qualitative: it does not provide an expression-size or depth bound for a prescribed tolerance. To complement it, we analyze an explicit shallow approximation. A second-order finite-difference-like extraction from one exponential approximates $x^2$ on $[0,1]$. A uniform Taylor remainder estimate gives an $O(h)$ bound, hence an $O(1/N)$ guarantee at scale $h=1/N$.

The contributions are therefore threefold:

1. a universal approximation theorem for exponential-coordinate algebras on compact Euclidean-embeddable spaces;
2. a uniqueness theorem for continuous linear observables agreeing on that algebra; and
3. a certified uniform error rate for a one-exponential approximation to the square function.

No general fixed-depth rate is claimed. Establishing rates for arbitrary targets requires additional regularity assumptions and constructive approximation machinery. The present results isolate the qualitative foundation and one transparent quantitative model case.

## 2. Definitions and framework

### 2.1 Compact domains and the uniform norm

Let $X$ be a compact topological space. Denote by $C(X)$ the real vector space of continuous functions $f:X\to\mathbb R$. It is equipped with the uniform norm

$$
\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|.
$$

Compactness ensures that the supremum is finite and attained. With pointwise addition and multiplication, $C(X)$ is a commutative real algebra; with the uniform norm, it is a Banach space.

A subset $A\subseteq C(X)$ is **uniformly dense** if, for every $f\in C(X)$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $g\in A$ satisfying

$$
\|f-g\|_\infty<\varepsilon.
$$

Equivalently, the closure of $A$ in the uniform norm is all of $C(X)$.

### 2.2 Euclidean coordinate embeddings

Fix a positive integer $n$. A **Euclidean coordinate embedding** is a continuous injective map

$$
\iota:X\longrightarrow\mathbb R^n.
$$

Write

$$
\iota(x)=\bigl(\iota_1(x),\ldots,\iota_n(x)\bigr),
$$

where each coordinate $\iota_i:X\to\mathbb R$ is continuous.

For each $i$, define the **exponential coordinate**

$$
E_i(x)=e^{\iota_i(x)}.
$$

The function $E_i$ is continuous as a composition of continuous maps.

### 2.3 The exponential-coordinate algebra

The **exponential-coordinate algebra** $A_\iota$ is the smallest real subalgebra of $C(X)$ containing $E_1,\ldots,E_n$. Concretely, its elements are finite real polynomial expressions in the exponential coordinates:

$$
g(x)=P\bigl(e^{\iota_1(x)},\ldots,e^{\iota_n(x)}\bigr),
$$

where $P\in\mathbb R[z_1,\ldots,z_n]$.

This description includes constants, addition, subtraction, multiplication, and real scalar multiplication. Products of exponentials produce expressions such as

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n e^{k_i\iota_i(x)}
=e^{\sum_{i=1}^n k_i\iota_i(x)}
$$

for nonnegative integers $k_i$. The larger language generated by exponentials, logarithms where defined, and field operations contains $A_\iota$, so density of $A_\iota$ implies density of any such larger continuous function class.

Care is required with division and logarithms: arbitrary expressions may have poles or leave the positive domain. The density result needs neither operation and therefore avoids these domain issues.

### 2.4 Point separation

A family $A\subseteq C(X)$ **separates points** if, whenever $x,y\in X$ and $x\ne y$, there is $g\in A$ such that $g(x)\ne g(y)$.

Point separation is the key hypothesis in the real Stone–Weierstrass theorem.

**Stone–Weierstrass Theorem.** Let $X$ be compact. If a real subalgebra $A\subseteq C(X)$ contains all constant functions and separates points, then $A$ is uniformly dense in $C(X)$.

This theorem converts a local distinguishability condition into a global approximation statement.

## 3. Separation by exponential coordinates

We first establish the elementary geometric lemma driving all subsequent results.

**Lemma 1 (Exponential coordinates separate points).** Let $X$ be a topological space and let $\iota:X\to\mathbb R^n$ be injective. For every distinct $x,y\in X$, there is an index $i$ such that

$$
e^{\iota_i(x)}\ne e^{\iota_i(y)}.
$$

Consequently, the algebra $A_\iota$ separates points.

**Proof sketch.** Since $\iota$ is injective, $x\ne y$ implies $\iota(x)\ne\iota(y)$. Two unequal vectors differ in at least one coordinate, so $\iota_i(x)\ne\iota_i(y)$ for some $i$. The real exponential is strictly increasing and hence injective, giving $e^{\iota_i(x)}\ne e^{\iota_i(y)}$. Since each exponential coordinate belongs to $A_\iota$, the algebra separates the pair. $\square$

The use of the exponential is structurally simple: any continuous injective scalar transformation could preserve coordinate separation. The exponential is important because it belongs to the intended elementary function language and because its algebraic combinations have useful analytic structure.

## 4. Qualitative universal approximation

**Theorem 2 (Exponential-coordinate universal approximation).** Let $X$ be a compact topological space admitting a continuous injective map $\iota:X\to\mathbb R^n$. Then the exponential-coordinate algebra $A_\iota$ is uniformly dense in $C(X)$. Equivalently, for every $f\in C(X)$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a polynomial $P$ in $n$ variables such that

$$
\sup_{x\in X}
\left|f(x)-P\bigl(e^{\iota_1(x)},\ldots,e^{\iota_n(x)}\bigr)\right|
<\varepsilon.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The algebra $A_\iota$ contains constants by definition. Lemma 1 shows that it separates points. The real Stone–Weierstrass theorem therefore implies that its uniform closure is $C(X)$. Unpacking membership in $A_\iota$ gives the stated polynomial form. $\square$

Several aspects of the theorem deserve emphasis. First, compactness is essential to the stated uniform framework. Second, the theorem gives existence for each target and tolerance, not a common finite expression that handles all targets. Third, no complexity estimate follows directly from Stone–Weierstrass. Finally, the construction is exp-only at the transcendental level; logarithms are not required.

### 4.1 Specialization to the cube

For the unit cube

$$
Q_n=[0,1]^n,
$$

use the canonical embedding $\iota(x)=x$. It is continuous and injective.

**Corollary 3 (Density on the unit cube).** For every $n\ge1$, the algebra generated by

$$
x\longmapsto e^{x_1},\ldots,x\longmapsto e^{x_n}
$$

is uniformly dense in $C([0,1]^n)$. Thus, for every continuous $f:[0,1]^n\to\mathbb R$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $P\in\mathbb R[z_1,\ldots,z_n]$ with

$$
\max_{x\in[0,1]^n}
\left|f(x)-P(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 2 to the canonical inclusion of the cube into $\mathbb R^n$. $\square$

### 4.2 A transformed-coordinate interpretation

Define $T:[0,1]^n\to[1,e]^n$ by

$$
T(x_1,\ldots,x_n)=(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n}).
$$

This is a homeomorphism with inverse

$$
T^{-1}(y_1,\ldots,y_n)=(\log y_1,\ldots,\log y_n).
$$

For $f\in C([0,1]^n)$, define $F=f\circ T^{-1}$ on $[1,e]^n$. Then approximating $f(x)$ by $P(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})$ is equivalent to approximating $F(y)$ by an ordinary polynomial $P(y)$. This gives a constructive conceptual route via multivariate polynomial approximation on $[1,e]^n$, although the theorem itself does not select a particular polynomial algorithm or rate.

The transformed view also clarifies why exp-only expressions are sufficient. Exponentiation is a change of coordinates from the original cube to another compact box; polynomials in the transformed variables then form a dense algebra.

## 5. Continuous linear observables

A **continuous linear observable** is a continuous linear map

$$
L:C(X)\longrightarrow\mathbb R.
$$

Continuity means there exists a constant $M\ge0$ such that

$$
|L(f)|\le M\|f\|_\infty
$$

for every $f\in C(X)$. Evaluation at a point, weighted finite averages, and integration against finite signed regular Borel measures are standard examples.

**Theorem 4 (Determination of continuous linear observables).** Let $X$ be compact, let $\iota:X\to\mathbb R^n$ be continuous and injective, and let $L_1,L_2:C(X)\to\mathbb R$ be continuous linear observables. If

$$
L_1(g)=L_2(g)
$$

for every $g\in A_\iota$, then $L_1=L_2$ on all of $C(X)$.

**Proof sketch.** Fix $f\in C(X)$. By Theorem 2, there is a sequence $(g_k)$ in $A_\iota$ such that $\|g_k-f\|_\infty\to0$. Continuity of $L_1$ and $L_2$ gives

$$
L_1(g_k)\to L_1(f),\qquad L_2(g_k)\to L_2(f).
$$

The hypothesis gives $L_1(g_k)=L_2(g_k)$ for every $k$. Taking limits yields $L_1(f)=L_2(f)$. Since $f$ was arbitrary, the observables coincide. $\square$

**Corollary 5 (Observable determination on the cube).** Two continuous linear observables on $C([0,1]^n)$ are equal if they agree on every polynomial in $e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n}$.

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 4 to the canonical cube embedding. $\square$

This result is a bridge between approximation and uniqueness. A dense family is not only capable of reproducing continuous targets; it is also a complete testing family for continuous linear measurements.

### 5.1 Measure-theoretic interpretation

Suppose $\mu$ and $\nu$ are finite regular Borel measures on a compact space $X$, and define

$$
L_\mu(f)=\int_X f\,d\mu,
\qquad
L_\nu(f)=\int_X f\,d\nu.
$$

These are continuous linear observables. Therefore, if their integrals agree for all $g\in A_\iota$, they agree for every continuous $f$. Under the standard uniqueness principle for regular Borel measures, this implies $\mu=\nu$. On the cube, it is enough to compare quantities of the form

$$
\int_{[0,1]^n}P(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})\,d\mu(x).
$$

This is an exponential-coordinate analogue of moment determination. The approximation theorem supplies the density step; measure equality additionally invokes the usual representation and uniqueness theory for measures.

## 6. An explicit shallow approximation to the square

We now turn from qualitative density to a quantitative example. For $h>0$, define

$$
S_h(x)=\frac{2}{h^2}\left(e^{hx}-1-hx\right).
$$

The expression contains one occurrence of the exponential and no nested composition. It is therefore shallow in the direct syntactic sense, although no general depth formalism is imposed here.

### 6.1 Exponential remainder bound

The key analytic estimate is the following.

**Lemma 6 (Quadratic exponential remainder).** For every $u\in[0,1]$,

$$
\left|e^u-\left(1+u+\frac{u^2}{2}\right)\right|
\le \frac{2}{9}u^3.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Expand $e^u$ through second order and control the tail uniformly for $|u|\le1$. A standard exponential-series remainder estimate at order two bounds the tail by a constant multiple of $u^3$. On the nonnegative interval, the absolute values simplify, and the resulting finite estimate yields the constant $2/9$. $\square$

The constant is chosen for a simple certified bound; it is not asserted to be the optimal remainder constant.

### 6.2 Uniform error theorem

**Theorem 7 (One-exponential square approximation).** If $0<h\le1$, then for every $x\in[0,1]$,

$$
|S_h(x)-x^2|\le\frac{4}{9}h.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\|S_h-x^2\|_\infty\le\frac{4}{9}h.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Algebraically,

$$
S_h(x)-x^2
=
\frac{2}{h^2}
\left(e^{hx}-1-hx-\frac{(hx)^2}{2}\right).
$$

Because $0<h\le1$ and $0\le x\le1$, the product $u=hx$ belongs to $[0,1]$. Applying Lemma 6 gives

$$
|S_h(x)-x^2|
\le
\frac{2}{h^2}\cdot\frac{2}{9}(hx)^3
=
\frac{4}{9}h x^3.
$$

Since $x^3\le1$ on $[0,1]$, the result follows. $\square$

**Corollary 8 (Explicit reciprocal rate).** For every integer $N\ge1$ and every $x\in[0,1]$,

$$
\left|
2N^2\left(e^{x/N}-1-\frac{x}{N}\right)-x^2
\right|
\le\frac{4}{9N}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Substitute $h=1/N$ into Theorem 7. $\square$

The rate is an upper guarantee. It establishes that a one-exponential expression reaches any tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ for the square target by choosing an integer

$$
N>\frac{4}{9\varepsilon}.
$$

This target-specific result does not imply a corresponding shallow rate for every continuous function.

## 7. Algorithms and numerical evaluation

### 7.1 Stable evaluation of the shallow approximant

Direct evaluation of $e^{hx}-1-hx$ is vulnerable to cancellation when $hx$ is small. Computing $e^{hx}$ and subtracting $1$ loses relative precision because the two numbers are nearly equal. The standard function $\operatorname{expm1}(z)$ evaluates $e^z-1$ accurately near zero.

A stable algorithm is therefore

$$
S_h(x)=\frac{2}{h^2}\bigl(\operatorname{expm1}(hx)-hx\bigr).
$$

For a grid $x_j=j/m$, one may compute the empirical maximum error

$$
E_m(h)=\max_{0\le j\le m}|S_h(x_j)-x_j^2|
$$

and compare it to $4h/9$. The theorem controls the continuum, whereas the grid is only an illustration; no finite grid by itself proves the uniform statement.

The grid algorithm uses $O(m)$ time and $O(1)$ auxiliary storage if values are streamed. Floating-point underflow, overflow, and roundoff remain implementation considerations, especially for extremely small $h$ where division by $h^2$ amplifies residual numerical errors.

### 7.2 Constructive transformed-coordinate pipeline

The density theorem suggests a general approximation pipeline:

1. map $x\in[0,1]^n$ to $y=(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})\in[1,e]^n$;
2. form $F(y)=f(\log y_1,\ldots,\log y_n)$;
3. approximate $F$ on $[1,e]^n$ by a multivariate polynomial $P$;
4. return $g(x)=P(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})$.

If a chosen polynomial scheme guarantees $\|F-P\|_\infty<\varepsilon$, then the transformed approximant satisfies $\|f-g\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ because $T$ is onto and

$$
|f(x)-g(x)|=|F(T(x))-P(T(x))|.
$$

Complexity depends on the polynomial approximation method, degree, dimension, and regularity of $F$. A dense tensor polynomial of coordinate degree at most $d$ has $(d+1)^n$ coefficients, exposing the usual curse of dimensionality. The qualitative theorem alone supplies neither $d$ nor an efficient coefficient-selection rule.

## 8. Applications

### 8.1 Elementary-function models

The theorem establishes expressive completeness, in the uniform approximation sense, for models assembled from coordinate exponentials and algebra operations on compact domains. Any larger grammar containing this fragment inherits qualitative density, provided candidate expressions are continuous on the domain.

### 8.2 Stable sensing and inverse problems

Suppose an unknown continuous field is interrogated through linear sensors. If all continuous linear sensors are under consideration, their action on the dense exponential-coordinate algebra determines their action everywhere. Conversely, two candidate sensors agreeing on all exponential-field probes cannot differ on any continuous signal. This gives a mathematical completeness criterion for a probe family.

### 8.3 Probability and generalized moments

For a random vector $X$ supported on $[0,1]^n$, expectations of polynomials in $e^{X_1},\ldots,e^{X_n}$ form generalized moments. Agreement of these expectations for two laws implies agreement on all continuous test functions, and hence, under standard compact-space measure uniqueness, equality of the laws. Compact support is important because it provides the relevant uniform density and avoids integrability issues.

### 8.4 Approximation of smooth primitives

The square construction illustrates a broader Taylor-extraction idea. After subtracting lower-order terms from an exponential and rescaling, a desired monomial appears as the leading term. Extending this systematically could realize higher monomials and polynomials, after which classical approximation operators could transfer smoothness-dependent rates to the exponential-field language. Such extensions require separate remainder estimates and are not consequences of the square theorem alone.

## 9. Discussion and limitations

The principal density theorem is strong in scope but qualitative in complexity. It guarantees an approximant for every continuous target and every positive tolerance, yet it does not bound:

- polynomial degree in the transformed variables;
- number of monomials or arithmetic operations;
- compositional depth or width;
- coefficient magnitude;
- numerical conditioning; or
- dependence on dimension and target regularity.

Accordingly, the density theorem should not be read as an efficiency theorem. Universal expressiveness can coexist with severe resource growth. In high dimension, straightforward polynomial constructions may require exponentially many terms.

The explicit square result addresses complexity only for one target. It uses one exponential and achieves a uniform $O(1/N)$ certified rate under the parameterization $h=1/N$. The theorem does not claim this rate is optimal. Taylor’s expansion suggests that the actual leading error behaves like a constant times $h x^3$, and sharper constants may be possible. Nor does the construction prove that a fixed shallow grammar efficiently approximates arbitrary continuous targets.

The observable-determination theorem requires continuity. A discontinuous linear functional need not be determined by agreement on a dense subspace, because passage to uniform limits may fail. Likewise, compactness and finite-dimensional point-separating coordinates are central to the stated framework.

Finally, while logarithms provide the inverse coordinate transformation and are natural in a full exponential–logarithmic language, they play no role in the generating algebra needed for density. This identifies an exp-only sufficient fragment, not a proof that it is minimal under every possible grammar or domain convention.

## 10. Future work

A constructive multivariate theory should replace pure existence with explicit operators whose errors depend on a modulus of continuity and whose expression resources are controlled in terms of dimension and tolerance. The transformed-coordinate interpretation provides a starting point, but careful bounds must account for how logarithmic coordinate inversion changes regularity.

A second priority is a rigorous depth hierarchy. One may define a typed syntax carrying depth and width, prove semantic closure under composition, and investigate upper and lower approximation bounds at each fixed depth. Lower bounds may require oscillation counts, zero counts, or metric entropy.

The shallow Taylor construction should be extended from $x^2$ to higher monomials and multivariate polynomials with uniform constants. Combining those realizations with polynomial approximation estimates could yield rates for $C^k$, Hölder, and Sobolev classes.

The dual theorem invites a fully developed measure-theoretic corollary. Integration against finite regular Borel measures turns exponential-coordinate expressions into a determining class. Quantitative stability questions—how approximate agreement of generalized moments controls a metric between measures—would be especially useful for applications.

It is also natural to compare minimal fragments: coordinate exponentials alone, logarithms on positive domains, rational closure with controlled denominators, and full nested exp–log expressions. Density is only the first distinction; efficiency and conditioning may separate grammars that are qualitatively equally expressive.

## 11. Conclusion

A compact space with finitely many continuous injective coordinates admits a simple universal function language. Exponentiating those coordinates preserves point separation, and the algebra they generate contains constants. Stone–Weierstrass therefore makes the algebra uniformly dense in every continuous real-valued function on the space. On $[0,1]^n$, every continuous target can consequently be approximated by a polynomial in $e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n}$.

Density also yields a uniqueness principle: continuous linear observables are completely determined by their values on the exponential-coordinate algebra. Thus the same family that approximates all continuous functions also tests all stable linear measurements.

For the square function, the qualitative theory is complemented by a direct shallow formula. The approximant $S_h(x)=2(e^{hx}-1-hx)/h^2$ has uniform error at most $4h/9$ on $[0,1]$, giving a reciprocal $4/(9N)$ bound at $h=1/N$. Together, these results identify both a broad universality mechanism and a concrete quantitative foothold for a deeper theory of resource-sensitive exponential–field approximation.

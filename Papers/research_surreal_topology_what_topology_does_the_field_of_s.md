## Abstract

The topology generated by open intervals on a linearly ordered set is its order topology; it is not a distinct topology. For a universe-relative surreal number field, this topology is governed by a strong non-Archimedean phenomenon. Below every positive surreal scale $d$ there exists $\varepsilon>0$ such that $n\varepsilon<d$ for every natural number $n$. We use this infinitesimal to construct, between any two distinct surreal numbers, an additive finite-step ray that is both open and closed. It follows that the order-topological surreal line is totally separated and hence totally disconnected and nonconnected. Every continuous map from a connected space into the surreal line is constant; in particular, every path is constant, distinct points lie in distinct path components, and the space is not contractible. Nevertheless, its fundamental group at every basepoint is trivial because all loops are constant. We distinguish this mechanism from simple connectedness, contractibility, and local compactness, and explain the foundational scope of the result for universe-relative rather than proper-class surreal numbers.

## 1. Introduction

The surreal numbers form a totally ordered field containing the real numbers, ordinal scales, infinitely large elements, infinitesimals, and iterated hierarchies of both. Their order is dense: whenever $x<y$, there are surreal numbers strictly between them. Such richness makes a connected “surreal continuum” seem plausible. One might therefore conjecture that the topology generated by intervals is connected, locally connected, locally compact, or even contractible.

The first point requiring clarification is terminological. On a linear order, the topology generated by open bounded intervals together with the appropriate open rays is precisely the order topology. There is no second “interval topology” that can be chosen to evade known features of the order topology. The real question is therefore whether the surreal field, with its order topology, is connected.

The answer is negative in a strong sense. The decisive difference between the real and surreal lines is not density but the Archimedean property. On the real line, for positive $d$ and $\varepsilon$, sufficiently many $\varepsilon$-steps exceed $d$. In the surreal field, given $d>0$, one can select $\varepsilon>0$ so small that no natural multiple $n\varepsilon$ reaches $d$. The points reachable from a fixed origin by lying below some finite $\varepsilon$-threshold form a nontrivial clopen lower set. Choosing $d$ to be the distance between two points produces a clopen separator for that pair.

This paper develops the argument from its order-theoretic input to its algebraic-topological consequences. Section 2 fixes the setting and definitions. Section 3 establishes the infinitesimal scale principle. Section 4 constructs finite-step rays and proves that they are clopen. Section 5 derives total separation and total disconnectedness. Section 6 proves rigidity of maps from connected spaces, including paths and homotopies. Section 7 computes the fundamental group. Sections 8 and 9 discuss algorithms, illustrative finite models, local compactness, proper-class issues, and future directions.

## 2. Ordered-topological setting

### 2.1 Universe-relative surreal fields

Conway’s full collection $\mathbf{No}$ is a proper class. Standard topology, however, is formulated for sets. Accordingly, let $\mathbf{No}_u$ denote a universe-relative surreal field at a fixed set-theoretic level $u$. It is a set within a larger ambient universe and is a totally ordered field. The subscript will be omitted when no ambiguity arises.

The universe qualification matters. A boundedness statement for a family of surreals may depend on the family being indexed by a type small relative to $u$. The natural numbers form such a small index type, which is exactly what the argument below requires. No conclusion in this paper silently equips the proper class of all surreals with an ordinary set-based topology.

### 2.2 The order topology

Let $L$ be a linear order. Its **order topology** is generated by the open rays

$$
(-\infty,b)=\{x\in L:x<b\},\qquad (a,\infty)=\{x\in L:a<x\}.
$$

Finite intersections of these rays give bounded intervals

$$
(a,b)=\{x\in L:a<x<b\}.
$$

Conversely, bounded intervals together with rays at the ends generate the same topology. Thus the topology described as “generated by intervals with surreal endpoints” is the order topology on $\mathbf{No}$.

A subset $U$ of a topological space is **clopen** if it is both open and closed. A space $X$ is **totally separated** if, for every $x\ne y$, there exists a clopen subset $U$ with $x\in U$ and $y\notin U$. A space is **totally disconnected** if every connected component is a singleton. Total separation implies total disconnectedness.

A **path** from $x$ to $y$ is a continuous map $p:[0,1]\to X$ satisfying $p(0)=x$ and $p(1)=y$. A space is **contractible** if there are a point $x_0\in X$ and a continuous map $H:X\times[0,1]\to X$ with $H(x,0)=x$ and $H(x,1)=x_0$ for all $x$.

## 3. Infinitesimal scales

The separation argument begins with a specifically surreal form of non-Archimedean behavior.

### Theorem 3.1 (Infinitesimal scale theorem)

For every positive surreal number $d>0$, there exists a positive surreal number $\varepsilon>0$ such that

$$
(n: \mathbf{No})\varepsilon<d
$$

for every $n\in\mathbb N$.

### Proof sketch

Construct a positive surreal $\delta$ lying below every positive dyadic scale:

$$
0<\delta<2^{-k}\qquad(k\in\mathbb N).
$$

Conceptually, $\delta$ is represented by a Conway cut whose left options include $0$ and whose right options include the sequence $1,1/2,1/4,\ldots$. The simplicity construction places a surreal strictly between the left and all right options.

Set $\varepsilon=d\delta$. Positivity of the ordered field gives $\varepsilon>0$, and for every $k$,

$$
\varepsilon<d2^{-k}.
$$

Given $n\in\mathbb N$, choose $k$ with $n<2^k$. Multiplication by the positive quantity $\varepsilon$ yields

$$
n\varepsilon<2^k\varepsilon<2^kd2^{-k}=d.
$$

This proves the claim. $\square$

### Remark 3.2

The theorem fails in every Archimedean ordered field. Indeed, the Archimedean property says that for positive $d$ and $\varepsilon$, some natural multiple of $\varepsilon$ exceeds $d$. The theorem therefore isolates the exact algebraic feature used in the topological argument.

### Proposition 3.3 (Surreal domination of natural scales)

There exists a surreal number $M$ such that

$$
n<M
$$

for every $n\in\mathbb N$.

### Proof sketch

A universe-relative boundedness property for small families of surreals supplies an upper bound for the natural-number image. If $B$ is such an upper bound, then a surreal strictly above $B$ also lies strictly above every natural number. This proposition is another manifestation of non-Archimedean size, though Theorem 3.1 supplies the form needed below. $\square$

## 4. Finite-step rays

Fix $a\in\mathbf{No}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Define the **finite-step ray**

$$
R(a,\varepsilon)
=
\{z\in\mathbf{No}:\exists n\in\mathbb N,\ z<a+n\varepsilon\}.
$$

This is the lower region eventually dominated by one of the finite thresholds $a+n\varepsilon$. Importantly, the word “eventually” ranges only over natural numbers. It does not include transfinite or infinitely large multiples.

### Lemma 4.1 (Openness of finite-step rays)

For every $a$ and $\varepsilon$, the set $R(a,\varepsilon)$ is open in the order topology.

### Proof

By definition,

$$
R(a,\varepsilon)
=
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}(-\infty,a+n\varepsilon).
$$

Each lower ray in the union is open in the order topology, and arbitrary unions of open sets are open. $\square$

### Lemma 4.2 (Closedness of positive finite-step rays)

If $\varepsilon>0$, then $R(a,\varepsilon)$ is closed.

### Proof sketch

It suffices to show that the complement is open. Let $z\notin R(a,\varepsilon)$. Then

$$
z\ge a+n\varepsilon
$$

for every natural number $n$. Consider the open lower neighborhood segment immediately below $z$, namely points $w$ satisfying $z-\varepsilon<w$; this can be intersected with any convenient upper ray to obtain a bounded neighborhood if desired.

Suppose such a $w$ belonged to $R(a,\varepsilon)$. Then $w<a+n\varepsilon$ for some $n$. Adding $\varepsilon$ gives

$$
z<w+\varepsilon<a+(n+1)\varepsilon,
$$

contradicting $z\notin R(a,\varepsilon)$. Hence a neighborhood of $z$ misses the finite-step ray. Every point of the complement is interior, so the complement is open and the ray is closed. $\square$

### Corollary 4.3

For every $a$ and every positive $\varepsilon$, the finite-step ray $R(a,\varepsilon)$ is clopen.

The closedness has no analogue for positive real step sizes on the real line. There, the thresholds $a+n\varepsilon$ are unbounded above, so their lower rays cover all of $\mathbb R$. In the surreal field, a natural sequence of thresholds may remain bounded below a point at a larger scale.

## 5. Pairwise separation and disconnectedness

### Theorem 5.1 (Clopen separation theorem)

For any distinct surreal numbers $x$ and $y$, there exists a clopen set $U$ such that $x\in U$ and $y\notin U$.

### Proof

Assume first that $x<y$ and put $d=y-x>0$. By Theorem 3.1, choose $\varepsilon>0$ with

$$
n\varepsilon<y-x
$$

for every $n\in\mathbb N$. Let $U=R(x,\varepsilon)$. Corollary 4.3 shows that $U$ is clopen. Since $\varepsilon>0$,

$$
x<x+\varepsilon,
$$

so $x\in U$. If $y\in U$, then for some $n$,

$$
y<x+n\varepsilon,
$$

and therefore $y-x<n\varepsilon$, contradicting the choice of $\varepsilon$. Thus $y\notin U$.

If $y<x$, apply the preceding argument to $y<x$ to obtain a clopen set containing $y$ and excluding $x$, then take its complement. $\square$

### Theorem 5.2 (Total separation)

The order-topological surreal line is totally separated.

### Proof

This is precisely the pairwise property established by Theorem 5.1. $\square$

### Theorem 5.3 (Total disconnectedness)

Every connected subset of the order-topological surreal line contains at most one point. Consequently, all connected components are singletons.

### Proof sketch

Suppose a connected subset $C$ contained distinct points $x$ and $y$. Choose a clopen separator $U$ with $x\in U$ and $y\notin U$. Then

$$
C=(C\cap U)\cup(C\cap U^c)
$$

is a union of disjoint, nonempty subsets that are both open in the subspace topology. This separates $C$, contradicting connectedness. $\square$

### Corollary 5.4 (Nonconnectedness)

The order-topological surreal line is not connected.

### Proof

The field contains distinct points, for example $0$ and $1$. By Theorem 5.3, no connected subset can contain both. $\square$

### Discussion: density is insufficient

The conclusion does not contradict order density. Density states that for $x<y$ there exists $z$ with $x<z<y$. Connectedness is stronger. The rational line illustrates the distinction: it is densely ordered but disconnected because irrational cuts are absent. The surreal case has a different mechanism. Its non-Archimedean hierarchy creates additive cuts determined by all finite multiples of an infinitesimal. Thus informal assertions that “every cut is filled” must specify the size and kind of cut under discussion. A natural-number-indexed finite-step region can have a boundary at an infinitely larger scale even in an extraordinarily saturated ordered field.

## 6. Rigidity of continuous maps

The preceding theorem has consequences for every connected source space, not only for intervals.

### Theorem 6.1 (Connected-source rigidity)

Let $C$ be a connected topological space and let

$$
f:C\longrightarrow\mathbf{No}
$$

be continuous. Then $f$ is constant.

### Proof

The continuous image $f(C)$ is connected. By Theorem 5.3, every connected subset of $\mathbf{No}$ has at most one point. Hence $f(C)$ is a singleton. $\square$

### Corollary 6.2 (Path rigidity)

Every continuous path in the order-topological surreal line is constant. In particular, a path from $x$ to $y$ exists only if $x=y$.

### Proof

The unit interval $[0,1]$ is connected. Apply Theorem 6.1 to the path map. $\square$

### Corollary 6.3 (Path components)

Every path component of the order-topological surreal line is a singleton. Hence the space is not path connected.

### Theorem 6.4 (Noncontractibility)

The order-topological surreal line is not contractible.

### Proof

Suppose $H:\mathbf{No}\times[0,1]\to\mathbf{No}$ were a contraction to $x_0$. For each $x$, the map

$$
t\longmapsto H(x,t)
$$

would be a path from $x$ to $x_0$. Corollary 6.2 forces this path to be constant, so $x=x_0$ for every $x$. This contradicts $0\ne1$. $\square$

### Remark 6.5 (Failure of algebraic interpolation)

A formula that resembles scalar interpolation does not by itself define a continuous path. If an expression $H(x,t)$ satisfies endpoint identities $H(x,0)=x$ and $H(x,1)=0$ for $x\ne0$, then Theorem 6.4 implies that it cannot be jointly continuous in the order topology. Endpoint algebra and topological continuity are separate requirements.

### Corollary 6.6 (Maps from spheres)

Every continuous map from a connected sphere $S^n$ with $n\ge1$ into the order-topological surreal line is constant.

### Proof

Each $S^n$ for $n\ge1$ is connected, so Theorem 6.1 applies. $\square$

## 7. Fundamental group

Fix a basepoint $x_0\in\mathbf{No}$. A based loop is a continuous map $\ell:[0,1]\to\mathbf{No}$ with $\ell(0)=\ell(1)=x_0$. The fundamental group consists of based homotopy classes of such loops.

### Theorem 7.1 (Trivial fundamental group)

For every basepoint $x_0$,

$$
\pi_1(\mathbf{No},x_0)=0.
$$

### Proof

By Corollary 6.2, every path and hence every loop in $\mathbf{No}$ is constant. Therefore the only based loop is the constant loop at $x_0$, and the fundamental group has one element. $\square$

### Discussion: triviality without contractibility

Contractible spaces have trivial fundamental groups, but the converse is false. The surreal line provides an especially transparent distinction. In a contractible region such as a disk, nonconstant loops exist and can be deformed to a point. In the surreal line, nonconstant loops do not exist. The same group invariant arises from opposite geometric behavior: abundant deformation in one case and complete path rigidity in the other.

No inference from $\pi_1=0$ to path connectedness or contractibility is valid here. Indeed, the path components are singletons and the space contains more than one point.

## 8. Constructive and computational viewpoint

The proof suggests a symbolic separation procedure.

### Algorithm 8.1 (Infinitesimal clopen separator)

**Input:** distinct surreals $x$ and $y$.

**Output:** a symbolic description of a clopen set containing $x$ and excluding $y$.

1. If necessary, exchange $x$ and $y$ so that $x<y$, recording whether a final complement is required.
2. Compute the positive gap $d=y-x$.
3. Choose a positive surreal $\varepsilon$ satisfying $n\varepsilon<d$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.
4. Form
   $$
   R(x,\varepsilon)=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb N}(-\infty,x+n\varepsilon).
   $$
5. Return this ray, or its complement if the original ordering was reversed.

The procedure is mathematical rather than a finite real-arithmetic algorithm because Step 3 invokes surreal cut construction and Step 4 describes a countable union. Once an exact representation of $\varepsilon$ is available, testing a proposed witness threshold is constant-time relative to the underlying ordered-field operations; enumerating the first $N$ thresholds costs $O(N)$ such operations.

### Numerical analogues and their limits

Ordinary floating-point numbers cannot represent a nonzero $\varepsilon$ satisfying $n\varepsilon<d$ for every natural $n$. Numerical demonstrations must therefore use a finite cutoff $N$. Given $d>0$, one may choose

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{d}{N+1}.
$$

Then $n\varepsilon<d$ for $0\le n\le N$, while $(N+1)\varepsilon=d$. This finite experiment visualizes the thresholds and explains exactly where Archimedean arithmetic diverges from surreal arithmetic: every real finite-step barrier is eventually crossed, whereas an appropriately chosen surreal infinitesimal survives all natural indices simultaneously.

A second useful visualization plots the maximum reached threshold $x+N\varepsilon$ against $N$. For a fixed positive real $\varepsilon$, the plot eventually crosses $y$. A symbolic surreal infinitesimal remains below $y$ for every finite $N$. The numerical code accompanying this paper presents finite windows honestly as analogues, not as simulations of the entire surreal field.

## 9. Local compactness and foundational scope

An interval neighborhood basis does not establish local compactness. Every linearly ordered topological space has order-convex basic neighborhoods, but local compactness requires each point to possess a neighborhood with compact closure, or an equivalent compact-neighborhood condition under standard separation assumptions.

The results above neither prove nor assume that nontrivial closed surreal intervals are compact. A promising route toward a negative result would be to use nested or translated finite-step clopen cuts to construct an open cover of a candidate interval that has no finite subcover. Such an argument must be carried out separately: clopenness alone does not imply noncompactness, and total disconnectedness is compatible with compactness, as the Cantor set demonstrates.

The proper-class issue is equally important. The object considered here is a universe-relative surreal field $\mathbf{No}_u$. The crucial constructions use natural-number-indexed families, which are small relative to the chosen universe. A topology on Conway’s full proper class would require a framework specifying what class-indexed unions, open classes, compactness, and continuity mean. Results for every universe-relative level strongly motivate such a theory but should not be conflated with it.

## 10. Applications and broader consequences

The connected-source rigidity theorem can be reused without repeating the clopen construction. Any continuous observable from a connected parameter space into the surreal line must be constant. Thus a continuously time-dependent surreal-valued state, with ordinary connected time, cannot change under the order topology. Likewise, continuous families indexed by intervals, disks, connected manifolds, or connected graphs have singleton images.

This makes the order topology unsuitable for applications that require continuously varying surreal-valued quantities across ordinary real time. An application seeking such variation would need a different topology or a restricted codomain. That alternative topology could not simultaneously be described simply as the topology generated by order intervals, because those intervals already determine the topology studied here.

The results also offer a caution in topological algebra. Ordered-field operations may be compatible with an order topology, yet the resulting topology can exhibit behavior radically different from Euclidean intuition when the field is strongly non-Archimedean. Density, algebraic interpolation formulas, and trivial homotopy invariants must each be interpreted with care.

## 11. Future directions

Several consequences invite further development.

First, connected-source rigidity should imply that all maps from connected spheres $S^n$ are constant, and consequently all positive-dimensional homotopy groups vanish at each basepoint. The point is again not higher-dimensional contractibility but the absence of nonconstant maps from connected domains.

Second, every singular simplex of positive dimension has connected domain and is therefore constant. This suggests vanishing reduced singular homology in positive degrees, while degree zero records the enormous set of singleton path components. A complete treatment should carefully describe chain groups and augmentation at the chosen universe level.

Third, compactness of intervals deserves an independent theorem. The finite-step cuts provide natural candidates for open covers and may show that no nontrivial interval supplies a compact neighborhood. Whether local compactness fails everywhere should be settled by an explicit cover argument rather than by the existence of an interval basis.

Fourth, one may ask which coarser topologies permit meaningful continuous motion while preserving some ordered-field structure. Any connected topology must be strictly coarser than the order topology or alter the intended class of opens. Characterizing such topologies could separate algebraically natural requirements from geometric ones.

Finally, a genuine proper-class topology for the full surreal field would clarify which set-level results persist under class-indexed constructions. Such a framework must state its size conventions openly and distinguish set compactness from possible class-compactness notions.

## 12. Conclusion

The topology generated by surreal open intervals is the ordinary order topology. Its geometry is controlled by non-Archimedean scale separation. Below every positive gap $d$ lies a positive $\varepsilon$ whose natural multiples all remain below $d$. The associated finite-step ray is a clopen set, and it separates the endpoints of the gap. Hence the universe-relative surreal line is totally separated, totally disconnected, and not connected.

All continuous maps from connected spaces into it are constant. In particular, every path and loop is constant; the space is not path connected and not contractible, while its fundamental group is trivial at every basepoint. This combination is not paradoxical. Contractibility trivializes loops by deforming them; total separation trivializes loops by preventing motion. The surreal line thus demonstrates how an order can be maximally rich in arithmetic scales while its order topology remains maximally rigid with respect to connected motion.

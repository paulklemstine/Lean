# The Interval Topology of the Surreal Numbers: Separation, Noncompactness, and Path Rigidity

## Abstract

The surreal numbers form a proper-class-sized totally ordered field containing the real numbers, the ordinals, infinitesimals, and infinitely large elements. It is natural to ask whether the topology generated by open intervals makes this ordered field into a connected and locally compact “universal line.” We show that the opposite behavior occurs. The interval topology is exactly the order topology. Open intervals with surreal endpoints form a neighborhood basis, but the space is totally separated and therefore disconnected. Every continuous path has equal endpoints, so the space is not contractible. Nevertheless, every fundamental group is trivial because every loop is constant. We also establish a local compactness obstruction. For every positive surreal $\varepsilon$, the set of natural multiples $\{n\varepsilon:n\in\mathbb N\}$ is closed and has no greatest element. Every neighborhood contains, after translation, such a closed ladder for a sufficiently small positive infinitesimal $\varepsilon$. Since a nonempty compact subset of a linearly ordered topological space has a greatest element, no surreal number has a compact neighborhood. These results clarify why order density and algebraic interpolation do not imply connectedness or path continuity in the universe-relative surreal line.

## 1. Introduction

Conway’s surreal numbers, denoted here by $\mathbf{No}$, form an ordered field of exceptional scope. They include every real number, every ordinal, positive infinitesimals such as $1/\omega$, infinitely large numbers, and combinations of these at all levels of the recursive hierarchy. The field operations and total order suggest a geometric analogy with the real line.

That analogy motivates a plausible but incorrect conjecture: equip $\mathbf{No}$ with the topology generated by open order intervals and expect a connected, locally connected, locally compact, contractible ordered field. One might reason that there are abundant points between any two surreals and that a linear interpolation should connect them.

There are two difficulties with this intuition. First, the topology generated by intervals is not distinct from the order topology; it is the order topology. Second, density of an order does not imply topological connectedness. The rational line is the elementary warning, and the proper-class scale of the surreal hierarchy creates subtler universe-relative cuts and clopen barriers.

We develop the consequences systematically. The principal results are:

1. the interval topology equals the order topology;
2. intervals $(a,b)$ with $a<x<b$ form a neighborhood basis at $x$;
3. the surreal line is totally separated and hence disconnected;
4. every path has equal endpoints, implying noncontractibility;
5. every fundamental group is trivial;
6. no point has a compact neighborhood, so the space is not locally compact.

The local compactness result is driven by a closed infinitesimal ladder. Given any neighborhood of $x$, choose a positive infinitesimal $\varepsilon$ so small that all natural multiples $n\varepsilon$ fit before the neighborhood’s right endpoint. The set of these multiples is closed but has no greatest element. It therefore cannot lie inside a compact set.

Because the totality of all surreals is a proper class, topology must be interpreted relative to a fixed foundational universe in which the relevant collections and constructions are available. All statements below concern that universe-relative surreal line. This convention avoids treating a proper class as an ordinary small set while retaining the mathematical content of its order topology.

## 2. Ordered-topological preliminaries

### 2.1 The surreal ordered field

We use only a small part of the algebraic structure of $\mathbf{No}$. It is a linearly ordered field: addition and multiplication satisfy the field axioms, the order is total, addition preserves strict inequalities, and multiplication by a positive element preserves strict inequalities. Natural numbers embed into $\mathbf{No}$, so $n\varepsilon$ is defined for every $n\in\mathbb N$ and $\varepsilon\in\mathbf{No}$.

A crucial non-Archimedean feature is the availability of arbitrarily small positive infinitesimals in the following strong form.

**Infinitesimal Selection Principle.** For every positive surreal $d$, there exists a positive surreal $\varepsilon$ such that

$$
n\varepsilon<d
$$

for every natural number $n$.

Thus no finite repetition of the step $\varepsilon$ reaches the prescribed positive distance $d$.

### 2.2 Interval and order topologies

For $a,b\in\mathbf{No}$ with $a<b$, define

$$
(a,b)=\{x\in\mathbf{No}:a<x<b\}.
$$

The **interval topology** is generated by all such open intervals. The **order topology** on a linearly ordered set is generated by open intervals together with open rays as needed at endpoints. The surreal order has no endpoints: for each $x$, both $x-1$ and $x+1$ exist. Consequently, open intervals already form a basis.

**Theorem 2.1 (Identification of the topology).** The topology generated by surreal open intervals is exactly the order topology.

**Proof sketch.** In a linear order without endpoints, the standard basis for the order topology consists of open intervals. Since the proposed generators are precisely these intervals, the generated topologies coincide. $\square$

**Theorem 2.2 (Interval neighborhood basis).** For every $x\in\mathbf{No}$, the family

$$
\mathcal B_x=\{(a,b):a<x<b\}
$$

is a neighborhood basis at $x$. Equivalently, a set $U$ is a neighborhood of $x$ if and only if there exist $a<x<b$ such that $(a,b)\subseteq U$.

**Proof sketch.** This is the local basis characterization of the order topology. Every basis interval containing $x$ has endpoints $a<x<b$, and every neighborhood contains a basis element containing $x$. $\square$

The existence of this familiar basis should not be confused with local compactness. A basis of bounded intervals need not consist of compact sets, nor need its members have compact closures.

### 2.3 Compactness and extrema

We use a standard ordered-topological fact.

**Lemma 2.3 (Compact sets have extrema).** Every nonempty compact subset $C$ of a linearly ordered topological space has a least element and a greatest element.

**Proof sketch.** We prove the assertion about a greatest element; the least-element argument is dual. If $C$ had no greatest element, then the open rays $(-\infty,c)$, indexed by $c\in C$, would cover $C$: for each $x\in C$, choose $c\in C$ with $x<c$. A finite subfamily has a largest index $c_{\max}$, and its union is $(-\infty,c_{\max})$, which fails to contain $c_{\max}$. Hence the cover has no finite subcover, contradicting compactness. $\square$

We also use that a closed subset of a compact space is compact and that a continuous image of a compact set is compact.

## 3. Total separation and disconnectedness

A subset is **clopen** if it is both open and closed. A space $X$ is **totally separated** if, for every two distinct points $x,y\in X$, there is a clopen set containing one and not the other. Total separation implies total disconnectedness: every connected subset is a singleton.

The surreal order topology admits clopen barriers generated by the hierarchy of finite steps and universe-relative cuts. The underlying separation result can be stated independently of a particular construction of the barrier.

**Theorem 3.1 (Total separation).** If $x,y\in\mathbf{No}$ and $x\ne y$, then there exists a clopen subset $U\subseteq\mathbf{No}$ such that $x\in U$ and $y\notin U$.

**Proof sketch.** Assume $x<y$. Choose a sufficiently fine positive surreal step and consider the initial region reachable from $x$ by finitely many such steps. The recursive scale of the surreal order permits the step to be chosen so that this region contains $x$ but not $y$. The finite-step region is closed: a point beyond all of its finite stages has an interval neighborhood still beyond them. It is also open: each point already reached has room for a smaller interval that remains within a later finite stage. Thus it is clopen and separates $x$ from $y$. The case $y<x$ is symmetric. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2 (Disconnectedness).** The interval-topological surreal line is not connected. More strongly, every connected subset of $\mathbf{No}$ is a singleton.

**Proof sketch.** A clopen set separating two points would split any connected subset containing both into two nonempty relatively clopen pieces. Therefore no connected subset contains two distinct points. Since $\mathbf{No}$ contains $0$ and $1$, the whole space is disconnected. $\square$

This refutes the inference from “many points between points” to connectedness. Order density is insufficient; connectedness of an ordered space demands a suitable order completeness condition, and proper-class scale prevents an unqualified transfer of real-line completeness.

## 4. Natural-step ladders

The key tool for local noncompactness is an arithmetic progression with infinitesimal spacing.

**Definition 4.1 (Natural-step set).** For $\varepsilon\in\mathbf{No}$, define

$$
S_\varepsilon=\{n\varepsilon:n\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

When $\varepsilon>0$, this is the increasing sequence

$$
0,\varepsilon,2\varepsilon,3\varepsilon,\ldots.
$$

To analyze its closure, it is useful to introduce the associated finite-step ray.

**Definition 4.2 (Finite-step ray).** For $\varepsilon>0$, define

$$
R_\varepsilon=\{y\in\mathbf{No}:y<N\varepsilon\text{ for some }N\in\mathbb N\}.
$$

This is the region lying below at least one finite multiple of $\varepsilon$. Clearly $S_\varepsilon\subseteq R_\varepsilon$, because $n\varepsilon<(n+1)\varepsilon$.

**Lemma 4.3 (Finite-step containment).** If $\varepsilon>0$, then every point of $S_\varepsilon$ belongs to $R_\varepsilon$.

**Proof.** For $n\in\mathbb N$, positivity gives $n\varepsilon<(n+1)\varepsilon$. Hence $n\varepsilon$ lies below a finite multiple and belongs to $R_\varepsilon$. $\square$

The finite-step ray is closed in the surreal order topology. Intuitively, crossing its upper edge requires more than every finite number of $\varepsilon$-steps, and points outside it possess neighborhoods that remain outside. This clopen-style barrier controls points far from the ladder. Points inside the ray can be handled by a finite deletion.

**Lemma 4.4 (Local avoidance inside the finite-step ray).** Let $\varepsilon>0$ and let $y\in R_\varepsilon\setminus S_\varepsilon$. Then $y$ has an open neighborhood disjoint from $S_\varepsilon$.

**Proof sketch.** Choose $N$ with $y<N\varepsilon$. Below $N\varepsilon$, only the finitely many ladder points

$$
0,\varepsilon,\ldots,(N-1)\varepsilon
$$

can occur. A finite subset of a linearly ordered Hausdorff space is closed. Remove these finitely many points from the open ray $(-\infty,N\varepsilon)$. The result is an open set containing $y$ and avoiding every natural multiple of $\varepsilon$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.5 (Local avoidance outside the finite-step ray).** Let $\varepsilon>0$ and let $y\notin R_\varepsilon$. Then $y$ has an open neighborhood disjoint from $S_\varepsilon$.

**Proof sketch.** The complement of the closed finite-step ray is open and contains $y$. Since $S_\varepsilon\subseteq R_\varepsilon$, this complement is disjoint from the ladder. $\square$

Combining the two cases gives the essential closedness statement.

**Theorem 4.6 (Closed natural-step ladder).** If $\varepsilon>0$, then $S_\varepsilon$ is closed in the surreal order topology.

**Proof sketch.** Every point outside $S_\varepsilon$ either lies in $R_\varepsilon$ or outside it. Lemmas 4.4 and 4.5 provide in either case a neighborhood disjoint from $S_\varepsilon$. Therefore the complement of $S_\varepsilon$ is open. $\square$

**Theorem 4.7 (No greatest rung).** If $\varepsilon>0$, then $S_\varepsilon$ has no greatest element.

**Proof.** If $n\varepsilon$ is any member, then $(n+1)\varepsilon$ is another member and

$$
(n+1)\varepsilon-n\varepsilon=\varepsilon>0.
$$

Thus every rung has a strictly larger successor. $\square$

**Corollary 4.8 (Compact containment obstruction).** Let $K\subseteq\mathbf{No}$ be compact and let $\varepsilon>0$. Then $S_\varepsilon$ is not a subset of $K$.

**Proof.** If $S_\varepsilon\subseteq K$, Theorem 4.6 makes $S_\varepsilon$ a closed subset of the compact set $K$, hence compact. It is nonempty because $0=0\varepsilon$ belongs to it. Lemma 2.3 would then give it a greatest element, contradicting Theorem 4.7. $\square$

## 5. Failure of local compactness

A space is **locally compact at $x$** if $x$ has a compact neighborhood; it is **locally compact** if this holds at every point. In a Hausdorff setting this is equivalent to several familiar formulations, but the compact-neighborhood formulation is the most direct here.

**Theorem 5.1 (No compact neighborhoods).** For every $x\in\mathbf{No}$, there is no compact set $K$ that is a neighborhood of $x$.

**Proof.** Suppose $K$ were a compact neighborhood of $x$. By Theorem 2.2, choose $a<x<b$ such that

$$
(a,b)\subseteq K.
$$

Set $d=b-x>0$. By the Infinitesimal Selection Principle, choose $\varepsilon>0$ satisfying

$$
n\varepsilon<d
$$

for every $n\in\mathbb N$. Since $n\varepsilon\ge0$, we have

$$
a<x\le x+n\varepsilon<x+d=b.
$$

Therefore $x+n\varepsilon\in(a,b)\subseteq K$ for every natural number $n$.

Translate by $-x$. Translation is a homeomorphism in an ordered topological field, so

$$
K-x=\{z-x:z\in K\}
$$

is compact. The preceding containment gives $S_\varepsilon\subseteq K-x$, contradicting Corollary 4.8. Hence no compact neighborhood exists. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (Non-local compactness).** The interval-topological surreal line is not locally compact at any point, and therefore is not a locally compact space.

The proof identifies more than a global defect. Every proposed compact neighborhood fails for the same structural reason: it contains a translated closed ladder of infinitesimal steps with no maximal rung.

### 5.1 Computational finite shadows

No finite numerical calculation can represent the full proper-class surreal line or certify its topological properties. Nevertheless, finite rational models can illustrate the inequalities used in the proof. Given a positive width $d$ and a cutoff $N$, choose

$$
\varepsilon=\frac{d}{N+1}.
$$

Then $n\varepsilon<d$ for $0\le n\le N$. This finite shadow demonstrates how an increasingly fine ladder can fit beneath a fixed boundary. The genuine surreal argument is stronger: one positive infinitesimal works simultaneously for every natural $n$, not merely up to a chosen cutoff.

An algorithmic experiment can generate the first $N+1$ rungs, verify strict increase, translate them into an interval around $x$, and test that no sampled rung is greatest because the next rung always exists. Such experiments are explanatory rather than substitutes for the order-topological argument.

## 6. Path rigidity and homotopy

A **path** from $x$ to $y$ in a topological space $X$ is a continuous map $p:[0,1]\to X$ with $p(0)=x$ and $p(1)=y$. A space is **path-connected** if every two points are joined by a path.

**Theorem 6.1 (Path endpoint rigidity).** If $p:[0,1]\to\mathbf{No}$ is continuous, then $p(0)=p(1)$. Indeed, $p$ is constant.

**Proof.** The real interval $[0,1]$ is connected, and a continuous image of a connected space is connected. Thus $p([0,1])$ is a connected subset of $\mathbf{No}$. By Corollary 3.2, every connected subset is a singleton. Therefore the image of $p$ consists of one point, so $p$ is constant and its endpoints agree. $\square$

**Corollary 6.2.** Distinct surreal numbers lie in distinct path components. Every path component is a singleton.

The usual algebraic interpolation fails topologically.

**Corollary 6.3 (Failure of linear interpolation).** If $x\ne y$, the function

$$
p(t)=(1-t)x+ty
$$

cannot be continuous as a map from the real unit interval into the interval-topological surreal line.

**Proof.** It has endpoints $p(0)=x$ and $p(1)=y$. Theorem 6.1 rules out continuity when these endpoints differ. $\square$

A space $X$ is **contractible** if the identity map is homotopic to a constant map. Equivalently, there are a point $c\in X$ and a continuous map $H:X\times[0,1]\to X$ such that $H(x,0)=x$ and $H(x,1)=c$ for every $x$.

**Theorem 6.4 (Noncontractibility).** The interval-topological surreal line is not contractible.

**Proof.** Suppose $H$ contracted $\mathbf{No}$ to $c$. For each fixed $x$, the map $t\mapsto H(x,t)$ would be a path from $x$ to $c$. By Theorem 6.1, its endpoints must agree, so $x=c$ for every $x$. This contradicts $0\ne1$. $\square$

In particular, expressions resembling $H(x,t)=tx$ cannot define a continuous contraction. Their algebraic validity does not imply continuity.

## 7. Fundamental groups

Fix a basepoint $x\in\mathbf{No}$. A loop based at $x$ is a path $\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathbf{No}$ with $\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=x$. The fundamental group $\pi_1(\mathbf{No},x)$ consists of based homotopy classes of loops, with multiplication induced by concatenation.

**Theorem 7.1 (Trivial fundamental group).** For every surreal basepoint $x$,

$$
\pi_1(\mathbf{No},x)=0.
$$

Equivalently, every loop class is the identity element.

**Proof.** By Theorem 6.1, every path in $\mathbf{No}$ is constant. Hence every loop based at $x$ is the constant loop at $x$, whose homotopy class is the identity. $\square$

Theorem 7.1 and Theorem 6.4 are compatible. Contractibility implies trivial fundamental groups, but the converse fails without additional hypotheses such as path-connectedness and suitable local conditions. Here the fundamental group vanishes for a degenerate reason: there are no nonconstant loops. The space is not contractible because there are also no paths connecting distinct points.

## 8. Consequences and interpretation

The results separate four notions that are easy to conflate.

First, **order density** concerns the existence of intermediate points. It does not ensure connectedness. Second, **algebraic interpolation** provides formulas indexed by scalars, but those formulas need not be continuous for the selected topology. Third, an **interval neighborhood basis** gives a local description of open sets but says nothing by itself about compact closure or local compactness. Fourth, **trivial homotopy invariants** may reflect absence of paths rather than global contractibility.

The infinitesimal ladder is the central geometric object. On the real line, if $\varepsilon>0$ is fixed, the sequence $n\varepsilon$ eventually exceeds every prescribed finite bound. In the surreal field, one can first choose a positive infinitesimal relative to a prescribed surreal width $d$ so that all natural multiples remain below $d$. This reverses the Archimedean mechanism underlying boundedness arguments on $\mathbb R$.

The ladder’s closedness is also essential. An infinite subset without a greatest element can lie in a compact space if it accumulates at a missing endpoint within the subset, as $\{1-1/n:n\ge1\}$ does inside $[0,1]$. The surreal ladder avoids this resolution: it is itself closed, so compact containment would force the ladder to be compact and hence to possess a greatest element.

The proper-class character of $\mathbf{No}$ should remain visible in interpretation. Claims that “every cut is filled” require size qualifications. A surreal can be formed from set-sized left and right option collections satisfying the defining inequalities; this does not identify the total surreal order with an ordinary set-sized Dedekind-complete line. The order topology records that distinction through its clopen separations.

## 9. Algorithms and applications

The theoretical statements suggest three useful computational illustrations.

**Finite ladder construction.** Given rational $d>0$ and a natural cutoff $N$, set $\varepsilon=d/(N+1)$ and output $0,\varepsilon,\ldots,N\varepsilon$. The runtime is $O(N)$ and the output demonstrates strict increase below $d$.

**Translated neighborhood test.** Given rational endpoints $a<x<b$ and cutoff $N$, choose $\varepsilon=(b-x)/(N+1)$ and verify that $x+n\varepsilon\in(a,b)$ for $0\le n\le N$. This costs $O(N)$ exact rational comparisons and mirrors the containment step of Theorem 5.1.

**Finite path-rigidity model.** Equip a finite ordered sample with the discrete topology, the finite analogue of total separation. A discretized map from a connected chain is continuous only when adjacent values agree, hence only when it is constant. Checking adjacent equality costs $O(N)$. This models the connected-image argument behind Theorem 6.1, while not claiming that finite samples reproduce the full surreal topology.

Potential applications are primarily conceptual. Any analytic theory on surreal values must specify its topology rather than infer continuity from formulas. Optimization procedures based on continuous deformation cannot use the interval topology to move between distinct surreal states. Likewise, compactness-based existence arguments require replacement hypotheses, because no point admits a compact neighborhood.

## 10. Future directions

Several concrete problems remain.

1. **Extrema of compact sets.** Determine whether every nonempty compact subset of the universe-relative surreal line has both a least and a greatest element in the strongest available foundational setting.

2. **Noncompactness of intervals.** Prove or refute that every nondegenerate interval—open, closed, or half-open—is noncompact.

3. **Components and quasicomponents.** Total separation strongly suggests that both the connected component and the quasicomponent of every point are singletons; the latter requires the intersection of all clopen neighborhoods to be analyzed explicitly.

4. **Higher homotopy groups.** Since every map from a connected sphere into a totally separated space should be constant, one expects all positive-dimensional homotopy groups to vanish, despite noncontractibility.

5. **Birthday bounds.** Investigate whether every compact family of universe-relative surreals is contained in a fixed birthday stratum. Such a theorem would connect topological compactness with the recursive complexity of surreal construction.

## 11. Conclusion

The topology generated by open surreal intervals is the ordinary order topology, but its behavior is far from that of the real line. It is totally separated, so every connected subset is a singleton. Consequently every path is constant, the space is not contractible, and every fundamental group is trivial. At the same time, every neighborhood contains a translated natural-step ladder for a sufficiently small positive infinitesimal. This ladder is closed and has no greatest element, preventing compact containment and showing that the surreal line is nowhere locally compact.

The surreal field’s algebraic abundance therefore does not produce topological continuity. Its infinitesimals and proper-class hierarchy instead create a landscape of clopen barriers and endless bounded ladders: rich in order, rigid in paths, and locally beyond compact control.

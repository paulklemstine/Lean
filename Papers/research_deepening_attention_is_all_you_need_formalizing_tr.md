## Abstract

We determine the exact approximation-theoretic reach of attention-based sequence models that are constrained by a group of token symmetries, and we quantify precisely what that constraint costs. Working with sequences $x \in \mathbb{R}^{\iota \times \kappa}$ (finite index sets $\iota$ of positions and $\kappa$ of features) and with an arbitrary finite group $\Gamma$ acting on positions through a homomorphism $\mathrm{act} : \Gamma \to \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$, we prove a two-sided dichotomy for the *attention algebra* $\mathcal{A}$, the unital $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra of $C(\mathbb{R}^{\iota\times\kappa}, \mathbb{R})$ generated by linear attention readouts (equivalently, by softmax attention heads with uniform scores).

**Positive half.** On every compact $\Gamma$-saturated domain $K$, every $\Gamma$-invariant continuous functional is a uniform limit of $\Gamma$-invariant elements of $\mathcal{A}$. The proof combines Stone–Weierstrass with group averaging, which is legitimate because $\mathcal{A}$ is stable under the token action. Specializations recover the fully permutation-symmetric case (transformers with no positional encoding) and the cyclic case (relative positional encoding).

**Negative half.** The $\Gamma$-invariant class is uniformly closed, and on each orbit the optimal invariant error equals *exactly* half the orbit oscillation of the target, attained by the constant orbit-midpoint model. As a corollary, if a symmetry moves position $i_0$, no invariant model reads coordinate $i_0$ with uniform error below $1/2$.

**The repair.** With the one-hot positional encoding $\iota_{\mathrm{enc}}$ into feature space $\kappa \sqcup \iota$, *fully permutation-invariant* models regain complete universality — for scalar functionals and for sequence-to-sequence maps — via an explicit permutation-invariant continuous decoder that inverts the encoding. Positional encoding therefore works not by breaking the model's symmetry but by relocating order information into the data.

**Exact converse.** Through a marked-token encoding, continuous $\Gamma$-equivariant sequence-to-sequence maps are *precisely* the marked evaluations of continuous $\Gamma$-invariant functionals; consequently symmetric attention uniformly approximates every continuous equivariant map, and the constructed head family is exactly (not approximately) equivariant.

**Keywords:** attention mechanism, transformer, universal approximation, permutation invariance, equivariance, Stone–Weierstrass theorem, group averaging, positional encoding.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The question

Attention-based sequence models are, in their raw form, indifferent to order. A transformer layer without positional information treats its input as a multiset of tokens: permuting the input permutes the output and nothing else changes. Practitioners repair this by adding positional encodings, and the repair is universally regarded as necessary. But "necessary" is a qualitative statement. Three quantitative questions have remained open:

1. **Exactly which** functionals of a sequence can a symmetry-constrained attention model approximate?
2. **How badly** does it fail on the rest — is there a formula for the optimal error, or only a nonzero lower bound?
3. **How completely** does positional encoding repair the deficit, and by what mechanism?

We answer all three, and at the natural level of generality: not just full permutation symmetry, but an arbitrary finite group of token symmetries. The extra generality is not decorative. Relative positional encodings impose *cyclic* rather than full symmetry, hierarchical or block-structured attention imposes wreath-product symmetry, and set-of-sets architectures impose products of symmetric groups. All are instances of one theorem.

### 1.2 The shape of the answer

For a finite group $\Gamma$ acting on positions, let $\mathcal{A}^{\Gamma}$ denote the $\Gamma$-invariant part of the attention algebra. The main results say:

$$\overline{\mathcal{A}^{\Gamma}}\big|_K \;=\; \left\{ g \in C(K,\mathbb{R}) \;:\; g \text{ is } \Gamma\text{-invariant} \right\}$$

for every compact $\Gamma$-saturated $K$, with closure taken in the uniform norm. The inclusion $\subseteq$ is the negative half (a uniform closedness statement), the inclusion $\supseteq$ is the positive half (a density statement). Off the invariant class, the distance from a target $g$ to $\mathcal{A}^{\Gamma}$, measured on a single orbit, is *exactly* half the orbit oscillation.

Three further theorems complete the picture: a representation theorem identifying equivariant maps with marked invariant functionals; an equivariant universality theorem; and the positional-restoration theorems.

### 1.3 Relation to classical universality

The classical universal-approximation results for neural networks — and their transformer analogues — establish density of an unconstrained model class in $C(K,\mathbb{R})$. They are one-sided: they say what is achievable and are silent on what is not. The content of the present work is that when a symmetry constraint is imposed, the two-sided answer is available in closed form, and the barrier is computable rather than merely nonzero. The technique for the positive half — averaging a Stone–Weierstrass approximant over the group — is elementary; its correctness hinges on a stability lemma for the attention algebra, which is where the specific structure of attention enters.

## 2. Setup and definitions

Throughout, $\iota$ and $\kappa$ are finite nonempty index sets; write $n = |\iota|$ (sequence length) and $d = |\kappa|$ (feature dimension).

**Definition 2.1 (Sequences).** A *sequence* is an element of
$$\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa) \;=\; \iota \to \kappa \to \mathbb{R} \;\cong\; \mathbb{R}^{n \times d},$$
i.e. an $n \times d$ real table $x$, with $x_{i,a}$ the $a$-th feature of the $i$-th token. We endow $\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ with the product (equivalently, Euclidean) topology.

**Definition 2.2 (Token action).** For a permutation $\sigma \in \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ define
$$(\sigma \cdot x)_i \;=\; x_{\sigma(i)}, \qquad x \in \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa).$$
This is a right action in the sense that $\sigma \cdot (\tau \cdot x) = (\tau\sigma)\cdot x$, it fixes the identity permutation, and each $\sigma \cdot (-)$ is continuous (indeed it is a coordinate permutation, hence a linear isometry).

**Definition 2.3 (Symmetry group).** A *token-symmetry group* is a pair $(\Gamma, \mathrm{act})$ with $\Gamma$ a finite group and $\mathrm{act} : \Gamma \to \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ a group homomorphism. We abbreviate $g \cdot x := \mathrm{act}(g)\cdot x$.

Two running specializations:

- $\Gamma = \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ with $\mathrm{act} = \mathrm{id}$: **full permutation symmetry**, the symmetry of an attention stack with no positional information.
- $\Gamma = \langle r \rangle$ cyclic, generated by a rotation $r$ of positions: **shift symmetry**, the symmetry of relative positional encoding.

**Definition 2.4 (Invariance, saturation).** A functional $f : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa) \to \mathbb{R}$ is *$\Gamma$-invariant* if $f(g\cdot x) = f(x)$ for all $g \in \Gamma$ and all $x$. A set $K \subseteq \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ is *$\Gamma$-saturated* if $x \in K \Rightarrow g\cdot x \in K$ for all $g \in \Gamma$.

**Definition 2.5 (Readouts).** For a weight table $w \in \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$, the *readout* $R_w$ is the continuous functional
$$R_w(x) \;=\; \sum_{i \in \iota} \sum_{a \in \kappa} w_{i,a}\, x_{i,a}.$$

**Definition 2.6 (Softmax attention head).** For scores $s : \iota \to \mathbb{R}$ and values weighted by $w$, the *softmax head* is
$$H_{s,w}(x) \;=\; \sum_{j \in \iota} \frac{e^{s_j}}{\sum_{k \in \iota} e^{s_k}} \left( \sum_{a\in\kappa} w_{j,a}\, x_{j,a} \right).$$

**Proposition 2.7 (Readouts are attention heads).** For all $w, x$,
$$H_{0,w}(x) \;=\; R_{w/n}(x), \qquad \text{where } (w/n)_{j,a} = w_{j,a}/n.$$

*Proof.* With $s \equiv 0$ every exponential equals $1$, so the softmax weight is $1/\sum_k 1 = 1/n$ for each $j$. Distributing the constant $1/n$ through the inner sum gives $\sum_j\sum_a (w_{j,a}/n) x_{j,a}$. $\square$

Proposition 2.7 is what licenses the following definition as a model of attention rather than of arbitrary linear algebra: the generators are literally attention heads, in the uniform-score regime.

**Definition 2.8 (Attention algebra).** The *attention algebra* is
$$\mathcal{A} \;=\; \mathcal{A}(\iota,\kappa) \;=\; \mathbb{R}\text{-algebra generated by } \{\, R_w \;:\; w \in \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa) \,\} \;\subseteq\; C(\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa), \mathbb{R}).$$
Equivalently, $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of real polynomials in finitely many readouts. It contains the constants, is closed under sums and products, and is the smallest such collection containing all readouts.

Two remarks justify $\mathcal{A}$ as a proxy for a deep attention stack. First, sums of readouts model multi-head attention with a linear output projection. Second, products model the multiplicative interactions generated by stacking layers with gating and residual connections: a depth-$L$ stack computes, in its polynomial part, products of layerwise linear reads. We are therefore studying the closure of the architecture class under the operations the architecture actually implements, and no more.

**Definition 2.9 (One-hot positional encoding).** Let $\mathrm{enc} : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa) \to \mathrm{Seq}(\iota, \kappa \sqcup \iota)$ be
$$\mathrm{enc}(x)_i \;=\; \big( x_i \,,\, \mathbf{1}_{\{\cdot = i\}} \big),$$
i.e. token $i$ keeps its $\kappa$-block of features and acquires an $\iota$-block whose $j$-th coordinate is $1$ if $j=i$ and $0$ otherwise. It is continuous (each coordinate is either a coordinate projection or a constant).

**Definition 2.10 (Marked-token encoding).** For $i \in \iota$ let $m_i : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa) \to \mathrm{Seq}(\iota, \kappa\sqcup\{\ast\})$ be
$$m_i(x)_j \;=\; \big( x_j \,,\, \mathbf{1}_{\{j=i\}} \big),$$
which appends a single mark feature flagging the queried position. For a marked sequence $z$ write $\mathrm{feat}(z)_{i,a} = z_{i,a}$ (the $\kappa$-block) and $\mathrm{mark}(z)_i = z_{i,\ast}$.

**Definition 2.11 (Equivariance).** A map $F : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)\to\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ is *$\Gamma$-equivariant* if
$$F(g\cdot x)_{i,a} \;=\; F(x)_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i),\, a} \qquad \text{for all } g\in\Gamma,\ x,\ i,\ a.$$

## 3. Stability and separation

Two structural lemmas carry all the weight.

**Lemma 3.1 (Readouts separate sequences).** If $x \neq y$ then some readout takes different values at $x$ and $y$. Consequently $\mathcal{A}$ separates points of $\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$.

*Proof.* If $x \neq y$ there are $i_0, a_0$ with $x_{i_0,a_0}\neq y_{i_0,a_0}$. Take $w$ the one-hot table $w_{i,a} = \mathbf{1}_{\{i=i_0,\,a=a_0\}}$. Then $R_w(z) = z_{i_0,a_0}$ for every $z$, so $R_w(x)\neq R_w(y)$. Since $R_w\in\mathcal{A}$, the algebra separates points. $\square$

Note the by-product: **every coordinate evaluation $z\mapsto z_{i_0,a_0}$ is itself a readout**, hence a uniform-score attention head. This is used repeatedly.

**Lemma 3.2 (The attention algebra is stable under the token action).** For every $\sigma \in \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$, precomposition with $\sigma\cdot(-)$ maps $\mathcal{A}$ into $\mathcal{A}$.

*Proof.* Precomposition $p \mapsto p \circ (\sigma\cdot(-))$ is a unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra homomorphism of $C(\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa),\mathbb{R})$, since it is composition with a fixed continuous map. It suffices therefore to check it on generators. Compute
$$R_w(\sigma\cdot x) \;=\; \sum_i\sum_a w_{i,a}\, x_{\sigma(i),a} \;=\; \sum_i\sum_a w_{\sigma^{-1}(i),a}\, x_{i,a} \;=\; R_{w\circ\sigma^{-1}}(x),$$
reindexing the outer sum by $\sigma$. So the image of a readout is a readout. Since the preimage of $\mathcal{A}$ under an algebra map is a subalgebra containing all generators, it contains $\mathcal{A}$. $\square$

Lemma 3.2 is the one place where the architecture's specific structure matters. A model class that is *not* closed under relabelling positions cannot be symmetrized, and the entire positive half would collapse.

**Definition 3.3 (Symmetrization).** For $q \in C(\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa),\mathbb{R})$ set
$$(\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q)(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma} q(g\cdot x).$$

**Lemma 3.4 (Properties of symmetrization).**
(i) $\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q$ is $\Gamma$-invariant.
(ii) If $q\in\mathcal{A}$ then $\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q \in \mathcal{A}$.
(iii) *(Averaging is free.)* If $f$ is $\Gamma$-invariant, $K$ is $\Gamma$-saturated, and $|q(x)-f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x\in K$, then $|(\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q)(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in K$.

*Proof.* (i) For $\tau \in \Gamma$, reindexing the sum by left multiplication $g \mapsto \tau g$ (a bijection of $\Gamma$) together with $\tau\cdot(g\cdot x) = (g\tau)\cdot x$ shows $(\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q)(\tau\cdot x) = (\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q)(x)$.
(ii) Each summand lies in $\mathcal{A}$ by Lemma 3.2; subalgebras are closed under finite sums and scalar multiples.
(iii) Fix $x\in K$. Saturation gives $g\cdot x\in K$ for each $g\in\Gamma$, and invariance of the target gives $f(g\cdot x) = f(x)$; hence $|q(g\cdot x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for every $g\in\Gamma$. Averaging,
$$\left| (\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q)(x) - f(x) \right| = \frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\left| \sum_{g\in\Gamma}\big( q(g\cdot x) - f(x)\big)\right| \le \frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{g\in\Gamma}\left| q(g\cdot x)-f(x)\right| < \varepsilon. \qquad\square$$

## 4. The positive half: symmetric universality

**Theorem 4.1 (Symmetric Universality).** Let $(\Gamma,\mathrm{act})$ be a finite token-symmetry group, let $K \subseteq \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ be compact and $\Gamma$-saturated, let $g$ be a continuous $\Gamma$-invariant functional, and let $\varepsilon > 0$. Then there exists $p \in \mathcal{A}$ such that

1. $p$ is $\Gamma$-invariant, and
2. $|p(x) - g(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in K$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 3.1, $\mathcal{A}$ is a point-separating subalgebra of $C(\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa),\mathbb{R})$ containing the constants. The Stone–Weierstrass theorem, applied on the compact set $K$, yields $q\in\mathcal{A}$ with $\sup_{x\in K}|q(x)-g(x)| < \varepsilon$. Put $p = \mathcal{S}_\Gamma q$. Lemma 3.4(ii) gives $p\in\mathcal{A}$, Lemma 3.4(i) gives invariance, and Lemma 3.4(iii) gives the error bound. $\square$

The proof is worth pausing over. The unconstrained approximant $q$ is generically *not* symmetric; symmetry is imposed afterwards, by a projection, at zero cost in accuracy. Symmetry is not a constraint that must be respected during the search; it is a retraction that can be applied at the end.

**Corollary 4.2 (Permutation-symmetric transformers).** Taking $\Gamma = \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$: on every compact permutation-saturated domain, every continuous permutation-invariant functional is uniformly approximated by permutation-invariant elements of the attention algebra. This is exactly the reach of a positional-encoding-free attention stack.

**Corollary 4.3 (Relative/shift symmetry).** Let $r \in \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ and suppose $g$ satisfies $g(r^k\cdot x) = g(x)$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and $K$ satisfies $x\in K \Rightarrow r^k\cdot x\in K$. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $p\in\mathcal{A}$ with $p(r^k\cdot x) = p(x)$ for all $k, x$, and $|p-g|<\varepsilon$ on $K$.

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 4.1 with $\Gamma = \langle r\rangle$, the (finite, since $\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ is finite) cyclic subgroup generated by $r$, with $\mathrm{act}$ the inclusion. Every element of $\langle r\rangle$ is a nonnegative power of $r$ — in a finite group, the subgroup generated by $r$ coincides with the set of its natural-number powers — so the hypotheses transfer and the conclusion specializes back. $\square$

## 5. The negative half: a sharp barrier

The following inequality is the engine of every impossibility result below. It costs nothing and gives everything.

**Lemma 5.1 (Invariance defect inequality).** Let $\sigma$ be a permutation, let $p$ satisfy $p(\sigma\cdot x) = p(x)$ for all $x$, and let $g$ be arbitrary. Then for all $x$,
$$\big| g(x) - g(\sigma\cdot x) \big| \;\le\; \big| p(x)-g(x) \big| \;+\; \big| p(\sigma\cdot x)-g(\sigma\cdot x)\big|.$$

*Proof.* Using $p(\sigma\cdot x) = p(x)$,
$$g(x) - g(\sigma\cdot x) \;=\; -\big(p(x)-g(x)\big) + \big(p(\sigma\cdot x) - g(\sigma\cdot x)\big),$$
and apply the triangle inequality. $\square$

The left-hand side is a property of the *target alone*; the right-hand side is at most twice the model's uniform error. Everything follows.

**Theorem 5.2 (The symmetric class is uniformly closed).** Let $\sigma$ be a permutation and $K$ a $\sigma$-stable set. Suppose $g$ has the property that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\sigma$-invariant $p$ with $|p-g|<\varepsilon$ on $K$. Then $g(\sigma\cdot x) = g(x)$ for every $x\in K$.

*Proof.* Suppose $\delta := |g(x)-g(\sigma\cdot x)| > 0$ for some $x \in K$. Choose $p$ $\sigma$-invariant with uniform error $< \delta/4$ on $K$. Both $x$ and $\sigma\cdot x$ lie in $K$, so Lemma 5.1 gives $\delta < \delta/4 + \delta/4 = \delta/2$, a contradiction. $\square$

Theorem 5.2 says the invariant class is closed in the uniform topology: *nothing outside it is even a limit of things inside it*. Combined with Theorem 4.1, the uniform closure of $\mathcal{A}^\Gamma$ on a compact saturated $K$ is exactly the $\Gamma$-invariant continuous functions on $K$.

Closedness is qualitative. The next result is quantitative, and it is sharp.

**Theorem 5.3 (Orbit Barrier — lower bound).** Let $g : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)\to\mathbb{R}$, let $x$ be a sequence, and define the *orbit oscillation*
$$\mathrm{osc}(g,x) \;=\; \max_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)} g(\sigma\cdot x) \;-\; \min_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)} g(\sigma\cdot x).$$
Then every permutation-invariant $p$ satisfies
$$\max_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)} \big| p(\sigma\cdot x) - g(\sigma\cdot x)\big| \;\ge\; \frac{\mathrm{osc}(g,x)}{2}.$$

*Proof.* Let $\sigma_M$ and $\sigma_m$ achieve the maximum and minimum of $\sigma\mapsto g(\sigma\cdot x)$ over the (finite) symmetric group, and write $E$ for the left-hand side. Invariance gives $p(\sigma_M\cdot x) = p(x) = p(\sigma_m\cdot x)$; call this common value $c$. Then
$$g(\sigma_M\cdot x) - c \;\le\; |c - g(\sigma_M\cdot x)| \;\le\; E, \qquad c - g(\sigma_m\cdot x) \;\le\; |c - g(\sigma_m\cdot x)| \;\le\; E.$$
Adding, $\mathrm{osc}(g,x) = g(\sigma_M\cdot x)-g(\sigma_m\cdot x) \le 2E$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Orbit Barrier — attained).** With $M = \max_\sigma g(\sigma\cdot x)$ and $m=\min_\sigma g(\sigma\cdot x)$, the constant model $p \equiv (M+m)/2$ is permutation-invariant and satisfies
$$\big| p(\sigma\cdot x) - g(\sigma\cdot x)\big| \;\le\; \frac{\mathrm{osc}(g,x)}{2} \qquad \text{for every } \sigma.$$

*Proof.* A constant function is invariant under everything. For any $\sigma$ we have $m \le g(\sigma\cdot x)\le M$, hence
$$-\frac{M-m}{2} \;\le\; \frac{M+m}{2} - g(\sigma\cdot x) \;\le\; \frac{M-m}{2}. \qquad \square$$

Together, Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 give an *equality*: the optimal invariant approximation error on a single orbit is exactly $\mathrm{osc}(g,x)/2$, achieved by the orbit midpoint. This is a rare situation in approximation theory — a barrier with no slack.

**Theorem 5.5 (Position-reading barrier).** Let $\sigma$ be a token symmetry with $\sigma(i_0)\neq i_0$, fix a feature $a_0$, and let $p$ be any $\sigma$-invariant functional. Then there is a sequence $x$ with
$$\big| p(x) - x_{i_0,a_0} \big| \;\ge\; \tfrac12.$$

*Proof.* Let $u$ be the one-hot table $u_{i,a} = \mathbf{1}_{\{i=i_0,\ a=a_0\}}$, so $u_{i_0,a_0} = 1$ while $(\sigma\cdot u)_{i_0,a_0} = u_{\sigma(i_0),a_0} = 0$ because $\sigma(i_0)\neq i_0$. Apply Lemma 5.1 to the target $g(y)=y_{i_0,a_0}$ at the point $u$:
$$1 = |1-0| \;\le\; |p(u)-1| + |p(\sigma\cdot u)-0|.$$
If $|p(u)-u_{i_0,a_0}| \ge 1/2$ we are done with $x=u$; otherwise $|p(u)-1| < 1/2$, so the displayed inequality forces $|p(\sigma\cdot u) - (\sigma\cdot u)_{i_0,a_0}| > 1/2$ and we take $x = \sigma\cdot u$. $\square$

Theorem 5.5 applies verbatim to fully permutation-symmetric models and, crucially, to shift-symmetric ones: relative positional encodings do not read absolute position, and the failure is at least $1/2$ in the sup norm on the unit cube. The smallest instance is worth recording.

**Corollary 5.6.** No permutation-invariant functional $p$ of two-token, one-feature sequences approximates "the first coordinate" to uniform accuracy better than $1/2$: there is always an $x \in \mathbb{R}^{2}$ with $|p(x)-x_1|\ge 1/2$.

*Proof.* Theorem 5.5 with $\sigma$ the transposition of the two positions. $\square$

## 6. The repair: positional encoding restores universality

The barrier is real, so something must be added. What is striking is that the repair does not touch the model's symmetry: it changes the data.

**Definition 6.1 (Invariant decoder).** Define $\mathrm{dec} : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa\sqcup\iota) \to \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ by
$$\mathrm{dec}(z)_{i,a} \;=\; \sum_{j\in\iota} z_{j,\,\mathrm{pos}(i)} \; z_{j,\,\mathrm{feat}(a)},$$
where $\mathrm{pos}(i)$ is coordinate $i$ of the positional block and $\mathrm{feat}(a)$ is coordinate $a$ of the feature block. This is exactly a single attention pattern: the positional block supplies the attention weights, the feature block supplies the values.

**Lemma 6.2.** $\mathrm{dec}$ is continuous; it inverts the encoding, $\mathrm{dec}(\mathrm{enc}(x)) = x$; and it is permutation-invariant, $\mathrm{dec}(\sigma\cdot z) = \mathrm{dec}(z)$ for every $\sigma$.

*Proof.* Continuity: each output coordinate is a finite sum of products of coordinate projections. Inversion: $\mathrm{enc}(x)_{j,\mathrm{pos}(i)} = \mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}$, so the sum over $j$ collapses to the single term $j=i$, giving $\mathrm{enc}(x)_{i,\mathrm{feat}(a)} = x_{i,a}$. Invariance: replacing $z$ by $\sigma\cdot z$ replaces the summand indexed by $j$ with the summand indexed by $\sigma(j)$, and summing a function over a finite set is unchanged by a bijective reindexing. $\square$

**Definition 6.3 (Encoded saturation).** For $K\subseteq\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ put
$$\widehat{K} \;=\; \bigcup_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)} \sigma\cdot\big(\mathrm{enc}(K)\big).$$
It contains $\mathrm{enc}(K)$, is permutation-saturated by construction, and is compact when $K$ is (a finite union of continuous images of a compact set).

**Theorem 6.4 (Positional encoding restores universality).** Let $g$ be *any* continuous functional of sequences, $K$ compact (no saturation hypothesis), $\varepsilon>0$. Then there is a **permutation-invariant** $p \in \mathcal{A}(\iota, \kappa\sqcup\iota)$ with
$$\big| p(\mathrm{enc}(x)) - g(x) \big| < \varepsilon \qquad \text{for all } x\in K.$$

*Proof.* Set $G = g\circ\mathrm{dec}$, a continuous functional on the augmented sequence space. By Lemma 6.2 it is permutation-invariant. Apply Corollary 4.2 on the compact saturated set $\widehat{K}$ to obtain a permutation-invariant $p\in\mathcal{A}(\iota,\kappa\sqcup\iota)$ with $|p - G| < \varepsilon$ on $\widehat{K}$. For $x \in K$ we have $\mathrm{enc}(x)\in\widehat{K}$ and $G(\mathrm{enc}(x)) = g(\mathrm{dec}(\mathrm{enc}(x))) = g(x)$, which is the claim. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5 (Sequence-to-sequence universality with positional encoding).** Let $F : \mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)\to\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ have all coordinate functions $x\mapsto F(x)_{i,a}$ continuous, let $K$ be compact and $\varepsilon>0$. Then there is a family $(P_{i,a})_{i\in\iota, a\in\kappa}$ of permutation-invariant elements of $\mathcal{A}(\iota,\kappa\sqcup\iota)$ with
$$\big| P_{i,a}(\mathrm{enc}(x)) - F(x)_{i,a}\big| < \varepsilon \qquad \text{for all } x\in K,\ i\in\iota,\ a\in\kappa.$$

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 6.4 to each of the finitely many coordinate functionals $x\mapsto F(x)_{i,a}$, with the same $K$ and the same $\varepsilon$, and collect the resulting models. Because $\iota$ and $\kappa$ are finite, the accuracy is simultaneous over all output slots with no loss. $\square$

**Discussion.** The conceptual content of Theorems 6.4 and 6.5 is that positional encoding is *not* symmetry breaking. The approximating models remain exactly permutation-invariant as functions on the augmented space; what has changed is that the input now carries its own order, in a form a symmetric model is permitted to consult. The decoder $\mathrm{dec}$ is the explicit witness: an invariant, attention-shaped operation that reconstructs the ordered sequence from its tagged version. Order was never incompatible with symmetry; it was incompatible with *ignorance*.

Note also the pleasing fact that $\mathrm{dec}$ is a *quadratic* expression in the encoded input — the same bilinear "weights times values" shape as an attention head. The repair is implementable by the very mechanism it repairs.

## 7. The exact converse: equivariant sequence-to-sequence maps

Scalar functionals are only half the story. A transformer layer maps sequences to sequences, and the symmetry it possesses is *equivariance*, not invariance. We now show that the two notions carry the same information, and deduce an exact characterization of the expressive power of symmetric attention.

**Lemma 7.1 (Marking intertwines the action).** For every $\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$, every $i$, every $x$:
$$m_i(\sigma\cdot x) \;=\; \sigma\cdot m_{\sigma(i)}(x).$$

*Proof.* Compare token $j$ of each side. On the feature block both equal $x_{\sigma(j)}$. On the mark coordinate, the left side is $\mathbf{1}_{\{j=i\}}$ and the right is $\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma(j)=\sigma(i)\}}$; these agree because $\sigma$ is injective. $\square$

**Corollary 7.2 (Marked invariant functionals give equivariant heads).** If $G$ is $\sigma$-invariant then the head $i \mapsto G(m_i(x))$ is $\sigma$-equivariant:
$$G\big(m_i(\sigma\cdot x)\big) \;=\; G\big(m_{\sigma(i)}(x)\big).$$

*Proof.* Lemma 7.1 followed by invariance of $G$. $\square$

**Definition 7.3 (Marked functional).** Given $F:\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)\to\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ and $a\in\kappa$, define $G^F_a$ on marked sequences by
$$G^F_a(z) \;=\; \sum_{i\in\iota} F\big(\mathrm{feat}(z)\big)_{i,a}\;\mathrm{mark}(z)_i,$$
the contraction of the $a$-th output channel of $F$ against the mark coordinates.

**Lemma 7.4.** (i) If all coordinates of $F$ are continuous then each $G^F_a$ is continuous. (ii) If $F$ is $\Gamma$-equivariant then each $G^F_a$ is $\Gamma$-invariant. (iii) $G^F_a(m_i(x)) = F(x)_{i,a}$ for all $x,i,a$.

*Proof.* (i) $\mathrm{feat}$ and $\mathrm{mark}$ are coordinate projections, hence continuous; a finite sum of products of continuous functions is continuous.
(ii) Note $\mathrm{feat}(g\cdot z) = g\cdot\mathrm{feat}(z)$ and $\mathrm{mark}(g\cdot z)_i = \mathrm{mark}(z)_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i)}$. Then
$$G^F_a(g\cdot z) = \sum_i F(g\cdot\mathrm{feat}(z))_{i,a}\,\mathrm{mark}(z)_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i)} = \sum_i F(\mathrm{feat}(z))_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i),a}\,\mathrm{mark}(z)_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i)},$$
using equivariance, and the last sum equals $G^F_a(z)$ by reindexing along the bijection $\mathrm{act}(g)$.
(iii) $\mathrm{feat}(m_i(x)) = x$ and $\mathrm{mark}(m_i(x))_{i'} = \mathbf{1}_{\{i'=i\}}$, so the sum collapses to the term $i'=i$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.5 (Equivariance–Invariance Representation Theorem).** For $F:\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)\to\mathrm{Seq}(\iota,\kappa)$ the following are equivalent:

1. All coordinates of $F$ are continuous and $F$ is $\Gamma$-equivariant.
2. There is a family $(G_a)_{a\in\kappa}$ of continuous $\Gamma$-**invariant** functionals on marked sequences with
$$F(x)_{i,a} \;=\; G_a\big(m_i(x)\big) \qquad \text{for all } x, i, a.$$

*Proof.* $(1)\Rightarrow(2)$: take $G_a = G^F_a$ and invoke Lemma 7.4.
$(2)\Rightarrow(1)$: continuity of each coordinate is the composite of the continuous $m_i$ with the continuous $G_a$. Equivariance: for $g \in \Gamma$,
$$F(g\cdot x)_{i,a} = G_a\big(m_i(g\cdot x)\big) = G_a\big(m_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i)}(x)\big) = F(x)_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i),a},$$
where the middle step is Corollary 7.2. $\square$

Theorem 7.5 is the structural heart of the paper: **equivariant maps and invariant functionals are the same data, read through the marked-token window.** With it, the equivariant universality theorem is a corollary of the invariant one.

**Theorem 7.6 (Equivariant Universality).** Let $(\Gamma,\mathrm{act})$ be a finite token-symmetry group, let $F$ be a continuous $\Gamma$-equivariant sequence-to-sequence map, let $K$ be compact, and let $\varepsilon>0$. Then there is a family $(P_a)_{a\in\kappa}$ with:

1. $P_a \in \mathcal{A}(\iota, \kappa\sqcup\{\ast\})$ for every $a$;
2. each $P_a$ is $\Gamma$-invariant;
3. the induced head family is **exactly** $\Gamma$-equivariant: $P_a\big(m_i(g\cdot x)\big) = P_a\big(m_{\mathrm{act}(g)(i)}(x)\big)$ for all $a,g,i,x$;
4. $\big| P_a(m_i(x)) - F(x)_{i,a}\big| < \varepsilon$ for all $x\in K$, $i\in\iota$, $a\in\kappa$.

*Proof.* Fix $a$. By Lemma 7.4 the marked functional $G^F_a$ is continuous and $\Gamma$-invariant. Let
$$\widetilde{K} \;=\; \bigcup_{g\in\Gamma}\ \bigcup_{i\in\iota}\ g\cdot\big(m_i(K)\big),$$
a finite union of continuous images of a compact set, hence compact, and $\Gamma$-saturated by construction (applying $\tau\in\Gamma$ to $g\cdot m_i(K)$ lands in $(g\tau)\cdot m_i(K)$). Theorem 4.1 supplies a $\Gamma$-invariant $P_a\in\mathcal{A}$ with $|P_a - G^F_a| < \varepsilon$ on $\widetilde{K}$. Claim 3 is Corollary 7.2 applied to the invariant $P_a$. Claim 4 follows since $m_i(x)\in\widetilde{K}$ for $x\in K$ and $G^F_a(m_i(x)) = F(x)_{i,a}$ by Lemma 7.4(iii). $\square$

**Corollary 7.7 (Full permutation equivariance).** With $\Gamma = \mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$: positional-encoding-free attention, queried through the marked-token encoding, is a universal approximator of the continuous permutation-equivariant sequence-to-sequence maps, and the approximants are exactly equivariant.

Together with Theorem 5.5 — which shows that a *non*-equivariant target is unreachable, by a margin of at least $1/2$ — Corollary 7.7 pins down the expressive power of symmetric attention exactly: it is the class of continuous equivariant maps, nothing less and nothing more.

Point 3 of Theorem 7.6 deserves emphasis for applications. The approximation error appears only in the *values*; the symmetry identity holds exactly, at every input, for every model in the constructed family. In physics- or chemistry-oriented modelling, where equivariance is a hard structural requirement rather than a soft prior, this is the difference between a guarantee and a hope.

## 8. Algorithms

The proofs are constructive enough to run. We describe three procedures.

### 8.1 Group symmetrization

**Input:** a model $q$ (a polynomial in readouts), a symmetry group $\Gamma$ acting on positions.
**Output:** a $\Gamma$-invariant model $\mathcal{S}_\Gamma q$ with the same or better error on invariant targets.

```
for each g in Γ:
    q_g ← q composed with the coordinate permutation act(g)
return (1/|Γ|) · Σ_g q_g
```

Cost: $|\Gamma|$ evaluations per forward pass. Lemma 3.2 guarantees each $q_g$ is again in the architecture class — for readouts, the permuted model is obtained simply by permuting the rows of the weight table, at no evaluation cost. The naive cost $|\Gamma|$ is the target of the head-count conjecture in §10.

### 8.2 Exact orbit barrier

**Input:** a target $g$, a base sequence $x$, a symmetry group $\Gamma$.
**Output:** the optimal invariant error on the orbit of $x$, and the optimal model value.

```
values ← { g(σ · x) : σ ∈ Γ }
M ← max(values);  m ← min(values)
return  optimal_error = (M − m)/2,  optimal_value = (M + m)/2
```

Correctness is Theorems 5.3 and 5.4. Cost is $|\Gamma|$ target evaluations. For the "read position $i_0$" target on the unit cube the output is exactly $1/2$, recovering Theorem 5.5 numerically.

### 8.3 Positional decode-and-approximate

**Input:** a target functional $g$, a compact domain $K$, an accuracy $\varepsilon$.
**Output:** a permutation-invariant model $p$ on encoded sequences with $|p\circ\mathrm{enc} - g| < \varepsilon$ on $K$.

```
G ← g ∘ dec                       # invariant, continuous, on augmented space
q ← StoneWeierstrass(G, hat(K), ε)  # polynomial in readouts of the augmented sequence
p ← symmetrize(q, Sym(ι))
return p
```

In practice, "StoneWeierstrass" is replaced by fitting a polynomial in readouts (a shallow attention stack with multiplicative gating); the theory guarantees a solution exists at every accuracy. Crucially, the *decode* step is itself a single quadratic attention pattern, so the whole pipeline is expressible in the architecture.

## 9. Applications and interpretation

**Positional encodings, explained rather than assumed.** The folklore statement "transformers need positional encodings" acquires a precise form: without them the model is confined to permutation-invariant functionals (Theorem 5.2), the confinement costs exactly half the orbit oscillation (Theorems 5.3–5.4), reading absolute position costs at least $1/2$ (Theorem 5.5), and one-hot tags restore everything (Theorems 6.4–6.5) without breaking symmetry.

**Relative encodings are a genuinely weaker regime.** Because Theorem 5.5 needs only *some* symmetry moving $i_0$, a shift-symmetric model — the mathematical idealization of relative positional encoding — inherits the same $1/2$ barrier for absolute-position targets. Corollary 4.3 tells us what such a model *can* do: everything shift-invariant. This matches the empirical observation that relative-encoding models excel at translation-invariant structure and struggle with absolute indexing.

**Set and graph models.** Architectures for unordered data (point clouds, sets, molecular graphs with no canonical vertex order) impose permutation symmetry deliberately. Corollary 4.2 says they are fully expressive for the invariant targets they are designed for, and Corollary 7.7 says the equivariant per-element predictions they produce are fully expressive too, with exact structural symmetry.

**Data augmentation versus architectural symmetry.** Lemma 3.4(iii) is a statement about training as much as about approximation: symmetrizing a model never degrades its accuracy on a symmetric target. Averaging a trained model's predictions over the group is therefore a free improvement whenever the ground truth is invariant — a theoretical underpinning for test-time augmentation.

**A design criterion.** Lemma 3.2 identifies the one property an architecture must have for the whole theory to apply: closure under relabelling of positions. Any proposed layer that fails it cannot be symmetrized, and none of the positive results survive. This is a concrete, checkable criterion for new attention variants.

## 10. Future directions

The results above suggest sharp, falsifiable follow-up conjectures.

**C1. A head-count law.** The averaging proof of Theorem 4.1 spends $|\Gamma|$ terms — for the full symmetric group, $n!$. We conjecture that this is enormously wasteful: for every $\Gamma$-invariant continuous $g$ on a compact saturated $K$ and every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a $\Gamma$-invariant element of $\mathcal{A}$ that $\varepsilon$-approximates $g$ and is a sum of at most $\mathrm{poly}(d, 1/\varepsilon)$ products of readouts — in particular a bound independent of $|\Gamma|$. The intuition is that averaging is a *projection*, not a construction: the invariant subalgebra is already generated by the multisymmetric power sums
$$x \;\longmapsto\; \sum_i \prod_a (x_{i,a})^{e_a},$$
each of which is a single product of readouts. Eliminating the group sum in favour of such a generating set would turn an exponential construction into a polynomial one. The conjecture is falsifiable by explicit finite computation at small $n$, tested against the $1/2$ barrier of Theorem 5.5.

**C2. Softmax realizability.** We conjecture that the uniform closure on compacta of finite compositions of genuine softmax attention layers, with residual connections and coordinatewise multiplicative gating, contains the whole attention algebra $\mathcal{A}$, with an explicit depth/width bound. Proposition 2.7 gives the base case (uniform-score heads are readouts); the content of the conjecture is that products of readouts are realizable by composition and gating rather than merely postulated.

**C3. Quantitative barrier for approximate symmetry.** If a model is only $\eta$-approximately invariant, Lemma 5.1 degrades to a bound involving $\eta$. We expect a clean interpolation: optimal error at least $\mathrm{osc}(g,x)/2 - O(\eta)$, giving a continuous trade-off between symmetry and expressivity and a principled way to price "soft" positional encodings.

**C4. Infinite and variable-length index sets.** Every result here is stated for a finite position set. Extending to length-generalizing models — where $\iota$ varies and one asks for uniformity in $n$ — would require replacing $\mathrm{Sym}(\iota)$ by a compact group with Haar measure and Stone–Weierstrass by a suitable Banach-algebraic density theorem. The averaging argument survives verbatim with Haar averaging; the open question is whether the approximants can be chosen with $n$-independent complexity.

**C5. Optimal encodings.** The one-hot encoding uses $n$ extra features per token. Is $n$ necessary? We conjecture a lower bound of $\Omega(\log n)$ extra features for any continuous encoding admitting an invariant continuous decoder, and ask whether $O(\log n)$ suffices — which would explain the empirical success of low-dimensional sinusoidal encodings.

## 11. Conclusion

We have given a complete, two-sided description of the expressive power of attention architectures constrained by a finite group of token symmetries. On the positive side, symmetric attention is uniformly dense in the symmetric continuous functionals on compact saturated domains, for *any* finite symmetry group; the proof is Stone–Weierstrass followed by free group averaging, legitimate because the attention algebra is stable under relabelling positions. On the negative side, the symmetric class is uniformly closed, and the optimal error outside it is exactly half the orbit oscillation — in particular, reading an absolute position costs at least $1/2$. One-hot positional encoding removes the deficit entirely, by an explicitly invariant attention-shaped decoder, without any loss of symmetry in the model. And in the sequence-to-sequence setting, equivariance and invariance are the same data through the marked-token window, so symmetric attention approximates exactly the continuous equivariant maps, with symmetry holding on the nose.

What we find most satisfying is the absence of a gap. Density and closedness meet; the lower bound and the achievability meet. There is a single number — half the orbit oscillation — that says everything about what symmetry costs, and a single construction — tag the positions, decode invariantly — that says everything about how to get it back.

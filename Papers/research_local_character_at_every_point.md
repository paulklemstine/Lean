## Abstract

We study the order topology on Conway's field $\mathbf{No}$ of surreal numbers and determine its local structure completely. Our central result is that **no point of $\mathbf{No}$ admits a small neighbourhood basis**: for every surreal $c$ and every set-indexed family $(B_i)_{i \in I}$ of neighbourhoods of $c$, there exists a neighbourhood of $c$ containing no $B_i$. Consequently the character of every point exceeds every cardinal, the neighbourhood filter $\mathcal{N}(c)$ is never countably generated, and $\mathbf{No}$ is nowhere first countable and not metrizable.

The proof has two ingredients. The first is a *coinitiality failure* theorem: for any set-indexed family $(r_i)_{i \in I}$ of positive surreals, the Conway cut $\{0 \mid (r_i)\}$ is a positive surreal below all of them. This yields the result at the origin. The second is a *transfer* mechanism: we verify that $\mathbf{No}$ with its order topology is a Hausdorff topological additive group, so that each translation $x \mapsto x + c$ is a homeomorphism, carrying the statement at $0$ to every point.

We give a second, independent proof of the failure of first countability via **sequential discreteness**: a sequence in $\mathbf{No}$ converges if and only if it is eventually constant, while $\mathbf{No}$ is not a discrete space. We then situate these results within a complete description of the local topology: the *archimedean monads* $\mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ form a clopen neighbourhood basis at every point, making $\mathbf{No}$ zero-dimensional and totally separated (hence admitting no nonconstant continuous map from a connected space); and no neighbourhood of any point is compact, since the upper half of a monad has no least upper bound. The emergent picture is a rigid dichotomy: the local topology of $\mathbf{No}$ is governed entirely by archimedean classes, and every classical countability and compactness tool fails at every point simultaneously.

**Keywords:** surreal numbers, order topology, local character, first countability, Conway cut, archimedean class, zero-dimensional space, sequential discreteness, local compactness.

## 1. Introduction

Conway's surreal numbers $\mathbf{No}$ form a real-closed ordered field that contains, as ordered subfields or subclasses, the rationals, the reals, the ordinals, and a vast supply of infinitesimals. Its algebraic theory is by now classical. Its *topology*, by contrast, has an unusual reputation: everyone knows that "analysis on the surreals is hard," but the precise reason is rarely stated as a theorem.

This paper states it as a theorem, at every point at once.

### 1.1 The question

Any linearly ordered set carries a canonical **order topology**, generated by the open rays $(-\infty, a)$ and $(a, \infty)$, equivalently by the open intervals $(a,b)$ when the order is dense and has no endpoints. Give $\mathbf{No}$ this topology.

The *character* $\chi(c, X)$ of a point $c$ in a topological space $X$ is the least cardinality of a neighbourhood basis at $c$. Character $\le \aleph_0$ at every point is **first countability**, the hypothesis that underwrites the adequacy of sequences: in a first countable space, closure, continuity, and compactness all admit sequential characterizations, and metrizability implies it.

Our question: what is $\chi(c, \mathbf{No})$?

### 1.2 The answer

$\chi(c, \mathbf{No})$ is not a cardinal. For every set $I$ and every family $(B_i)_{i\in I}$ of neighbourhoods of $c$, some neighbourhood of $c$ contains none of the $B_i$; so no set-indexed family is a basis. In a set-theoretic framework where $\mathbf{No}$ is realized as a proper class (or as $\mathbf{No}_{\mathcal{U}}$ for a Grothendieck universe $\mathcal{U}$, with "small" meaning "$\mathcal{U}$-small"), this says the character of every point is a proper class — the class $\mathbf{On}$ of ordinals, conjecturally, in the exact sense discussed in §8.

### 1.3 Method and structure

The proof separates cleanly into an *engine* and a *transfer*.

- **The engine (§3).** Conway cuts allow one to construct a surreal strictly below an arbitrary set of positive surreals. This is a purely order-theoretic statement about $\mathbf{No}$ with no topological content, and it is the sole source of all the pathology.
- **The transfer (§4).** Density of the order plus the field structure makes $\mathbf{No}$ a topological group; translations are homeomorphisms; hence the local statement at $0$ propagates to every point.
- **The consequences (§5–§6).** Non-countable-generation of $\mathcal{N}(c)$, failure of first and second countability, non-metrizability, and — by a wholly separate route — sequential discreteness.
- **The positive description (§7).** What *does* control the local topology: archimedean monads, zero-dimensionality, total separation, and the failure of local compactness.

Sections 2 through 7 are self-contained: every definition and every statement is given in full, with a proof or a complete proof sketch.

## 2. Preliminaries: surreal numbers and Conway cuts

### 2.1 Games and numbers

A **pre-game** is a pair $x = (x^L, x^R)$ of indexed families of pre-games, written $x = \{x^L \mid x^R\}$, defined by recursion on a well-founded birthday. Order relations $\le$ and $<$ on pre-games are defined by the usual mutual recursion. A pre-game $x = \{x^L \mid x^R\}$ is **numeric** if every $x^L_i$ and $x^R_j$ is numeric and $x^L_i < x^R_j$ for all $i,j$. The **surreal numbers** $\mathbf{No}$ are the numeric pre-games modulo the equivalence $x \equiv y \iff x \le y \le x$. Addition, negation, and multiplication descend to $\mathbf{No}$, making it a linearly ordered field.

Throughout, we fix a universe level: $\mathbf{No}$ denotes the surreals whose defining trees are indexed by *sets* at that level, and "small" means "indexed by a set at that level." All families and index types below are small; the entire strength of our results lies in the fact that they hold for *every* small family, of arbitrary cardinality.

### 2.2 Cuts of arbitrary families

**Definition 2.1 (Conway cut).** Let $L$ and $R$ be index sets and let $\ell : L \to \mathbf{No}$, $r : R \to \mathbf{No}$ be families with $\ell_i < r_j$ for all $i \in L$, $j \in R$. Choose for each surreal a numeric representative pre-game, and set
$$\mathrm{cut}(\ell, r) := \{\, \ell_i \;(i \in L) \;\mid\; r_j \;(j \in R) \,\}.$$

**Lemma 2.2.** $\mathrm{cut}(\ell,r)$ is a well-defined surreal number, and for all $i \in L$, $j \in R$,
$$\ell_i \;<\; \mathrm{cut}(\ell,r) \;<\; r_j .$$

*Proof.* Numericity of the constructed pre-game is exactly the hypothesis $\ell_i < r_j$ together with numericity of the chosen representatives. The two inequalities are the defining "left options are below, right options are above" property of numeric games, transported along the identification of a surreal with the class of its representative. $\square$

Note the essential point: $L$ and $R$ may be *any* sets, of any cardinality. This is not a two-sided approximation like a Dedekind cut of rationals; it is a construction that consumes an arbitrarily large amount of data in a single step.

**Corollary 2.3 (Density).** $\mathbf{No}$ is densely ordered: if $x < y$ then $x < \{x \mid y\} < y$.

*Proof.* Lemma 2.2 with singleton index sets. $\square$

**Corollary 2.4 (No endpoints).** $\mathbf{No}$ has no maximum ($a < a+1$) and no minimum ($a - 1 < a$).

### 2.3 Dyadic scales

Mathematically we need the surreals $2^{-n}$, defined recursively by $2^{0} = 1$ and $2^{-(n+1)} = \{0 \mid 2^{-n}\}$. We write $\mathrm{ph}(n) := 2^{-n}$ and record:

**Lemma 2.5.** For all $n \in \mathbb{N}$: (i) $\mathrm{ph}(n) > 0$; (ii) $\mathrm{ph}(n+1) < \mathrm{ph}(n)$; (iii) $2\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) = \mathrm{ph}(n)$. Consequently, for $d > 0$: $d\,\mathrm{ph}(n) > 0$, $d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$, and
$$d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) + d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) \;=\; d\,\mathrm{ph}(n).$$

*Proof.* (iii) is the defining identity of the dyadic halving map; (i) and (ii) follow from it by induction, using positivity of $\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$ and $2\mathrm{ph}(n+1) = \mathrm{ph}(n)$. The scaled versions are multiplication by $d > 0$, which preserves order and distributes. $\square$

Identity (iii) — "two halves make a whole" — is used repeatedly below in the *doubling* arguments, where one shows that doubling an infinitesimal distance keeps it infinitesimal.

## 3. The engine: failure of coinitiality

**Theorem 3.1 (Coinitiality failure, arbitrary index).** Let $I$ be any set and $r : I \to \mathbf{No}$ a family with $r_i > 0$ for all $i$. Then there exists $y \in \mathbf{No}$ with
$$0 < y \qquad\text{and}\qquad y < r_i \ \text{ for all } i \in I.$$

*Proof.* The hypothesis says precisely that the singleton family $\{0\}$ lies strictly below the family $(r_i)$. Apply Lemma 2.2 with $\ell$ the constant family $0$ on a one-point index set and $r$ the given family, and put
$$y := \{\, 0 \mid (r_i)_{i\in I} \,\}.$$
Then $0 < y$ and $y < r_i$ for every $i$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2 (Countable case).** For every sequence $(r_n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ of positive surreals there is $y > 0$ with $y < r_n$ for all $n$.

**Corollary 3.3.** No countable — indeed no set-sized — subset of the positive surreals is coinitial in the positive surreals. Explicitly, for every countable $S \subseteq \mathbf{No}_{>0}$ there is $y > 0$ with $y < x$ for all $x \in S$. (If $S = \varnothing$, take $y = 1$.)

**Corollary 3.4 (Infinitesimals relative to a scale).** For every $d > 0$ there exists $e > 0$ with $e < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

*Proof.* Apply Corollary 3.2 to $r_n := d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$, which is positive by Lemma 2.5. $\square$

Theorem 3.1 is the *entire* source of the phenomena in this paper. It is worth isolating why it is so much stronger than the corresponding real statement. In $\mathbb{R}$, the family $(1/n)$ is coinitial in $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, and this is equivalent to the Archimedean property. In $\mathbf{No}$, coinitiality fails at *every* cardinality, because a Conway cut can absorb an arbitrarily large right-hand family in one construction step. The surreal line has no small "floor" above $0$.

## 4. The order topology and the transfer mechanism

### 4.1 The topology

**Definition 4.1.** $\mathbf{No}$ carries the order topology: the topology generated by $\{(-\infty,a)\} \cup \{(a,\infty)\}$. Since the order is dense with no endpoints (Corollaries 2.3, 2.4), the open intervals $(a,b)$ form a basis, and
$$s \in \mathcal{N}(c) \iff \exists\, \ell < c < r \text{ with } (\ell, r) \subseteq s. \tag{4.1}$$

The order topology on a linear order is Hausdorff, and on a densely ordered set it has no isolated points.

### 4.2 Continuity of addition

**Proposition 4.2.** Addition $\mathbf{No} \times \mathbf{No} \to \mathbf{No}$ is jointly continuous, and negation is continuous. Hence $\mathbf{No}$ is a Hausdorff topological additive group.

*Proof sketch.* Continuity of addition at $(a,b)$ requires, for each subbasic neighbourhood of $a+b$, a product of intervals mapping into it. Take the upper case: suppose $a + b < u$. Because the order is dense (Corollary 2.3), pick $e$ with $0 < e < u - a - b$ — concretely, apply density between $0$ and $u-a-b$. Then for $a' \in (a - 1, a + e)$ and $b' \in (b-1, b + (u - a - b - e))$ we get $a' + b' < a + b + e + (u-a-b-e) = u$. The lower case is symmetric. Continuity of negation is immediate from the fact that $x \mapsto -x$ is an order-reversing bijection, hence a homeomorphism for the order topology. $\square$

This is the technical heart of the *transfer*, and it is where density — itself a consequence of the Conway cut construction — enters a second time.

**Corollary 4.3 (Translations are homeomorphisms).** For each $c \in \mathbf{No}$, the map $T_c(x) = x + c$ is an order isomorphism and a homeomorphism of $\mathbf{No}$. Consequently
$$\mathcal{N}(x + c) \;=\; T_c{}_{*}\,\mathcal{N}(x), \qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad \mathcal{N}(c) \;=\; T_c{}_{*}\,\mathcal{N}(0).$$

*Proof.* $T_c$ is a bijection with inverse $T_{-c}$; both preserve order strictly, hence are continuous for the order topology (equivalently, both are continuous by Proposition 4.2). Pushforward of the neighbourhood filter along a homeomorphism is the neighbourhood filter of the image. $\square$

## 5. No point has a small neighbourhood basis

### 5.1 At the origin

**Theorem 5.1 (No small family of neighbourhoods of $0$ is a basis).** Let $I$ be any set and let $(B_i)_{i \in I}$ be neighbourhoods of $0$. Then there exists $s \in \mathcal{N}(0)$ with
$$B_i \not\subseteq s \quad\text{for every } i \in I.$$

*Proof.* By (4.1) choose for each $i$ surreals $\ell_i < 0 < r_i$ with $(\ell_i, r_i) \subseteq B_i$. The family $(r_i)_{i\in I}$ consists of positive surreals, so Theorem 3.1 supplies $y$ with
$$0 < y < r_i \quad (\forall i \in I).$$
Put $s := (-y, y)$, a neighbourhood of $0$ since $-y < 0 < y$. Fix $i$. Then $\ell_i < 0 < y < r_i$, so $y \in (\ell_i, r_i) \subseteq B_i$. But $y \notin s$, since membership in $(-y,y)$ would force $y < y$. Hence $B_i \not\subseteq s$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (Countable case).** For every sequence $(B_n)$ of neighbourhoods of $0$ there is $s \in \mathcal{N}(0)$ with $B_n \not\subseteq s$ for all $n$.

### 5.2 At every point

**Theorem 5.3 (Local character at every point).** Let $c \in \mathbf{No}$, let $I$ be any set, and let $(B_i)_{i\in I}$ be neighbourhoods of $c$. Then there exists $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ with $B_i \not\subseteq s$ for every $i$.

*Proof.* Let $h := T_{-c}$, the translation homeomorphism $x \mapsto x - c$, so $h(c) = 0$. Since $h$ is a homeomorphism, $h_*\mathcal{N}(c) = \mathcal{N}(0)$, and therefore each image $h(B_i)$ is a neighbourhood of $0$: indeed $h(B_i) \supseteq h(V)$ for an open $V \ni c$ inside $B_i$, and $h(V)$ is open containing $0$.

Apply Theorem 5.1 to the family $(h(B_i))_{i \in I}$, obtaining $s_0 \in \mathcal{N}(0)$ with $h(B_i) \not\subseteq s_0$ for all $i$. Set $s := h^{-1}(s_0)$; by continuity of $h$ at $c$ and $h(c) = 0$, $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$.

Suppose $B_i \subseteq s$ for some $i$. Then for any $z \in B_i$ we have $z \in h^{-1}(s_0)$, i.e. $h(z) \in s_0$; hence $h(B_i) \subseteq s_0$, contradicting the choice of $s_0$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.4 (Countable case).** For every $c$ and every sequence $(B_n)$ of neighbourhoods of $c$ there is $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ containing no $B_n$.

This is exactly the strengthening promised by the transfer principle: the failure of countable character migrates from $0$ to every surreal along translation.

## 6. Consequences

### 6.1 Countable generation

**Theorem 6.1.** For every $c \in \mathbf{No}$, the neighbourhood filter $\mathcal{N}(c)$ is **not countably generated**.

*Proof.* A countably generated filter admits an antitone countable basis $(B_n)_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$, so that $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ iff $B_n \subseteq s$ for some $n$. Apply Corollary 5.4 to $(B_n)$ to get $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ with $B_n \not\subseteq s$ for all $n$ — contradicting the basis property applied to that very $s$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.2 (Uncountable character).** Let $c \in \mathbf{No}$ and let $\mathcal{B}$ be any family of neighbourhoods of $c$ that is a neighbourhood basis, i.e. every $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ contains some $t \in \mathcal{B}$. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is uncountable.

*Proof.* $\mathcal{B}$ is nonempty (take $s = \mathbf{No}$). If $\mathcal{B}$ were countable, enumerate it as $\mathcal{B} = \{f_n : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$. By Corollary 5.4 there is $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ with $f_n \not\subseteq s$ for all $n$, contradicting the existence of some $t = f_n \in \mathcal{B}$ with $t \subseteq s$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Character exceeds every small cardinal).** Let $c \in \mathbf{No}$ and let $\mathcal{B}$ be a neighbourhood basis at $c$. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is not small: there is no bijection of $\mathcal{B}$ with a set at the ambient level.

*Proof.* Suppose $\mathcal{B}$ were small; index it as $(t_i)_{i \in I}$ for a set $I$, with each $t_i \in \mathcal{N}(c)$. Theorem 5.3 supplies $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ with $t_i \not\subseteq s$ for all $i$, contradicting the basis property. $\square$

Theorem 6.3 is the sharp form. It is not merely that $\chi(c) > \aleph_0$; it is that $\chi(c)$ is bounded below by *every* cardinal, so that the character is a proper class.

**Corollary 6.4.** $\mathbf{No}$ is not first countable; it is not second countable; it is not metrizable (nor pseudometrizable).

*Proof.* First countability would make $\mathcal{N}(0)$ countably generated, contradicting Theorem 6.1. Second countability implies first countability. Metrizability implies first countability via the basis $\{B(c, 1/n)\}$. $\square$

### 6.2 A second proof: sequential discreteness

**Theorem 6.5 (Sequential discreteness).** For a sequence $f : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbf{No}$ and $c \in \mathbf{No}$,
$$f_n \longrightarrow c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_n = c \text{ for all sufficiently large } n.$$

*Proof.* ($\Leftarrow$) A sequence eventually equal to $c$ agrees eventually with the constant sequence at $c$, which converges to $c$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $f_n \to c$ but the set $\{n : f_n \neq c\}$ is infinite. Define
$$d_n := \begin{cases} 1, & f_n = c,\\ |f_n - c|, & f_n \neq c.\end{cases}$$
Each $d_n$ is positive: in the first case trivially, in the second because $f_n - c \neq 0$ and absolute value of a nonzero element of a linearly ordered field is positive. By Corollary 3.2 there is $y > 0$ with $y < d_n$ for all $n$.

Now $(c - y,\, c + y)$ is a neighbourhood of $c$, so by convergence there is $N$ with $f_n \in (c-y, c+y)$ for $n \ge N$. Fix $n \ge N$ with $f_n \neq c$ (possible, since infinitely many indices qualify). From $c - y < f_n < c+y$ we get $|f_n - c| < y$; but $d_n = |f_n - c| > y$. Contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 6.6.** $\mathbf{No}$ is **not** discrete.

*Proof.* If it were, $\{0\}$ would be a neighbourhood of $0$, so by (4.1) there would be $\ell < 0 < r$ with $(\ell, r) \subseteq \{0\}$. By density (Corollary 2.3) choose $z$ with $0 < z < r$; then $z \in (\ell, r)$, so $z = 0$, contradicting $z > 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.7 (Independent proof of Corollary 6.4).** In a first countable space, a topology is determined by its convergent sequences; in particular, if every convergent sequence is eventually constant then every set is closed (a point in the closure of $A$ is the limit of a sequence in $A$, hence lies in $A$), so the space is discrete. Theorems 6.5 and 6.6 are therefore jointly incompatible with first countability.

This second route is conceptually independent of the translation argument: it uses only Corollary 3.2 and density, and it yields a memorable slogan — *sequences see the surreal line as a discrete set, but it is not one.*

## 7. The positive picture: archimedean monads

Having established what fails, we describe what governs the local topology.

### 7.1 Archimedean cuts are clopen

Two general criteria for the order topology on any linear order $\alpha$:

**Lemma 7.1.** A downward-closed $S \subseteq \alpha$ with no greatest element is open, since $S = \bigcup_{z \in S} (-\infty, z)$. Dually, an upward-closed $S$ with no least element is open, since $S = \bigcup_{z\in S}(z,\infty)$.

**Definition 7.2.** For $x, d \in \mathbf{No}$ put
$$\mathrm{Sm}(x,d) := \{\, z : \forall n \in \mathbb{N},\ z - x < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n) \,\}, \qquad \mathrm{Bg}(x,d) := \{\, z : \forall n\in\mathbb{N},\ x - z < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)\,\}.$$
$\mathrm{Sm}(x,d)$ consists of everything below $x$ together with the points exceeding $x$ by an amount infinitesimal relative to $d$; $\mathrm{Bg}(x,d)$ is its mirror image.

**Proposition 7.3.** For $d > 0$, $\mathrm{Sm}(x,d)$ and $\mathrm{Bg}(x,d)$ are clopen and contain $x$.

*Proof sketch (for $\mathrm{Sm}$; $\mathrm{Bg}$ is symmetric).* $x \in \mathrm{Sm}(x,d)$ because $0 < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$. Downward closure is immediate. No greatest element: given $a \in \mathrm{Sm}(x,d)$, if $a > x$ then $b := x + 2(a-x) > a$ still lies in $\mathrm{Sm}$, because $a - x < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$ gives $b - x = 2(a-x) < 2\,d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) = d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ using Lemma 2.5(iii); if $a \le x$ then take $b := x + e$ with $e$ from Corollary 3.4. So $\mathrm{Sm}$ is open by Lemma 7.1.

For the complement: it is upward-closed (if $z - x \ge d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ and $z \le w$ then $w - x \ge d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$), and has no least element, since from $a - x \ge d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ we get $x + d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) < a$ and $x + d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$ is again in the complement, witnessed at index $n+1$. So the complement is open, and $\mathrm{Sm}$ is clopen. $\square$

Notice that the "no greatest element" argument required *both* the doubling identity (for the generic case) and coinitiality failure (Corollary 3.4, for the degenerate case $a \le x$). The engine of §3 reappears here.

### 7.2 Total separation

**Lemma 7.4.** If $x < y$ then $y \notin \mathrm{Sm}(x, y-x)$.

*Proof.* Otherwise $y - x < (y-x)\,\mathrm{ph}(1)$, while $(y-x)\mathrm{ph}(1) + (y-x)\mathrm{ph}(1) = (y-x)\mathrm{ph}(0) = y-x$ and $(y-x)\mathrm{ph}(1) > 0$, forcing $(y-x)\mathrm{ph}(1) < y - x$. Contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 7.5 (Total separation).** $\mathbf{No}$ is totally separated: for $x \neq y$ there is a clopen partition $\mathbf{No} = U \sqcup V$ with $x \in U$, $y \in V$. In particular $\mathbf{No}$ is totally disconnected and not connected.

*Proof.* Assume $x < y$ (else swap). Take $U := \mathrm{Sm}(x, y-x)$ and $V := U^{c}$. Both are open by Proposition 7.3, they partition $\mathbf{No}$, $x \in U$ by Proposition 7.3, and $y \in V$ by Lemma 7.4. For non-connectedness, note $0 \in \mathrm{Sm}(0,1)$ and $1 \notin \mathrm{Sm}(0,1)$, so this clopen set is proper and nonempty. $\square$

### 7.3 Monads and zero-dimensionality

**Definition 7.6 (Archimedean monad).**
$$\mathrm{monad}(c,d) := \mathrm{Sm}(c,d) \cap \mathrm{Bg}(c,d) = \{\, z : \forall n,\ |z - c| < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n) \,\}.$$

**Proposition 7.7.** For $d > 0$: $\mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ is clopen, contains $c$, and satisfies $\mathrm{monad}(c,d) \subseteq (c-d, c+d)$.

*Proof.* Clopenness and membership are Proposition 7.3. For the inclusion, take $n = 0$: $z - c < d\,\mathrm{ph}(0) = d$ and $c - z < d$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.8 (Zero-dimensionality).** Every neighbourhood of every point contains a clopen neighbourhood of that point; the monads $\{\mathrm{monad}(c,d) : d > 0\}$ form a clopen neighbourhood basis at $c$.

*Proof.* Given $s \in \mathcal{N}(c)$, take $\ell < c < r$ with $(\ell,r) \subseteq s$ and set $d := \min(c - \ell,\, r - c) > 0$. Then $\mathrm{monad}(c,d) \subseteq (c-d, c+d) \subseteq (\ell, r) \subseteq s$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.9 (Monads partition each scale).** For fixed $d$, if $c' \in \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ then $\mathrm{monad}(c',d) = \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$; consequently any two monads at scale $d$ are equal or disjoint.

*Proof sketch.* Symmetry of the defining condition gives $c \in \mathrm{monad}(c',d)$. For containment, if $z \in \mathrm{monad}(c',d)$ then for each $n$, $|z - c| \le |z - c'| + |c' - c| < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) + d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1) = d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ by Lemma 2.5(iii). So $\mathrm{monad}(c',d) \subseteq \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$, and by symmetry equality. Disjointness follows: two intersecting monads at the same scale share a point $w$ and both equal $\mathrm{monad}(w,d)$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.10 (No nonconstant continuous curves).** If $X$ is a preconnected topological space and $f : X \to \mathbf{No}$ is continuous, then $f$ is constant. In particular there is no nonconstant continuous $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbf{No}$.

*Proof.* $f(X)$ is a preconnected subset of a totally disconnected space (Theorem 7.5), hence a subsingleton. $\square$

### 7.4 Nowhere local compactness

Zero-dimensional locally compact groups are the arena of Haar measure and Pontryagin duality; one might hope $\mathbf{No}$ joins them. It does not.

**Definition 7.11.** $U(c,d) := \mathrm{monad}(c,d) \cap (c, \infty)$, the *upper monad*.

**Lemma 7.12.** For $d > 0$, $U(c,d)$ is nonempty and $U(c,d) \subseteq (c, c+d)$.

*Proof.* Corollary 3.4 gives $e > 0$ with $e < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ for all $n$; then $c + e \in U(c,d)$. Inclusion is Proposition 7.7. $\square$

**Theorem 7.13 (No supremum).** For $d > 0$, no $x \in \mathbf{No}$ is a least upper bound of $U(c,d)$.

*Proof.* Let $x$ be an upper bound; then $x > c$ since $U(c,d)$ contains points $> c$.

*Case 1: $x \in \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$.* Put $w := c + 2(x - c)$. For each $n$, from $x - c < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$ we get $w - c = 2(x-c) < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$; and $c - w < 0 < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$. So $w \in \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ and $w > c$, i.e. $w \in U(c,d)$, yet $w > x$. So $x$ is not an upper bound after all — contradiction.

*Case 2: $x \notin \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$.* Then (since $c - x < 0 < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$ always) there is $n$ with $x - c \ge d\,\mathrm{ph}(n)$. The point $x' := c + d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$ satisfies $x' < x$ by Lemma 2.5(ii), and $x'$ is an upper bound of $U(c,d)$: any $z \in U(c,d)$ has $z - c < d\,\mathrm{ph}(n+1)$. So $x$ is not *least*.

Either way $x$ is not a least upper bound. $\square$

**Theorem 7.14 (Nowhere local compactness).** No neighbourhood $K$ of any $c \in \mathbf{No}$ is compact; hence $\mathbf{No}$ is not locally compact.

*Proof.* Suppose $K \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ is compact. Choose $\ell < c < r$ with $(\ell, r) \subseteq K$ and set $d := r - c > 0$. By Lemma 7.12, $U(c,d) \subseteq (c, c+d) = (c,r) \subseteq (\ell,r) \subseteq K$.

$K$ is compact hence closed, so $\overline{U(c,d)} \subseteq K$ and is compact. A nonempty compact subset of a linear order with the order topology has a least upper bound; let $x = \sup \overline{U(c,d)}$. Then $x$ is an upper bound of $U(c,d)$, and any upper bound $y$ of $U(c,d)$ bounds $\overline{U(c,d)}$ too (since $(-\infty, y]$ is closed and contains $U(c,d)$), whence $x \le y$. So $x$ is a least upper bound of $U(c,d)$, contradicting Theorem 7.13. $\square$

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 A single mechanism

Every result above is downstream of one construction: the Conway cut of an arbitrary set-indexed family (Lemma 2.2). It yields:

- density, hence continuity of addition, hence the transfer by translation (§4);
- coinitiality failure, hence the local character theorems (§3, §5);
- the "no greatest element" step making archimedean cuts clopen (§7.1);
- the existence of relative infinitesimals populating upper monads (§7.4).

So the topological pathology of $\mathbf{No}$ is not a collection of unrelated defects; it is one algebraic fact — *the surreals absorb arbitrarily large cuts* — reflected four times in the topology.

### 8.2 The dichotomy

The results assemble into a sharp dichotomy:

| Classical local tool | Status on $\mathbf{No}$ |
|---|---|
| Countable neighbourhood basis | Fails at every point |
| Small (set-sized) basis | Fails at every point |
| Metric | Does not exist |
| Sequential convergence | Trivial: only eventually-constant sequences converge |
| Discreteness | Fails: order is dense |
| Connectedness | Fails: totally separated |
| Compact neighbourhood | Fails at every point |
| Clopen basis | **Holds** at every point |

The one surviving classical structure is zero-dimensionality, and it is exactly what identifies the correct replacement for "distance": the *archimedean class*. Shrinking a neighbourhood of $c$ means passing to a smaller archimedean scale $d$, and the scales are classified — via the Conway normal form — by ordinals. There is no set of ordinals cofinal in $\mathbf{On}$; hence no set-sized basis. Every theorem in §5 and §6 is a shadow of that single sentence.

### 8.3 What replaces analysis

Analysts working over $\mathbf{No}$ must abandon sequences for **nets** or **filters**: the neighbourhood filter is a perfectly good object, it is simply not countably generated, so it must be used directly rather than through a countable cofinal chain. Convergence indexed by ordinals (transfinite sequences of length $\ge$ the cofinality of $\mathbf{On}$) is the natural substitute. Similarly, "small" quantitative statements must be reformulated relative to a fixed scale $d$, with the monad $\mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ playing the role of the $\epsilon$-ball; the resulting *relative* analysis is well-behaved because monads are clopen and partition each scale.

This is not merely a workaround: it is a structural insight. The surreal line is best regarded not as a line but as a transfinite hierarchy of nested discrete-looking scales — a "tower of granularities" indexed by $\mathbf{On}$.

### 8.4 Relation to neighbouring fields

Fields with nontrivial infinitesimals — the Levi-Civita field, Hahn series fields $\mathbb{R}((t^{\Gamma}))$, hyperreal fields — all exhibit related order-topology pathologies: their order topologies are totally disconnected and typically not locally compact. What distinguishes $\mathbf{No}$ is *universality*: it embeds all of them, and its value group of archimedean classes is a proper class. Consequently the failure here is maximal rather than merely typical: for a Hahn field with value group $\Gamma$, the character at each point is the cofinality of $\Gamma$ from below, which is an honest cardinal; for $\mathbf{No}$, it is a proper class.

## 9. Algorithms and computation

Although $\mathbf{No}$ is a proper class, all the arguments above are *effective on finite data*, and this is what makes them checkable and demonstrable. Three algorithmic kernels underlie the paper.

**(A) Cut construction.** Given a finite (or effectively presented) family $(r_i)$ of positive elements in a computable ordered-field model of a fragment of $\mathbf{No}$ — for instance the Levi-Civita-style field of finite formal sums $\sum_k a_k \varepsilon^{q_k}$ with rational exponents — produce an element strictly between $0$ and all $r_i$. Concretely: compute $q^\star := \max_i (\text{leading exponent of } r_i)$ and return $\varepsilon^{q^\star + 1}$. Complexity: $O(N)$ in the number of terms.

**(B) Basis-defeating witness.** Given a finite family of intervals $(\ell_i, r_i)$ around $0$, output the interval $(-y, y)$ with $y$ produced by (A) applied to $(r_i)$; then verify $y \in (\ell_i, r_i) \setminus (-y,y)$ for every $i$. This is the executable content of Theorem 5.1.

**(C) Monad classification.** Given $c, z, d$, decide whether $z \in \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ by comparing leading exponents: $z \in \mathrm{monad}(c,d)$ iff $z = c$ or the leading exponent of $z - c$ strictly exceeds that of $d$ (larger exponent = smaller magnitude). This decides the archimedean-class partition of Theorem 7.9 in $O(1)$ once leading exponents are known.

Together these turn the abstract theorems into finite computations one can run and inspect: every basis is defeated by an explicitly computed witness, every convergent sequence is exposed as eventually constant by an explicitly computed lower bound $y$, and the monad partition is decided by exponent arithmetic.

## 10. Future directions

The cycle established, for the order topology on $\mathbf{No}$:

1. $\mathbf{No}$ is densely ordered, hence a Hausdorff **topological additive group**, so translations are homeomorphisms.
2. **No small family of positive surreals is coinitial**, via a Conway cut over an arbitrary index type.
3. **No point has countable character**: $\mathcal{N}(c)$ is never countably generated, and no neighbourhood basis at any point is even small. The statement at $0$ transfers to every point through the translation homeomorphism.
4. $\mathbf{No}$ is **sequentially discrete** yet not discrete.
5. $\mathbf{No}$ is **totally separated and zero-dimensional** — the archimedean monads form a clopen neighbourhood basis — and it admits **no nonconstant continuous curve** from a preconnected space.
6. $\mathbf{No}$ is **nowhere locally compact**: no neighbourhood of any point is compact, because an upper monad has no least upper bound.

The picture is a rigid dichotomy: everything local about the surreal line is governed by archimedean classes, and every classical countability/compactness tool fails at every point simultaneously. Two directions push it further.

### Direction 1 — Exact character of the surreal line

**Conjecture.** For $\mathbf{No}$, the character of every point equals the cofinality of the class $\mathbf{On}$ of ordinals; formally, a family $(B_i)_{i\in I}$ of neighbourhoods of $c$ is a basis only if $I$ surjects onto a cofinal family of ordinals, so "character $= \mathbf{On}$" in the proper-class sense.

*Key insight:* coinitiality failure bounds the character from below by every small cardinal, while the archimedean-monad basis bounds it from above by the class of scales $d$, and scales are classified up to archimedean equivalence by ordinals through the $\omega^{y}$ normal form.

*Why now?* Both halves are established; what is missing is the Conway normal form ($\omega^{y}$ exponentiation), a self-contained addition.

*Test.* Establish the map $d \mapsto$ leading exponent of $d$ and prove that monads at archimedean-equivalent scales coincide; then show a basis must meet every archimedean class.

### Direction 2 — Surreal-valued Haar measure obstruction

**Conjecture.** There is no nontrivial translation-invariant, countably additive Borel measure (real- or surreal-valued) on $\mathbf{No}$ giving finite nonzero mass to some clopen monad; equivalently, $\mathbf{No}$ supports no Haar-type integration theory.

*Key insight:* nowhere local compactness kills the usual construction of Haar measure from compact-open data; the monad partition then forces any invariant measure to assign the same mass to a proper class of disjoint clopen sets inside a bounded region, which is incompatible with countable additivity and finiteness.

## 11. Conclusion

The surreal number line is an ordered field of unmatched generality, and its order topology reports that generality faithfully and brutally. At every point simultaneously, no set-indexed family of neighbourhoods is a basis; the neighbourhood filter is never countably generated; there is no metric; sequences converge only when eventually constant; the space is totally separated, admits no nonconstant continuous curve, and has no compact neighbourhood anywhere. What remains is a clopen basis of archimedean monads, and the archimedean hierarchy is the true local invariant.

The mechanism is a single one: Conway cuts of arbitrary size. That one construction gives density (hence the topological group structure and the transfer by translation) and coinitiality failure (hence the character theorems). To do analysis on $\mathbf{No}$ is to replace the sequence by the ordinal-indexed net and the epsilon by the archimedean scale.

# The Apparition–Order Bridge: Reducing a Global Arithmetic Invariant to a Local Group Order

## Abstract

The *rank of apparition* (or *entry point*) of a prime p in an integer
divisibility sequence is the least positive index at which p divides a term
of the sequence. A priori it is a global invariant: its definition ranges
over the entire infinite sequence. We prove, for the Mersenne / repunit
family a(n) = bⁿ − 1, that this global invariant collapses to a single
local computation in the residue field ZMod p: the entry point of a prime
p (with p ∤ b) equals the multiplicative order of b in (ZMod p)ˣ. We
develop the surrounding apparition calculus over arbitrary *strong
divisibility sequences* — sequences satisfying gcd(aₘ, aₙ) = a_{gcd(m,n)}
— proving the *law of apparition* (p ∣ aₙ ⟺ entryPoint(p) ∣ n) and its
set-theoretic form, the *local-to-global gluing* theorem, which exhibits
the apparition support as a principal arithmetic progression. Combining the
bridge with Fermat's little theorem yields a *Fermat-descent* corollary
(entryPoint(p) ∣ p − 1) and the congruence p ≡ 1 (mod n) for every
primitive prime divisor of bⁿ − 1. The Fibonacci specialization recovers
the structural core of Carmichael's primitive-divisor theorem. All results
are fully formalized and machine-checked; we present the mathematics and
proof sketches here in self-contained form.

**Keywords:** rank of apparition, entry point, multiplicative order, strong
divisibility sequence, Mersenne numbers, Fibonacci numbers, Zsygmondy
primitive divisors, local-to-global principle.

**MSC 2020:** 11A07, 11B39, 11B50, 11A41.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The rank of apparition

Let a : ℕ → ℕ be an integer sequence and p a prime. The **rank of
apparition** (equivalently, the **entry point**) of p in a is

> entryPoint(a, p) = min { k > 0 : p ∣ a(k) },

with the convention entryPoint(a, p) = 0 when no such k exists. The notion
goes back to Lucas (1878), who used the apparition ranks of Lucas sequences
to build primality criteria, and it is central to the theory of divisibility
sequences: the Mersenne numbers 2ⁿ − 1, the more general bⁿ − 1, the
repunits (10ⁿ − 1)/9, the Fibonacci numbers Fₙ, and Lucas sequences in
general.

The definition is unmistakably *global*. To compute entryPoint(a, p) by its
definition one must, in principle, examine every term of an infinite
sequence and locate the first occurrence of divisibility by p. The aim of
this paper is to show that, for the Mersenne family, this global search is
unnecessary: the entry point is determined by a single computation internal
to the prime, namely the multiplicative order of b modulo p.

### 1.2 Strong divisibility sequences

A sequence a : ℕ → ℕ is a **strong divisibility sequence** (SDS) if

> a(gcd(m, n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))    for all m, n ∈ ℕ.        (SDS)

Two canonical examples drive this paper:

1. **The Mersenne family** a(n) = bⁿ − 1 (b ≥ 1), where
   gcd(bᵐ − 1, bⁿ − 1) = b^{gcd(m,n)} − 1.
2. **The Fibonacci sequence** a(n) = Fₙ, where gcd(Fₘ, Fₙ) = F_{gcd(m,n)}.

Property (SDS) is exactly the hypothesis needed to make the apparition
support behave perfectly periodically (Section 3).

### 1.3 Contributions

We prove, in full formal rigor, the following chain of results.

- **(Law of apparition.)** For any SDS a with at least one apparition index
  for p, p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n.
- **(Local-to-global gluing.)** The apparition support {n > 0 : p ∣ a(n)}
  equals the principal progression {n > 0 : entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n}.
- **(Stalk reduction.)** For b ≥ 1, p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ (b mod p)ⁿ = 1 in
  ZMod p.
- **(The Apparition–Order Bridge.)** For prime p with p ∤ b,
  entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = orderOf(b mod p).
- **(Fermat descent.)** entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣ p − 1, whence every
  primitive prime divisor of bⁿ − 1 is ≡ 1 (mod n).
- **(Fibonacci gluing.)** The Fibonacci apparition support is a principal
  progression generated by the Fibonacci entry point.

The conceptual content is a *local-to-global / duality dictionary*: the
global arithmetic invariant (an entry point in the divisor lattice of
indices) is identified with a local representation-theoretic invariant (a
group order in the cyclic unit group (ZMod p)ˣ).

## 2. The Entry-Point Calculus

We first build the apparition calculus over an arbitrary SDS. Throughout,
a : ℕ → ℕ.

### 2.1 Two structural lemmas

**Lemma 2.1 (divisibility along index divisibility).** If a is an SDS and
m ∣ n then a(m) ∣ a(n).

*Proof.* From m ∣ n we get gcd(m, n) = m, so (SDS) gives
a(m) = a(gcd(m, n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n)), and gcd(a(m), a(n)) ∣ a(n). ∎

**Lemma 2.2 (descent to a gcd index).** If a is an SDS and p ∣ a(m),
p ∣ a(n), then p ∣ a(gcd(m, n)).

*Proof.* By (SDS), a(gcd(m, n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n)); a common divisor of
a(m) and a(n) divides their gcd. ∎

### 2.2 The entry point and its defining properties

**Definition 2.3.** The **entry point** of p in a is

> entryPoint(a, p) = (Nat.find of) min{ k > 0 : p ∣ a(k) } if it exists,
> else 0.

When an apparition index exists, the entry point e := entryPoint(a, p)
satisfies, directly from the minimization:

- (positivity) e > 0;
- (witness) p ∣ a(e);
- (minimality) for 0 < m < e, p ∤ a(m).

### 2.3 The law of apparition

**Theorem 2.4 (law of apparition).** Let a be an SDS and suppose
∃ k > 0 with p ∣ a(k). Then for all n,

> p ∣ a(n)  ⟺  entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n.

*Proof.* Write e = entryPoint(a, p); by Section 2.2, e > 0 and p ∣ a(e).

(⟸) If e ∣ n then a(e) ∣ a(n) by Lemma 2.1, and p ∣ a(e) ∣ a(n).

(⟹) Suppose p ∣ a(n) but, for contradiction, e ∤ n. Then
g := gcd(e, n) is a proper divisor of e, so g < e (and g > 0 since
e > 0). By Lemma 2.2, p ∣ a(g). But g is a positive index strictly below
the entry point e with p ∣ a(g), contradicting minimality. Hence e ∣ n. ∎

The forward direction is where (SDS) is essential: the descent to the gcd
index produces a *smaller* apparition index unless e already divides n.

## 3. Local-to-Global Gluing

The law of apparition, read as an equality of sets, says the apparition
support is a single arithmetic progression.

**Theorem 3.1 (local-to-global gluing).** Let a be an SDS with an
apparition index for p. Then

> { n : 0 < n ∧ p ∣ a(n) } = { n : 0 < n ∧ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n }.

*Proof.* Pointwise: for each n with n > 0, Theorem 2.4 gives
p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n; take the conjunction with 0 < n and
apply set extensionality. ∎

**Sheaf interpretation.** Regard n ↦ { p prime : p ∣ a(n) } as a presheaf
of "supports" over the additive index semigroup (ℕ, +). Theorem 3.1 says
the stalk at p — the collection of all indices witnessing p — is the
*principal* sub-semigroup generated by entryPoint(a, p). There is no
higher gluing data: the global section is reconstructed from the single
generator. This is the precise sense in which the apparition data is
"local-to-global trivial," generated everywhere by one number.

## 4. The Stalk Reduction

We now specialize to a(n) = bⁿ − 1 and pass to the residue field.

**Theorem 4.1 (stalk reduction).** Let p be prime and b ≥ 1. Then for all
n,

> p ∣ bⁿ − 1  ⟺  (b mod p)ⁿ = 1   in ZMod p.

*Proof.* Since b ≥ 1, also bⁿ ≥ 1, so bⁿ − 1 is a genuine (non-truncated)
natural subtraction. Then

> p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ bⁿ ≡ 1 (mod p)        (since bⁿ ≥ 1)
>          ⟺ (bⁿ mod p) = (1 mod p)  in ZMod p
>          ⟺ (b mod p)ⁿ = 1,

using the congruence-to-residue dictionary and the multiplicativity of the
natural-cast ring homomorphism ℕ → ZMod p. ∎

The hypothesis b ≥ 1 is exactly what prevents the ℕ-truncation pathology
bⁿ − 1 = 0 (which occurs when b = 0). In the bridge below it comes for free
from p ∤ b.

**Lemma 4.2.** a(n) = bⁿ − 1 is a strong divisibility sequence.

*Proof.* gcd(bᵐ − 1, bⁿ − 1) = b^{gcd(m,n)} − 1, the standard identity for
exponential differences. ∎

## 5. The Apparition–Order Bridge

**Theorem 5.1 (Apparition–Order Bridge).** Let p be prime and b a natural
number with p ∤ b. Then

> entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = orderOf(b mod p),

the multiplicative order of b in the unit group (ZMod p)ˣ.

*Proof.* Set β = (b mod p) ∈ ZMod p and o = orderOf(β).

*Step 1 (β is a unit).* From p ∤ b we get β ≠ 0 in the field ZMod p, so β
is invertible; also b ≥ 1 (else b = 0 and p ∣ b). By Fermat's little
theorem β^{p−1} = 1, and since p ≥ 2 we have p − 1 > 0. Hence β has finite
order and o > 0.

*Step 2 (existence of an apparition index).* By Theorem 4.1 at n = o,
p ∣ b^o − 1 ⟺ β^o = 1, and β^o = 1 holds by definition of the order. So o
is a positive apparition index; in particular an apparition index for p
exists, and the entry-point calculus of Sections 2–3 applies.

*Step 3 (the two divisor relations coincide).* For every n, chaining the
law of apparition (Theorem 2.4, using Lemma 4.2 and Step 2), the stalk
reduction (Theorem 4.1), and the standard characterization of order
(orderOf(β) ∣ n ⟺ βⁿ = 1):

> entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣ n
>   ⟺ p ∣ bⁿ − 1
>   ⟺ βⁿ = 1
>   ⟺ o ∣ n.

Thus entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) and o have exactly the same set of multiples.

*Step 4 (antisymmetry).* Two naturals each dividing the other are equal.
Taking n = o gives entryPoint ∣ o; taking n = entryPoint gives o ∣
entryPoint. Hence entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = o. ∎

This is the central theorem: the global rank-of-apparition invariant is
*represented* by the single local group order in the cyclic stalk
⟨β⟩ ≤ (ZMod p)ˣ.

## 6. Fermat Descent and Primitive Divisors

**Theorem 6.1 (Fermat descent).** For prime p with p ∤ b,

> entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣ p − 1.

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.1 the entry point equals orderOf(β) with β = b mod p.
Fermat's little theorem gives β^{p−1} = 1, and the order divides any
exponent killing β, so orderOf(β) ∣ p − 1. ∎

**Corollary 6.2 (primitive divisors are ≡ 1 mod n).** A prime p is a
*primitive divisor* of bⁿ − 1 when n = entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) (i.e. bⁿ − 1
is the first term p divides). For such p,

> n ∣ p − 1,   hence   p ≡ 1 (mod n)   and   p ≥ n + 1.

*Proof.* Primitivity means entryPoint = n; Theorem 6.1 gives entryPoint ∣
p − 1; substitute. ∎

Corollary 6.2 is the elementary engine behind the **Bang–Zsygmondy
theorem**: for b ≥ 2, every bⁿ − 1 has a primitive prime divisor except
for the finite, explicit exceptional set (n = 1 in general; n = 6 for
b = 2; and n = 2 when b + 1 is a power of 2). The congruence p ≡ 1 (mod n)
constrains where such primes can live and underlies efficient search
heuristics for them.

## 7. The Fibonacci Specialization

**Lemma 7.1.** The Fibonacci sequence n ↦ Fₙ is a strong divisibility
sequence: gcd(Fₘ, Fₙ) = F_{gcd(m,n)}.

**Theorem 7.2 (Fibonacci gluing).** For a prime p with an apparition index
in the Fibonacci sequence,

> { n : 0 < n ∧ p ∣ Fₙ } = { n : 0 < n ∧ entryPoint(F, p) ∣ n }.

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 3.1 to the SDS of Lemma 7.1. ∎

The Fibonacci entry point is classically the **Pisano-type rank** of p in
F; e.g. entryPoint(F, 2) = 3, entryPoint(F, 11) = 10. Theorem 7.2 is the
structural backbone of **Carmichael's primitive-divisor theorem** (1913):
every Fₙ with n ∉ {1, 2, 6, 12} has a prime factor appearing at no smaller
index. The gluing theorem provides exactly the restriction maps (n ↦ d for
d ∣ n) on which such primitive-divisor arguments are built.

While the Fibonacci entry point does not literally equal an order in
(ZMod p)ˣ (the relevant stalk is two-dimensional — the order of the
companion matrix Q = [[1,1],[1,0]] in GL₂(ZMod p), equivalently the order
of the golden ratio in ZMod p[x]/(x² − x − 1)), the index-side calculus
(Sections 2–3) is identical, since it depends only on (SDS).

## 8. Algorithms

The bridge is not only conceptual; it converts an unbounded search into a
bounded one. We record the algorithmic content.

### 8.1 Entry point by the bridge

**Naïve entry point** computes entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) by scanning n =
1, 2, 3, … and testing p ∣ bⁿ − 1, with no a-priori bound. By Theorem 5.1
this is exactly the order of b modulo p, computable as follows.

**Order via Fermat descent.** Since orderOf(b mod p) ∣ p − 1
(Theorem 6.1), the order is found by factoring p − 1 = ∏ qᵢ^{eᵢ} and, for
each prime q ∣ p − 1, repeatedly dividing the candidate exponent by q while
b^{(p−1)/q} ≡ 1 (mod p) still holds. This costs O(log p) modular
exponentiations once p − 1 is factored, versus the unbounded naïve scan.
The two algorithms are *provably* equal in output by the bridge.

### 8.2 Apparition support enumeration

By Theorem 3.1, enumerating { n ≤ N : p ∣ a(n) } reduces to listing the
multiples of e = entryPoint(a, p) up to N: e, 2e, …, ⌊N/e⌋·e. The cost
drops from N divisibility tests to ⌊N/e⌋ trivial multiplications.

### 8.3 Primitive-divisor membership

By Corollary 6.2 a prime p can be a primitive divisor of bⁿ − 1 only if
p ≡ 1 (mod n). A primitive-divisor search may therefore restrict trial
primes to the arithmetic progression 1, 1 + n, 1 + 2n, …, a density-1/φ(n)
reduction of the search space.

## 9. Discussion

The Apparition–Order Bridge is a compact realization of the **local-to-
global principle**. The object of study, entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p), is defined
by an infinite minimization over the divisor lattice of indices; the
theorem identifies it with orderOf(b mod p), an invariant of a single
finite cyclic group. Conceptually, this is a *duality dictionary*:

> divisibility data on indices ⟷ representation-theoretic order data.

The "support sheaf" n ↦ {p : p ∣ a(n)} over (ℕ, +) has, at each prime
stalk, trivial gluing obstruction: its global sections are principal,
generated by the stalk order. The phenomenon is structurally identical to
the cohomological vanishing that makes a line bundle globally generated by
a single section.

The bridge also clarifies *why* classical apparition facts hold. The
periodicity of apparition (Theorem 3.1) is the periodicity of a cyclic
group; Fermat descent (Theorem 6.1) is Lagrange's theorem in (ZMod p)ˣ; the
congruence p ≡ 1 (mod n) for primitive primes (Corollary 6.2) is the
order-divides-group-order inequality. Each "number-theoretic" statement is
the shadow of an elementary group fact crossing the bridge.

## 10. Future Work

1. **The rank-2 stalk for Fibonacci.** Conjecture: for p ∤ 5,
   entryPoint(F, p) equals the order of the companion matrix
   Q = [[1,1],[1,0]] in GL₂(ZMod p), equivalently the order of the golden
   ratio in ZMod p[x]/(x² − x − 1). The index-side calculus is already in
   place; only the stalk identification needs a 2×2 analogue of Theorem 4.1.

2. **CRT gluing to composite moduli.** Conjecture: for m coprime to b,
   entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, m) = lcm over prime powers q ∥ m of
   entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, q) = orderOf(b mod q), via the ring isomorphism
   ZMod m ≅ ∏ ZMod qᵢ. Gluing principal progressions under intersection is
   precisely the lcm.

3. **A cohomological obstruction for Zsygmondy exceptions.** Conjecture: the
   finite exceptional sets of the primitive-divisor theorems are the support
   of a degree-1 obstruction class in a Čech-style H¹ of the apparition
   presheaf, vanishing exactly when some prime has maximal stalk order
   (= n).

4. **Quantitative primitive divisors.** Corollary 6.2 gives n ∣ p − 1 for
   every primitive divisor; the quantitative floor — that the smallest
   primitive divisor of bⁿ − 1 is O(n log n) for fixed b — would follow from
   Linnik-type bounds on the least prime in the progression 1 (mod n).

5. **Closing the Fibonacci–Carmichael tail.** The asymptotic case (composite
   n large) of the Fibonacci primitive-divisor theorem should follow from
   the cyclotomic factorization Fₙ = ∏_{d∣n} Φ_d combined with the growth
   bound Φₙ ≥ φ^{ϕ(n)/2}, reducing the global existence statement to a local
   size inequality at the single ramified prime.

---

## 11. Conclusion

We have shown that the rank of apparition of a prime in the Mersenne family
bⁿ − 1 — a global arithmetic invariant defined by minimization over an
infinite sequence — equals the multiplicative order of b in the residue
field ZMod p, a single local group computation. Around this bridge we built
the full apparition calculus over strong divisibility sequences (law of
apparition, principal-progression gluing), specialized the stalk reduction
to the residue field, and harvested Fermat descent and the primitive-divisor
congruence p ≡ 1 (mod n) as immediate corollaries, with the Fibonacci case
tying back to Carmichael's theorem. The work is a small but exact instance
of the local-to-global philosophy: a global pattern, fully determined by one
local heartbeat.

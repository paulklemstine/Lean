# Left-Ore Obstruction and Noncommutative Collision Resistance in the Berggren Free Semigroup

## Abstract

We prove a sharp classification of common left multiples in the Berggren free semigroup — the rank-3 free semigroup generated by the three linear transformations that enumerate all primitive Pythagorean triples. Our main theorem states that two elements possess a common left multiple if and only if they are comparable under left-divisibility (equivalently, one is a suffix of the other in the canonical word representation). This completely classifies the failure of the left Ore condition and yields a strong noncommutative collision-resistance property: incomparable tree prefixes can never produce equal evaluations, regardless of suffixes appended. All results are formalized and machine-verified in Lean 4 using Mathlib.

**Keywords:** Free semigroup, Ore condition, collision resistance, Berggren tree, Pythagorean triples, formal verification

---

## 1. Introduction

The Berggren tree, discovered independently by Berggren (1934) and Barning (1963), is a ternary tree structure that enumerates all primitive Pythagorean triples exactly once. Starting from the root triple (3, 4, 5), three linear transformations — traditionally denoted A, B, C — generate all primitive Pythagorean triples as the vertices of an infinite ternary tree.

The algebraic structure behind this enumeration is a **free semigroup of rank 3**: the three generators satisfy no nontrivial relations. This freeness was established computationally through a discriminant classifier argument and formalized in Lean 4 in the companion file `Cryptography.Freeness`.

In this paper, we push the algebraic theory further by proving a **sharp left-Ore obstruction theorem**. The classical Ore condition (left version) for a semigroup S asks: for any a, b ∈ S, do there exist x, y ∈ S with xa = yb? In a commutative semigroup (or more generally, in an Ore domain), this always holds. In the Berggren free semigroup, it sharply fails: common left multiples exist for a pair (a, b) if and only if the elements are comparable in the left-divisibility partial order.

### 1.1 Main Results

**Theorem 1 (Left-Ore Obstruction).** *Let S be the free semigroup on generators {A, B, C}. For elements a, b ∈ S:*

$$\exists x, y \in S : x \cdot a = y \cdot b \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad a \leq_L b \;\lor\; b \leq_L a$$

*where a ≤_L b means b = x · a for some x (i.e., a is a suffix of b in the word representation).*

**Theorem 2 (Collision Exclusion).** *Let evalTriple : S → ℤ³ be the evaluation map into Pythagorean triples. If words u and v are not prefix-comparable (neither is a prefix of the other), then for all suffixes α, β:*

$$\text{evalTriple}(u \cdot \alpha) \neq \text{evalTriple}(v \cdot \beta)$$

**Theorem 3 (Generator Ore Obstruction).** *For distinct generators g ≠ h ∈ {A, B, C}, there is no common left multiple of [g] and [h].*

All theorems are formalized in Lean 4 with complete machine-verified proofs.

## 2. Mathematical Framework

### 2.1 The Berggren Generators

The three Berggren matrices act on triples (a, b, c) ∈ ℤ³:

- **Generator A:** (a, b, c) ↦ (a − 2b + 2c, 2a − b + 2c, 2a − 2b + 3c)
- **Generator B:** (a, b, c) ↦ (a + 2b + 2c, 2a + b + 2c, 2a + 2b + 3c)  
- **Generator C:** (a, b, c) ↦ (−a + 2b + 2c, −2a + b + 2c, −2a + 2b + 3c)

Starting from the root (3, 4, 5), every primitive Pythagorean triple appears exactly once in the resulting tree.

### 2.2 Free Semigroup Structure

A **Berggren word** is a finite list of generators: w = [g₁, g₂, …, gₙ] where each gᵢ ∈ {A, B, C}. The evaluation map sends a word to the triple obtained by applying the generators from right to left:

evalTriple([g₁, g₂, …, gₙ]) = g₁(g₂(…(gₙ(3, 4, 5))…))

The fundamental theorem (proved in `Cryptography.Freeness`) is:

**Theorem (Freeness).** *The evaluation map evalTriple is injective: distinct words produce distinct triples.*

The proof uses a discriminant classifier: for any triple in the image of a generator applied to a positive Pythagorean triple, the signs of the linear forms x = a + 2b − 2c and y = 2a + b − 2c uniquely determine which generator was applied.

### 2.3 Left-Divisibility

For words a, b in the free semigroup (with concatenation as multiplication):

**Definition.** a ≤_L b (a left-divides b) iff ∃ x : b = x ++ a.

This means a is a suffix of b. The left-divisibility relation is:
- **Reflexive:** a ≤_L a (take x = [])
- **Transitive:** a ≤_L b and b ≤_L c imply a ≤_L c
- **Antisymmetric:** a ≤_L b and b ≤_L a imply a = b (by length argument)

Hence ≤_L is a partial order on BergWord.

### 2.4 Common Left Multiples

**Definition.** HasCommonLeftMultiple(a, b) iff ∃ x y : x ++ a = y ++ b.

## 3. Proof of the Left-Ore Obstruction

### 3.1 The Combinatorial Core

The proof rests on a single, elegant combinatorial fact about lists:

**Lemma (Append Decomposition).** *If u ++ a = v ++ b, then either:*
1. *∃ t : v = u ++ t and a = t ++ b, or*
2. *∃ t : u = v ++ t and b = t ++ a.*

This is `List.append_eq_append_iff` in Mathlib. It says that when two concatenations are equal, the two "left halves" must be prefix-comparable, and the relationship between the right halves is determined by the bridging segment t.

### 3.2 Forward Direction (The Nontrivial Implication)

Suppose HasCommonLeftMultiple(a, b), i.e., ∃ x y with x ++ a = y ++ b.

By the Append Decomposition Lemma:
- **Case 1:** v = u ++ t and a = t ++ b. Then b is a suffix of a, so b ≤_L a. ✓
- **Case 2:** u = v ++ t and b = t ++ a. Then a is a suffix of b, so a ≤_L b. ✓

In both cases, a and b are ≤_L-comparable.

### 3.3 Reverse Direction

If a ≤_L b, say b = x ++ a, then x ++ a = [] ++ b (taking y = []), witnessing HasCommonLeftMultiple(a, b). Similarly for b ≤_L a.

### 3.4 The Collision Exclusion

If u and v are not prefix-comparable, suppose for contradiction that evalTriple(u ++ α) = evalTriple(v ++ β). By injectivity of evalTriple, u ++ α = v ++ β. By the Append Decomposition, u <+: v or v <+: u — contradiction.

## 4. Concrete Ore Obstruction

### 4.1 Generator-Level Result

For distinct generators g ≠ h, we prove ¬HasCommonLeftMultiple([g], [h]).

By the main theorem, this reduces to showing neither [g] ≤_L [h] nor [h] ≤_L [g]. Since LeftDivides([g], [h]) means [h] = x ++ [g] for some x, and both lists have length 1, we must have x = [] and hence g = h — contradicting g ≠ h.

### 4.2 Specific Instances

| Pair | Common Left Multiple? | Reason |
|------|----------------------|--------|
| (A, A) | Yes | Reflexivity: A ≤_L A |
| (A, B) | **No** | Incomparable generators |
| (A, C) | **No** | Incomparable generators |
| (B, C) | **No** | Incomparable generators |
| (A, BA) | Yes | A is a suffix of BA |
| (AB, CB) | **No** | Neither is a suffix of the other |

## 5. Formalization in Lean 4

All results are formalized in the file `Cryptography/SPB/BerggrenLeftOreObstruction.lean`, building on the freeness proof in `Cryptography/Freeness.lean`. The formalization uses Lean 4.28.0 with Mathlib.

### 5.1 Key Definitions

```lean
def LeftDivides (a b : BergWord) : Prop := ∃ x : BergWord, b = x ++ a
def HasCommonLeftMultiple (a b : BergWord) : Prop := ∃ x y : BergWord, x ++ a = y ++ b
```

### 5.2 Key Theorems

```lean
theorem hasCommonLeftMultiple_iff_comparable_leftDivides (a b : BergWord) :
    HasCommonLeftMultiple a b ↔ a ≤L b ∨ b ≤L a

theorem no_prefix_collision_of_incomparable
    {u v α β : BergWord} (huv : ¬ u <+: v ∧ ¬ v <+: u) :
    evalTriple (u ++ α) ≠ evalTriple (v ++ β)

theorem gen_no_common_left_multiple {g h : BergGen} (hgh : g ≠ h) :
    ¬ HasCommonLeftMultiple [g] [h]
```

### 5.3 Proof Architecture

The proof has a clean layered structure:

1. **Layer 1 (Combinatorics):** `suffix_comparison_of_append_eq_append` — pure list fact from Mathlib's `List.append_eq_append_iff`
2. **Layer 2 (Free Semigroup):** `eq_mul_imp_comparable_leftDivides` — translates list decomposition to divisibility comparability
3. **Layer 3 (Evaluation):** `evalTriple_concat_eq_implies_prefix` — uses injectivity of `evalTriple` to lift to the triple level
4. **Layer 4 (Security):** `no_prefix_collision_of_incomparable` — packages the result as a collision-resistance theorem

The axioms used are exactly `propext`, `Classical.choice`, and `Quot.sound` — all standard foundational axioms.

## 6. Applications

### 6.1 SPB Diffie-Hellman Protocol Security

In the Semigroup-based Public-key Berggren (SPB) Diffie-Hellman protocol, each party's secret key is a path in the Berggren tree — equivalently, a word in the free semigroup. The public key is obtained by evaluating this word, yielding a Pythagorean triple (or, after reduction modulo a prime p, an element of a matrix group over 𝔽_p).

The collision resistance theorem provides a structural security guarantee: **an adversary who observes two public keys corresponding to incomparable tree paths cannot find any pair of suffixes that would make the evaluations collide.** This is stronger than computational collision resistance — it is an unconditional, information-theoretic guarantee derived from the algebraic structure.

### 6.2 Transcript Integrity

In multi-round protocols, each round appends a generator to the running transcript. The collision exclusion theorem ensures that **transcripts that diverge at any point can never reconverge.** This prevents transcript substitution attacks where an adversary tries to replace one transcript prefix with a different one while maintaining consistency with the observed public outputs.

### 6.3 Key Derivation Trees

The left-divisibility partial order provides a natural key derivation hierarchy. A master key (long word) has all of its suffixes as "parent keys," and the Ore obstruction ensures that keys in different branches of the hierarchy can never produce colliding derived values.

### 6.4 Comparison with RSA/DLP-Based Schemes

| Property | RSA/DLP | SPB Berggren |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| Collision resistance | Computational (hash function) | Algebraic (free semigroup structure) |
| Basis | Number-theoretic hardness | Tree geometry + freeness |
| Quantum resistance | Vulnerable (Shor) | Structure-preserving (reducible to SLP in matrices) |
| Transcript integrity | Requires MAC/signature | Built into algebraic structure |

## 7. Discussion: Why Trees Make Good Locks

*For the general reader*

Imagine you're designing a combination lock, but instead of a single sequence of numbers, the lock opens based on a path through a maze. The maze is a tree — at each junction, you must choose one of three corridors (A, B, or C). Your "key" is the sequence of choices you make.

The Berggren tree is exactly such a maze, but with a remarkable mathematical property: **every path leads to a unique destination** (a primitive Pythagorean triple — a right triangle with integer sides that share no common factor). The triple (3, 4, 5) is at the entrance. Going left (A) leads to (5, 12, 13). Going right (C) leads to (15, 8, 17). Going straight (B) leads to (21, 20, 29). And so on, forever, with no two paths ever arriving at the same triangle.

Our theorem says something even stronger. Suppose two people, Alice and Bob, both start at the entrance and begin making choices. If at any point they take different corridors — say Alice goes left (A) while Bob goes right (B) — then **no matter what choices they make afterward, they can never end up at the same triangle.** It doesn't matter if Alice goes ABCCBA and Bob goes BCAACB. Once they diverge, they can never reconverge.

This is exactly the kind of guarantee you want for a cryptographic protocol. It's not just that it's *hard* to find a collision — it's that collisions are *mathematically impossible* for divergent paths. The security comes not from the difficulty of a computation, but from the rigid geometry of the tree itself.

What makes this particularly interesting is that it's a **noncommutative** phenomenon. In ordinary arithmetic, if you multiply numbers, the order doesn't matter: 3 × 5 = 5 × 3. In the Berggren semigroup, the order matters enormously: following A then B gives a completely different triple than following B then A. This noncommutativity is what makes the collision resistance possible — and it's a genuinely different source of cryptographic strength than the number-theoretic assumptions (like the difficulty of factoring) that underlie most current cryptography.

### Historical Context

The Berggren tree itself dates to 1934, when the Swedish mathematician B. Berggren showed that every primitive Pythagorean triple can be generated from (3, 4, 5) by three specific linear transformations. The same structure was independently rediscovered by Barning in 1963 and has since been studied extensively in number theory.

The observation that this tree structure has cryptographic applications is more recent. The key insight is that the *freeness* of the semigroup (proved in our companion formalization) means that the tree faithfully represents the algebra — there are no hidden shortcuts or collapses. Our left-Ore obstruction theorem converts this algebraic fact into a precise security statement.

### Future Directions

Several natural questions arise:

1. **Quantitative hardness.** While our theorem gives *unconditional* collision resistance for the word-level structure, the actual cryptographic protocol operates modulo a prime p. How does the reduction modulo p interact with the tree structure?

2. **Right-Ore condition.** Our theorem addresses the *left* Ore condition. The analogous question for the right side — whether there exist x, y with a·x = b·y — involves a different algebraic structure and may yield additional security invariants.

3. **Generalizations.** The Berggren tree is one of many tree structures in number theory (Stern-Brocot tree, Calkin-Wilf tree, etc.). Which of these yield similar Ore obstructions, and which have useful cryptographic properties?

4. **Formal verification of the full protocol.** Our results formalize the algebraic foundation. Extending the formalization to the full SPB Diffie-Hellman protocol — including modular reduction, key exchange, and security proofs — is an important goal.

## 8. Conclusion

We have proved a complete classification of common left multiples in the Berggren free semigroup: they exist if and only if the elements are comparable under left-divisibility. This result is mathematically clean (a biconditional classification), cryptographically useful (collision resistance for transcript prefixes), and rigorously verified (machine-checked in Lean 4).

The proof strategy — combining Mathlib's `List.append_eq_append_iff` with the injectivity of the Berggren evaluation map — demonstrates the power of layered formalization: generic combinatorial facts compose cleanly with domain-specific injectivity results to yield security theorems.

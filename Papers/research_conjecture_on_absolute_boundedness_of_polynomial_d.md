# Structural Constraints on Polynomial Diophantine Tuples with a $k$-th Power Condition

## Abstract

A *Diophantine tuple* in the classical sense is a set of integers such that the
product of any two of them, increased by one, is a perfect square. We study a
broad polynomial generalization: given a commutative ring $R$, an exponent
$k \ge 2$, and a shift $n \in R$, a set $A \subseteq R$ has the **$k$-th power
Diophantine property $D_k(n)$** when $ab + n$ is a perfect $k$-th power in $R$ for
every pair of distinct $a, b \in A$. Over the polynomial ring $F[x]$, with $F$ an
algebraically closed field of characteristic zero and $n \neq 0$, it is
conjectured that every such set has at most six members, unless $n$ is a perfect
$k$-th power and $A$ is contained in the principal ideal generated by a $k$-th
root of $n$. We isolate and prove three structural results that delimit this
conjecture and demonstrate that each of its hypotheses is load-bearing. First,
over any algebraically closed field, *every* set of constants is a $D_k(n)$ set,
so no absolute size bound survives among constants; the conjecture must genuinely
use the degree filtration of $F[x]$. Second, a **degree rigidity law**: if all
members of a $D_k(n)$ set share a common degree $d \ge 1$ and $\deg n < 2d$, then
$k \mid 2d$; in particular there is no pair of distinct degree-one polynomials
satisfying $D_3(n)$ with $\deg n < 2$. Third, a **zero-extension
characterization**: a $D_k(n)$ set can be extended by the element $0$ if and only
if $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power, matching exactly the exceptional clause of the
conjecture. We also explain why a natural common-factor heuristic is false and
must be replaced by a coprimality statement. We accompany the theory with
numerical demonstrations and algorithms.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Historical background

The study of Diophantine tuples begins with Diophantus of Alexandria, who found
four rationals — $\frac{1}{16}, \frac{33}{16}, \frac{17}{4}, \frac{105}{16}$ —
with the property that the product of any two, increased by one, is a perfect
square. Fermat produced the integer quadruple $\{1, 3, 8, 120\}$ with the same
property. A central question, open for decades, asked whether a *Diophantine
quintuple* — five positive integers all of whose pairwise products plus one are
perfect squares — could exist. It was proved in 2019 that no such quintuple
exists; the maximal size in the integer, square, shift-by-one setting is four.

Replacing $\mathbb{Z}$ by a polynomial ring $F[x]$, the square by an arbitrary
$k$-th power, and the shift $1$ by an arbitrary polynomial $n$ yields a far more
flexible playground in which the mechanisms responsible for boundedness become
visible. This paper contributes a structural analysis of the polynomial problem.

### 1.2 The central object and conjecture

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring and, in the sharpest results, $R = F[x]$
for a field $F$.

> **Definition 1.1 ($k$-th power Diophantine set).** Let $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and
> $n \in R$. A set $A \subseteq R$ has the **$k$-th power Diophantine property
> $D_k(n)$**, written $\mathrm{Dio}_k(n)(A)$, if for all distinct $a, b \in A$
> there exists $c \in R$ with
> $$a\,b + n = c^{\,k}.$$

The research target is the following.

> **Conjecture 1.2 (Absolute boundedness).** Let $F$ be an algebraically closed
> field of characteristic zero, $k \ge 2$, and $n \in F[x]$ nonzero. Then any
> $D_k(n)$ set $A \subseteq F[x]$ satisfies $|A| \le 6$, unless $n$ is a perfect
> $k$-th power and $A$ is contained in the principal ideal $(c)$, where $c^k = n$.

We do not resolve Conjecture 1.2. Instead we prove three theorems (Sections 3–5)
that (a) explain why each hypothesis is necessary, (b) provide genuine
nonexistence and unboundedness results, and (c) localize the remaining difficulty
to nonconstant tuples of mixed degrees.

### 1.3 Summary of contributions

1. **Constant unboundedness (Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.2).** Over an
   algebraically closed field, every set of constants is $D_k(n)$; hence $D_k(n)$
   sets of arbitrarily large cardinality exist. The degree hypothesis in
   Conjecture 1.2 is therefore indispensable.
2. **Degree rigidity (Theorem 4.1).** If every element of a $D_k(n)$ set has a
   common degree $d \ge 1$ and $\deg n < 2d$, then $k \mid 2d$. Corollary 4.3
   deduces the nonexistence of degree-one cubic Diophantine pairs.
3. **Zero-extension characterization (Theorem 5.1 and its converse).** A $D_k(n)$
   set can be extended by $0$ iff $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power; if a set contains
   $0$ and a nonzero element while $n$ is not a perfect $k$-th power, no such set
   exists (Corollary 5.3).
4. **A false heuristic corrected (Section 6).** The guess "a common factor of two
   members divides $n$" is false; the correct principle is a coprimality
   statement.

## 2. Preliminaries

We work with the polynomial ring $R = F[x]$ where needed. Two elementary facts
about the degree function underpin everything.

- **(P1) Degree of a product.** For nonzero $p, q \in F[x]$ over an integral
  domain, $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$.
- **(P2) Degree of a power.** For any $c \in F[x]$ and $k \ge 1$, $\deg(c^k) = k
  \cdot \deg c$. In particular, the degree of a perfect $k$-th power is a multiple
  of $k$.

A basic closure property, used repeatedly, is monotonicity.

> **Lemma 2.1 (Monotonicity).** If $A \subseteq B$ and $B$ is a $D_k(n)$ set, then
> $A$ is a $D_k(n)$ set.
>
> *Proof.* Distinct $a, b \in A$ are distinct elements of $B$, so the required
> $c$ with $ab + n = c^k$ exists by hypothesis. $\qquad\blacksquare$

## 3. Constants carry no size bound

The first structural fact shows that the boundedness phenomenon cannot come from
the ring axioms alone: it must use degrees.

> **Theorem 3.1 (Constants are always Diophantine).** Let $F$ be an algebraically
> closed field, $k \ge 1$, and $n \in F$. Then every set $A \subseteq F$ of
> constants has the property $D_k(n)$.
>
> *Proof.* Let $a, b \in A$ be distinct. The element $ab + n$ lies in $F$. Since
> $F$ is algebraically closed, the polynomial $T^k - (ab + n) \in F[T]$ has a root
> $c \in F$, i.e. $c^k = ab + n$. Thus $ab + n$ is a perfect $k$-th power, as
> required. $\qquad\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 3.2 (Arbitrarily large Diophantine sets).** Over an algebraically
> closed field $F$ (in particular over $\mathbb{C}$), for every $N \in \mathbb{N}$
> there is a finite $D_k(n)$ set $s \subseteq F$ with $|s| > N$. In the classical
> normalization $k = 2$, $n = 1$ over $\mathbb{C}$, Diophantine tuples of every
> cardinality exist.
>
> *Proof.* An infinite field has finite subsets of every cardinality; take any
> subset $s$ of size $N + 1$. By Theorem 3.1 it is $D_k(n)$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** Corollary 3.2 shows that Conjecture 1.2 is false without the
degree/polynomial hypothesis: the bound $6$ is genuinely a statement about
nonconstant polynomials. Constants, having degree $0$, are the unique source of
unboundedness, and any correct formulation must exclude them.

## 4. Degree rigidity: a divisibility obstruction

We now assume genuine polynomials and extract the arithmetic constraint that
degrees impose. The key input is (P2): a perfect $k$-th power has degree divisible
by $k$.

> **Theorem 4.1 (Degree rigidity / same-degree divisibility).** Let $F$ be a field
> and $A \subseteq F[x]$ a $D_k(n)$ set. Suppose there is $d \ge 1$ such that every
> $p \in A$ has $\deg p = d$, and suppose $\deg n < 2d$. Then for any two distinct
> $a, b \in A$,
> $$k \mid 2d.$$
>
> *Proof.* Fix distinct $a, b \in A$. Since $\deg a = \deg b = d \ge 1$, both are
> nonzero, and by (P1)
> $$\deg(ab) = \deg a + \deg b = 2d.$$
> Because $\deg n < 2d = \deg(ab)$, adding $n$ does not affect the leading term:
> $$\deg(ab + n) = 2d.$$
> By the $D_k(n)$ property there is $c \in F[x]$ with $ab + n = c^k$; note $c \ne
> 0$ since $ab + n \ne 0$. By (P2),
> $$2d = \deg(ab + n) = \deg(c^k) = k \cdot \deg c,$$
> so $k \mid 2d$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Necessity of the hypotheses.** Both hypotheses are load-bearing. If $d = 0$ were
allowed, $a$ could vanish or be constant and (P1) would fail; indeed Theorem 3.1
shows constants place no constraint at all. If $\deg n \ge 2d$, the top-degree
terms of $ab$ and $n$ could cancel, breaking $\deg(ab+n) = 2d$ and hence the
conclusion.

> **Corollary 4.2 (Cubes force a divisible degree).** For $k = 3$, a same-degree
> $D_3(n)$ set with $\deg n < 2d$ can only exist when $3 \mid d$: since $3 \mid 2d$
> and $\gcd(3, 2) = 1$, we get $3 \mid d$.

> **Corollary 4.3 (No degree-one cubic Diophantine pair).** Over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$
> (indeed over any field), there is no pair of distinct degree-one polynomials
> $a \ne b$ lying in a $D_3(n)$ set with $\deg n < 2$.
>
> *Proof.* Apply Theorem 4.1 with $d = 1$. The two members have common degree $1$,
> and $\deg n < 2 = 2d$, so the theorem yields $3 \mid 2 \cdot 1 = 2$. But $3
> \nmid 2$, a contradiction. Hence no such pair exists. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Interpretation.** The exponent $k$ enters the entire theory through the single
identity (P2). Corollary 4.3 is a clean, nonvacuous impossibility: two lines can
never have their shifted product be a perfect cube for a small shift. It also
corrects the naive "the degrees must match" heuristic — matching alone is not the
constraint; the constraint is the modular condition $k \mid 2d$.

## 5. The zero polynomial and the perfect-power exception

The final structural result explains the exception clause of Conjecture 1.2. It
is governed entirely by the element $0$.

> **Theorem 5.1 (Zero forces a $k$-th power shift).** Let $R$ be a commutative
> ring and $A$ a $D_k(n)$ set with $0 \in A$. If $A$ contains a nonzero element
> $a \ne 0$, then $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power: there exists $c \in R$ with
> $n = c^k$.
>
> *Proof.* Since $0, a \in A$ are distinct (as $a \ne 0$), the $D_k(n)$ property
> applied to the pair $(0, a)$ gives $c \in R$ with
> $$0 \cdot a + n = c^k, \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad n = c^k. \qquad\blacksquare$$

> **Theorem 5.2 (Perfect-power shift permits adjoining zero).** Conversely, if $n
> = c^k$ is a perfect $k$-th power and $A$ is a $D_k(n)$ set with $0 \notin A$,
> then $A \cup \{0\}$ is again a $D_k(n)$ set.
>
> *Proof.* Take distinct $u, v \in A \cup \{0\}$. If both lie in $A$, the required
> $k$-th power exists by hypothesis. Otherwise one of them, say $v$, equals $0$;
> then $uv + n = 0 + n = n = c^k$ is a perfect $k$-th power. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Combining the two directions gives the characterization.

> **Corollary 5.3 (Zero-extension iff perfect power).** A $D_k(n)$ set $A$ with a
> nonzero member admits the extension $A \cup \{0\}$ as a $D_k(n)$ set **if and
> only if** $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power. In particular, over $\mathbb{C}[x]$, if
> a $D_k(n)$ set contains both $0$ and a nonzero element while $n$ is not a perfect
> $k$-th power, the configuration is impossible.

**Interpretation.** The perfect-power condition on $n$ is exactly the "attachment
condition" allowing the most degenerate element $0$ to join a Diophantine set.
Once $n = c^k$, the principal ideal $(c)$ is the natural infinite home suggested
by the conjecture's exception, and the zero element is the seed of that regime.
This precisely matches the exceptional clause of Conjecture 1.2.

## 6. A false heuristic and its correction

Progress in this subject requires discarding a natural but false guess.

> **False heuristic.** *If $g$ is a common factor of two members $a, b$ of a
> $D_k(n)$ set, then $g \mid n$.*

This fails. From $ab + n = c^k$ and $g \mid a$ we obtain $g \mid ab$, hence
$$g \mid c^k - n.$$
There is no reason for $g$ to divide $n$ rather than $c^k - n$; the divisor lands
on the difference, not on the shift. Concretely, one can choose $a, b$ sharing a
factor $g$ while $n$ is a unit, so $g \nmid n$.

> **Correct principle (coprimality).** The productive replacement is: *if a member
> is coprime to $n$, it is coprime to the other members.* This shifts attention
> from shared factors to coprimality with the shift and is the correct structural
> lens for controlling the pairwise interactions of members.

## 7. Algorithms

We describe the procedures used in the accompanying numerical demonstrations.

### 7.1 Diophantine verification

Given a finite set $A \subseteq F[x]$, exponent $k$, and shift $n$, verify the
$D_k(n)$ property by checking, for each unordered pair $\{a, b\}$, whether $ab + n$
is a perfect $k$-th power. Over $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ this reduces to computing the
$k$-th root of the polynomial (if it exists) and confirming its $k$-th power
returns the original. Complexity is $O(|A|^2)$ pair checks, each dominated by a
polynomial factorization/root extraction.

### 7.2 Degree-residue analysis

Given a candidate same-degree family, compute $d$ and test the rigidity law
$k \mid 2d$. If it fails, the family is impossible; this discards infinite classes
of candidates in $O(1)$ arithmetic after reading degrees.

### 7.3 Zero-extension test

Given a $D_k(n)$ set with a nonzero member, decide whether $0$ may be adjoined by
testing whether $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power — a single root extraction.

## 8. Applications and discussion

The three theorems jointly localize Conjecture 1.2. Any counterexample or proof
must live among **nonconstant polynomials of mixed degrees**: constants are
explained (Section 3), same-degree families obey the divisibility law (Section 4),
and the exceptional unbounded regime is exactly the perfect-power/principal-ideal
case detected by the zero polynomial (Section 5). The residue mechanism $k \mid
2d$ suggests that the true bound is controlled by how many distinct degree
residues modulo $k$ can be realized among pairwise products, rather than by raw
cardinality.

Beyond the specific conjecture, the framework $D_k(n)$ over an arbitrary
commutative ring unifies the classical integer problem, its function-field
analogues, and the higher-power variants under one definition, and the proofs
above use only elementary degree arithmetic and the algebraic closure of the
coefficient field.

## 9. Future work

- **Degree-spectrum bound.** Conjecture: over an algebraically closed field of
  characteristic zero, with nonzero shift $n$ and $k \ge 2$, any Diophantine tuple
  of nonconstant polynomials has at most $k + 4$ members of pairwise distinct
  degrees. The mechanism is the count of residues modulo $k$ achievable by the
  product degrees.
- **Arithmetic progression of degrees.** Conjecture: in a maximal nonconstant
  Diophantine tuple with fixed shift, the multiset of degrees forms an arithmetic
  progression whose common difference divides $k$.
- **Uniqueness of the unbounded regime.** Conjecture: a polynomial Diophantine
  tuple is infinite iff $n$ is a perfect $k$-th power and every member lies in the
  ideal generated by a fixed $k$-th root of $n$.
- **Coprimality structure.** Conjecture: if a member is coprime to $n$, it is
  coprime to every other member — the correct replacement for the false
  common-factor heuristic.

## 10. Conclusion

We have proved three structural theorems about $k$-th power Diophantine sets over
polynomial rings: constants provide unbounded families and force the degree
hypothesis; a divisibility law $k \mid 2d$ rigidly constrains same-degree
families and outright forbids degree-one cubic pairs; and the zero polynomial can
join a Diophantine set exactly when the shift is a perfect $k$-th power, capturing
the conjecture's exception. Together they draw a sharp boundary around the
remaining open problem — the absolute bound of six for nonconstant tuples of mixed
degrees.

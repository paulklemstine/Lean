## Abstract

Every primitive Pythagorean triple $a^2+b^2=c^2$ is an instance of the $abc$ situation $A+B=C$ with $A=a^2$, $B=b^2$, $C=c^2$, and the primitive triples form a ternary tree, rooted at $(3,4,5)$, generated by three unimodular matrices. We develop the complete quality theory of this tree. The starting point is the *radical collapse* $\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, which reduces the $abc$ quality of a node to $q=2\log c/\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ and converts every quality threshold into an exact integer inequality: $q>1$ if and only if $\operatorname{rad}(abc)<c^2$, $q<2$ if and only if $\operatorname{rad}(abc)>c$, and more generally $q>m/k$ if and only if $\operatorname{rad}(abc)^m<c^{2k}$.

Using this dictionary we prove: (i) an unconditional lower edge $q>2/3$ for every node, in the quantitative form $q\ge 2\log c/(3\log c-\log 2)\ge \tfrac23+2\log 2/(9\log c)$, so the infimum $2/3$ is approached no faster than $1/\log c$; (ii) an unconditional gap $q<2$ for every node whose product is not powerful ($abc\le\operatorname{rad}(abc)^2$), together with the observation that the residual gap is only $\Theta(1/\log c)$, so that a *uniform* bound $q\le 2-\varepsilon$ over the tree is genuinely of $abc$-strength; (iii) a conditional sharp bound: under an effective $abc$ inequality $C^{12}\le K\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{13}$, every node with $K\le c^4$ has $q\le 13/10$, and under the Masser–Oesterlé conjecture every node with hypotenuse beyond an ineffective threshold has $q\le 1+2\varepsilon$; (iv) an explicit infinite family of $abc$ hits — for odd $d\ge3$ and $d\le2^k$, the almost-isosceles node with parameter $n=d^{2^k}-1$ has $q>1$ — which is doubly exponentially sparse, contributing only $O(\log\log X)$ hits below $X$; and (v) a universal depth law: every Berggren step multiplies the hypotenuse by at most the squared silver ratio $3+2\sqrt2=(1+\sqrt2)^2$, so a node at depth $n$ obeys $c\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^n$, and the depth-$n$ quality window is $2/3<q\le 2(\log5+n\log(3+2\sqrt2))/\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$.

Consequence: the entire depth dependence of the spectrum is carried by the radical alone. The tree therefore isolates, in exact form, the geometric half of the $abc$ problem and leaves the multiplicative half standing alone. We also record explicit spectrum data — $q(3,4,5)\approx0.9464$, $q(7,24,25)\approx1.2040$, non-monotonicity of quality under descent, and the bracket $5/4<q(36207,18424,40625)<4/3$ for the best node in an exhaustive scan of all $159{,}139$ nodes with hypotenuse below $10^6$ — showing that the tree's high-quality region, while nonempty and infinite, stays well below the $abc$ record quality $\approx1.63$.

**Keywords:** $abc$ conjecture, quality, radical, Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, silver ratio, lifting the exponent, Pell numbers.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The $abc$ conjecture and quality

For a positive integer $n$ write
$$\operatorname{rad}(n)=\prod_{p\mid n} p$$
for its **radical**, the product of the distinct primes dividing $n$ (with $\operatorname{rad}(1)=1$). An **$abc$ triple** is a triple of positive integers with
$$A+B=C,\qquad \gcd(A,B)=1 .$$
The Masser–Oesterlé conjecture (1985) asserts that for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $K_\varepsilon$ with
$$C \;\le\; K_\varepsilon\,\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{1+\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for all such triples.}$$
The standard scalar attached to a single triple is its **quality**
$$q(A,B,C)=\frac{\log C}{\log\operatorname{rad}(ABC)} .$$
A triple with $q>1$ is called an **$abc$ hit**. The conjecture is equivalent to: for each $\varepsilon>0$, only finitely many triples have $q>1+\varepsilon$. Hits are known in abundance; the largest quality known is $q\approx1.6299$, from $2+3^{10}\cdot109=23^5$. No triple with $q\ge2$ has ever been found, and the trivial ceiling $q<2$ is not known unconditionally.

A persistent obstacle to intuition is that no natural infinite family of $abc$ triples has a *computable* quality structure. The families used to prove that hits are infinite (e.g. $2^{6n}-1$-type constructions) come with lower bounds on quality but no description of their spectrum, and general families come with no description at all.

### 1.2 Pythagorean triples as $abc$ triples

Let $a,b,c$ be positive integers with
$$a^2+b^2=c^2,\qquad \gcd(a,b)=1 .$$
Setting $A=a^2$, $B=b^2$, $C=c^2$ gives an $abc$ triple: coprimality of $a,b$ implies coprimality of $a^2,b^2$. Its quality is
$$q(a,b,c)=\frac{\log(c^2)}{\log \operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)} .$$

Primitive Pythagorean triples possess an exceptionally rigid global structure. Berggren (1934), rediscovered by Barning (1963) and Hall (1970), showed that the set of primitive triples with $a$ odd is the vertex set of an infinite rooted ternary tree: the root is $(3,4,5)$, and the three children of a node are obtained by the matrices
$$
M_A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\2&-1&2\\2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
M_B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\2&1&2\\2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\quad
M_C=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\-2&1&2\\-2&2&3\end{pmatrix}
$$
acting on the column vector $(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T}$. Each primitive triple occurs exactly once, at the end of a unique finite word in $\{A,B,C\}$, and inverting the matrices gives a descent that returns any triple to $(3,4,5)$ in finitely many steps.

This is therefore a fully explicit infinite family of $abc$ triples with explicit generation, explicit descent, and (as we shall see) an explicit growth constant. The purpose of this paper is to determine what can and cannot be said, exactly, about its quality spectrum.

### 1.3 Summary of results

Throughout, a **tree node** means a triple reached from $(3,4,5)$ by a finite word in $\{A,B,C\}$, and $q(a,b,c)$ denotes its quality as an $abc$ triple in the above sense. Our results:

1. **Radical collapse and the dictionary** (§3): $\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, hence $q=2\log c/\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, and every rational threshold becomes an integer inequality.
2. **Structure** (§4): every tree node is a primitive Pythagorean triple with legs $\ge3$, hypotenuse $\ge5$, and legs strictly below the hypotenuse.
3. **Lower edge** (§5): $q>2/3$ unconditionally, with rate $q\ge\frac23+\frac{2\log2}{9\log c}$.
4. **Upper edge** (§6): $q<2$ off the powerful locus; the mechanism cannot give a uniform gap; conditional gaps $13/10$ and $1+2\varepsilon$.
5. **Infinitely many hits** (§7): the family $n=d^{2^k}-1$ on the almost-isosceles spine, with double-exponential sparsity.
6. **Depth law** (§8): the squared silver ratio $3+2\sqrt2$ is the universal growth constant of the tree, giving the depth-$n$ quality window.
7. **Explicit spectrum data** (§9): computed points, non-monotonicity under descent, the bracket $5/4<q<4/3$ for the record node.
8. **Discussion** (§10–§11): why the supremum question is $abc$-strength, and what the model does isolate.

## 2. Radical toolkit

We record the elementary facts about radicals used throughout. All are proved by manipulating the multiset of prime factors.

**Lemma 2.1 (basic bounds).** For $n\ge1$, $\operatorname{rad}(n)\ge1$ and $\operatorname{rad}(n)\mid n$, hence $\operatorname{rad}(n)\le n$. If $n\ge2$ then $\operatorname{rad}(n)\ge2$.

*Proof.* The radical is a product of divisors of $n$ that are pairwise coprime primes, so it divides $n$; divisibility with $n>0$ gives $\operatorname{rad}(n)\le n$. If $n\ge2$ then $n$ has a prime divisor $p$, which divides $\operatorname{rad}(n)$, so $\operatorname{rad}(n)\ge p\ge2$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.2 (submultiplicativity).** For $m,n\ge1$, $\operatorname{rad}(mn)\le\operatorname{rad}(m)\operatorname{rad}(n)$, with equality when $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

*Proof.* The primes of $mn$ are the union of those of $m$ and of $n$, so
$$\operatorname{rad}(mn)\cdot\!\!\prod_{p\mid m,\;p\mid n}\!\!p \;=\;\operatorname{rad}(m)\operatorname{rad}(n),$$
by splitting the product over a union into product over union times product over intersection. The left factor is $\ge1$. If $m,n$ are coprime the intersection is empty. $\square$

**Lemma 2.3 (prime powers and exponent loss).** If $p$ is prime and $t\ge1$ then $\operatorname{rad}(p^t)=p$. More generally, if $p^s\mid n$ with $s\ge1$ and $n\ge1$, then
$$\operatorname{rad}(n)\cdot p^{\,s-1}\le n .$$

*Proof.* The first claim is immediate. For the second, write $t=v_p(n)\ge s$ and $n=p^t u$ with $p\nmid u$. By Lemma 2.2 (coprime case) and the first claim, $\operatorname{rad}(n)=p\operatorname{rad}(u)$, so
$$\operatorname{rad}(n)p^{s-1}=p^{s}\operatorname{rad}(u)\le p^{s}u\le p^{t}u=n. \qquad\square$$

**Lemma 2.4 (powers are free).** For $m\ge1$, $\operatorname{rad}(n^m)=\operatorname{rad}(n)$; and for positive $a,b,c$ and exponents $r,s,t\ge1$, $\operatorname{rad}(a^rb^sc^t)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$.

*Proof.* Raising to a power changes no prime's presence, only its multiplicity, and the radical depends only on the set of primes present. $\square$

Lemma 2.3 is the engine behind every $abc$ hit we construct: a large prime power dividing one of the terms forces the radical to be smaller than the term by that power, up to one factor of $p$.

## 3. Quality of a Pythagorean triple, and the exact dictionary

**Definition 3.1.** For positive integers $a,b,c$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ we define the **quality**
$$q(a,b,c)\;=\;\frac{\log\bigl(c^{2}\bigr)}{\log \operatorname{rad}\bigl(a^{2}b^{2}c^{2}\bigr)} .$$

**Theorem 3.2 (radical collapse).** For positive $a,b,c$,
$$\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc),\qquad\text{hence}\qquad q(a,b,c)=\frac{2\log c}{\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)} .$$

*Proof.* Lemma 2.4 with $r=s=t=2$; then $\log(c^2)=2\log c$. $\square$

Theorem 3.2 is the reason the tree is tractable. The squares — the source of most of the difficulty in an arbitrary $abc$ instance — disappear from the radical entirely, and the entire arithmetic content is carried by the single integer $abc$, whose factorization is that of the triple itself.

Since $\operatorname{rad}(abc)\ge2$ whenever $abc\ge2$, the denominator $\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ is positive and thresholds can be cleared.

**Theorem 3.3 (threshold dictionary).** Let $a,b,c\ge1$ with $c\ge2$ and $abc\ge2$, and let $m,k$ be positive integers. Then
$$q(a,b,c)>\frac mk \iff \operatorname{rad}(abc)^{\,m}<c^{\,2k},\qquad
q(a,b,c)<\frac mk \iff c^{\,2k}<\operatorname{rad}(abc)^{\,m}.$$
In particular:
$$q>1 \iff \operatorname{rad}(abc)<c^{2},\qquad q<2 \iff c<\operatorname{rad}(abc).$$

*Proof.* Since $\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)>0$ and $k>0$, the inequality $2\log c/\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)>m/k$ is equivalent to $2k\log c>m\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$, i.e. to $\log(c^{2k})>\log(\operatorname{rad}(abc)^m)$, i.e. (strict monotonicity of $\log$ on positive reals) to $\operatorname{rad}(abc)^m<c^{2k}$. The specializations are $m=k=1$ and $m=2,k=1$. $\square$

Thus every question about the quality spectrum of the tree is, verbatim, a question about the size of a single radical relative to a power of the hypotenuse. There is no analytic residue.

## 4. Structure of the tree

**Definition 4.1.** A triple $(a,b,c)$ of positive integers is a **tree node** if it is obtained from $(3,4,5)$ by applying a finite (possibly empty) sequence of the maps $M_A,M_B,M_C$.

**Theorem 4.2 (tree invariant).** Every tree node $(a,b,c)$ satisfies
$$a\ge3,\quad b\ge3,\quad a<c,\quad b<c,\quad a^2+b^2=c^2,\quad \gcd(a,b)=1 ;$$
in particular $c\ge5$.

*Proof.* By induction on the length of the generating word. The root $(3,4,5)$ satisfies all six conditions. For the inductive step, each matrix preserves the Pythagorean relation — a direct polynomial identity, e.g. for $M_B$,
$$(a+2b+2c)^2+(2a+b+2c)^2-(2a+2b+3c)^2 = a^2+b^2-c^2,$$
and similarly for $M_A,M_C$ — and preserves primitivity, since the matrices are unimodular over $\mathbb Z$ and their inverses have integer entries, so a common prime factor of the new legs would descend to a common factor of the old ones. The size inequalities follow from linear arithmetic: e.g. for $M_B$ the new legs are $a+2b+2c\ge 3$ and $2a+b+2c\ge3$, and both are less than $2a+2b+3c$ because $a,b\ge3>0$. Finally $c^2=a^2+b^2\ge 9+9$ and $c>a\ge3$ force $c\ge5$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.3 (every node is an $abc$ triple).** For every tree node, $a^2+b^2=c^2$ with $\gcd(a^2,b^2)=1$, and $\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$. $\square$

Two useful elementary inequalities, valid for any Pythagorean triple with legs $\ge3$:

**Lemma 4.4.** If $a,b\ge3$ and $a^2+b^2=c^2$, then $2ab\le c^2$ and $2c\le ab$.

*Proof.* The first is $(a-b)^2\ge0$. For the second, $(ab)^2-(2c)^2=a^2b^2-4a^2-4b^2 = (a^2-4)(b^2-4)-16 \ge (9-4)(9-4)-16=9>0$ using $a,b\ge3$; taking square roots of the nonnegative quantities gives $ab>2c$. $\square$

## 5. The lower edge of the spectrum

**Theorem 5.1 (quantitative lower edge).** Let $a,b\ge3$, $c\ge5$ with $a^2+b^2=c^2$. Then
$$q(a,b,c)\;\ge\;\frac{2\log c}{3\log c-\log 2}\;>\;\frac23 .$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 4.4, $2ab\le c^2$, so $2abc\le c^3$, i.e. $abc\le c^3/2$. By Lemma 2.1, $\operatorname{rad}(abc)\le abc\le c^3/2$. Taking logarithms, $\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)\le 3\log c-\log 2$, and this quantity is positive because $c\ge5$. Dividing $2\log c>0$ by a smaller positive denominator increases the ratio, so
$$q=\frac{2\log c}{\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)}\ \ge\ \frac{2\log c}{3\log c-\log 2} .$$
The final strict inequality is $6\log c>6\log c-2\log2$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.2 (rate).** Every tree node satisfies
$$q(a,b,c)\;\ge\;\frac23+\frac{2\log 2}{9\log c} .$$

*Proof.* Write $L=\log c\ge\log5$ and $D=3L-\log2>0$. Then
$$\frac{2L}{D}-\frac23-\frac{2\log2}{9L} = \frac{18L^2-6LD-2D\log2}{9LD} = \frac{18L^2-6L(3L-\log2)-2(3L-\log2)\log 2}{9LD} = \frac{2(\log 2)^2}{9LD}>0. \qquad\square$$

Two consequences. First, $2/3$ is a genuine barrier: no node of the tree has quality $\le2/3$. Second, the barrier is *asymptotically attained at most at rate $1/\log c$*: any sequence of nodes with $q\to2/3$ must have $c\to\infty$, and the approach is logarithmically slow. Numerically, the smallest quality among all nodes with $c\le3\cdot10^5$ is $0.6920$, against the bound $0.6785$ at that size.

**Remark 5.3 (why $2/3$).** The bound is saturated in shape by squarefree products: if $abc$ is squarefree then $\operatorname{rad}(abc)=abc\asymp c^3$ and $q\approx 2\log c/(3\log c)=2/3$. The lower edge of the tree's spectrum is exactly the squarefree regime, where the $abc$ mechanism has nothing to exploit.

## 6. The upper edge

By Theorem 3.3, $q<2$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{rad}(abc)>c$ — a weak-looking condition, since $abc>c^3/\text{const}$. It nevertheless cannot be verified unconditionally in general, because $\operatorname{rad}$ can in principle be tiny. What can be proved unconditionally is:

**Theorem 6.1 (unconditional gap off the powerful locus).** Let $a,b\ge3$, $c\ge5$, $a^2+b^2=c^2$, and suppose $abc\le\operatorname{rad}(abc)^2$ (which holds, for instance, whenever $abc$ is squarefree, and more generally whenever $abc$ is not powerful). Then $q(a,b,c)<2$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 4.4, $2c\le ab$, hence $2c^2\le abc\le \operatorname{rad}(abc)^2$, so $c^2<\operatorname{rad}(abc)^2$ and therefore $c<\operatorname{rad}(abc)$. Apply Theorem 3.3. $\square$

**Remark 6.2 (the gap is not uniform).** Theorem 6.1 gives $q<2$ but the *size* of the gap it yields is
$$2-q=2-\frac{2\log c}{\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)}\ \ge\ \frac{2\log(\operatorname{rad}(abc)/c)}{\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)},$$
which under the hypothesis $abc\le\operatorname{rad}(abc)^2$ is only $\Theta(1/\log c)$ in the worst case. Hence *no* uniform bound $q\le2-\varepsilon$ over the whole tree follows from this mechanism. This is not a deficiency of the argument. A uniform gap would say that $\operatorname{rad}(abc)\ge c^{1+\delta}$ for all tree nodes, i.e. that the radical of a Pythagorean product is never nearly as small as its hypotenuse — an $abc$-strength statement about an infinite family.

Making the dependence on $abc$-type input explicit:

**Theorem 6.3 (tree $abc$ theorem, effective form).** Suppose $K$ is a constant for which the effective $abc$ inequality
$$C^{12}\le K\cdot\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{13}$$
holds for all coprime $A+B=C$ of positive integers. Then every Pythagorean triple with $\gcd(a,b)=1$, $c\ge2$ and $K\le c^4$ satisfies
$$q(a,b,c)\le\frac{13}{10}.$$

*Proof.* Apply the hypothesis to $A=a^2$, $B=b^2$, $C=c^2$ and use Theorem 3.2 to rewrite $\operatorname{rad}(ABC)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)=:R$:
$$c^{24}=(c^2)^{12}\le K R^{13}\le c^4R^{13},$$
whence $c^{20}\le R^{13}$. Taking logarithms, $20\log c\le13\log R$, and therefore
$$q=\frac{2\log c}{\log R}\le\frac{2\cdot 13}{20}=\frac{13}{10}. \qquad\square$$

Thus a single effective $abc$ inequality beats the trivial ceiling $2$ for the whole tree with the explicit gap $\varepsilon=7/10$, except for the finitely many nodes with $c^4<K$.

**Theorem 6.4 (conditional asymptotic law).** Assume the Masser–Oesterlé conjecture. Then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a threshold $C_0=C_0(\varepsilon)$ such that every primitive Pythagorean triple with $c\ge C_0$ satisfies
$$q(a,b,c)\le1+2\varepsilon .$$

*Proof sketch.* Let $K=K_\varepsilon$ be the constant from the conjecture, applied to $A=a^2,B=b^2,C=c^2$ and simplified by Theorem 3.2: $c^2\le K R^{1+\varepsilon}$ with $R=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$. Taking logarithms,
$$2\log c\le\log K+(1+\varepsilon)\log R .$$
The problem is to absorb $\log K$. Set $M=\max(\log K,0)$ and choose
$$C_0=\exp\!\Bigl(\tfrac{M}{2}\bigl(1+\tfrac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\bigr)\Bigr).$$
If $c\ge C_0$ then $\log c\ge \tfrac M2(1+\tfrac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon})$, and combining with the displayed inequality one deduces $\log K\le \varepsilon\log R$: indeed if $\log K\le0$ this is trivial, and otherwise the displayed inequality gives $\log R\ge(2\log c-\log K)/(1+\varepsilon)\ge \log K/\varepsilon$ after substituting the lower bound for $\log c$. Feeding $\log K\le\varepsilon\log R$ back in,
$$2\log c\le(1+2\varepsilon)\log R,$$
which is exactly $q\le1+2\varepsilon$. $\square$

Combining Theorems 5.1 and 6.4: under $abc$, the limiting quality spectrum of the tree lies in the window $(2/3,\,1]$. Values above $1$ occur (§7) but, conditionally, only below an ineffective hypotenuse threshold.

## 7. Infinitely many hits, and their sparsity

### 7.1 The almost-isosceles spine

Iterating $M_A$ from the root produces the **$A$-spine**
$$T_n=(2n+1,\;2n(n+1),\;2n^2+2n+1),\qquad n\ge1,$$
so that $T_1=(3,4,5)$, $T_2=(5,12,13)$, $T_3=(7,24,25)$, $T_4=(9,40,41)$, …, the triples with $c=b+1$. That $M_A T_n=T_{n+1}$ is a two-line polynomial verification, and $T_n$ is the node at the end of the word $A^{\,n-1}$.

Along this spine the product is
$$abc=(2n+1)\cdot 2n(n+1)\cdot(2n^2+2n+1),$$
and by submultiplicativity (Lemma 2.2),
$$\operatorname{rad}(abc)\le \operatorname{rad}(2n+1)\cdot2\operatorname{rad}(n)\operatorname{rad}(n+1)\cdot\operatorname{rad}(2n^2+2n+1)\le (2n+1)\cdot 2\operatorname{rad}(n)\operatorname{rad}(n+1)\cdot c .$$

**Proposition 7.1 (hit criterion).** If $n\ge3$ and
$$(2n+1)\cdot 2\operatorname{rad}(n)\operatorname{rad}(n+1)\;<\;2n^2+2n+1,$$
then the spine node $T_n$ has $q(T_n)>1$.

*Proof.* The displayed inequality times $c$ gives $\operatorname{rad}(abc)<c^2$ by the bound above; apply Theorem 3.3. $\square$

So we need $n$ and $n+1$ to be simultaneously deficient in radical: both must be rich in repeated prime factors. Such pairs are produced by lifting the exponent.

### 7.2 The family $n=d^{2^k}-1$

**Lemma 7.2 (a lifting-the-exponent bound).** Let $d\ge3$ be odd and $k\ge1$. Then $2^{k+2}$ divides $d^{2^k}-1$.

*Proof.* Factor
$$d^{2^k}-1=(d-1)(d+1)(d^2+1)(d^4+1)\cdots(d^{2^{k-1}}+1).$$
Since $d$ is odd, $d-1$ and $d+1$ are consecutive even numbers, so their product is divisible by $8$; each of the further $k-1$ factors $d^{2^j}+1$ ($1\le j\le k-1$) is even. This gives $2^{3}\cdot 2^{k-1}=2^{k+2}$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (explicit infinite hit family).** Let $d\ge3$ be odd, let $k\ge1$ satisfy $d\le 2^k$, and put $n=d^{2^k}-1$. Then the spine node
$$T_n=(2n+1,\;2n(n+1),\;2n^2+2n+1)$$
is a tree node with $q(T_n)>1$.

*Proof sketch.* Two radical collapses, one in each of $n$ and $n+1$:

* $n+1=d^{2^k}$ is a pure power, so $\operatorname{rad}(n+1)=\operatorname{rad}(d)\le d$ by Lemma 2.4;
* $2^{k+2}\mid n$ by Lemma 7.2, so $\operatorname{rad}(n)\cdot2^{k+1}\le n$ by Lemma 2.3, i.e. $\operatorname{rad}(n)\le n/2^{k+1}$.

Substituting into the bound preceding Proposition 7.1,
$$\operatorname{rad}(abc)\;\le\;(2n+1)\cdot 2\cdot\frac{n}{2^{k+1}}\cdot d\cdot c\;=\;\frac{(2n+1)\,n\,d}{2^{k}}\;c .$$
It therefore suffices that $(2n+1)nd/2^k<c=2n^2+2n+1$. Since $d\le2^k$ we have $(2n+1)nd/2^k\le (2n+1)n=2n^2+n<2n^2+2n+1$, and the strict inequality holds. Apply Theorem 3.3. $\square$

**Corollary 7.4 (infinitely many hits).** For every bound $N$ there is a tree node $(a,b,c)$ with $c>N$ and $q(a,b,c)>1$. Indeed this holds along a family attached to *every* odd base $d\ge3$: choose $k$ large enough that $d\le2^k$ and $d^{2^k}-1>N$. $\square$

The condition $d\le 2^k$ is precisely the balance point of the two collapses: the gain $2^{k+1}$ from the powers of two in $n$ must dominate the loss $d$ incurred by replacing $\operatorname{rad}(n+1)=\operatorname{rad}(d)$ by $d$.

### 7.3 Sparsity

Write $c_k$ for the hypotenuse of the base-$3$ member, $n_k=3^{2^k}-1$, $c_k=2n_k^2+2n_k+1$.

**Theorem 7.5 (double-exponential sparsity).** For every $k\ge0$,
$$c_{k+1}<c_k^{\,2}<4c_{k+1}.$$
Consequently the number of members of the family with hypotenuse below $X$ is $O(\log\log X)$.

*Proof.* Put $m=3^{2^k}$, so $n_k=m-1$, $n_{k+1}=m^2-1$, and
$$c_k=2m^2-2m+1,\qquad c_{k+1}=2m^4-2m^2+1 .$$
Then
$$c_k^2-c_{k+1}=(2m^2-2m+1)^2-(2m^4-2m^2+1)=2m^4-8m^3+10m^2-4m = 2m(m-1)(m-2)(m-1) >0$$
for $m\ge3$, and
$$4c_{k+1}-c_k^2=4m^4+8m^3-16m^2+4m+3>0$$
for $m\ge1$. Iterating $c_{k+1}\asymp c_k^2$ gives $\log\log c_k\asymp k$. $\square$

So the mechanism we can fully control — the only mechanism producing provable hits here — is *doubly exponentially sparse*. That the tree contains infinitely many hits is therefore compatible with hits being extremely rare, and the numerics of §9 confirm rarity ($0.43\%$ of nodes with $c\le3\cdot10^5$).

## 8. The silver-ratio depth law

The tree has a growth constant, and it is not the golden ratio.

**Definition 8.1.** Let $\sigma=1+\sqrt2$ be the silver ratio, and $\sigma^2=3+2\sqrt2\approx5.828427$.

**Lemma 8.2.** Any Pythagorean triple satisfies $a+b\le\sqrt2\,c$.

*Proof.* $(a+b)^2=c^2+2ab\le 2c^2$ by Lemma 4.4 (or directly by $(a-b)^2\ge0$). $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (one step multiplies the hypotenuse by at most $\sigma^2$).** If $(a,b,c)$ is a tree node and $(a',b',c')$ is any of its three children, then
$$c'\le(3+2\sqrt2)\,c .$$

*Proof.* The three matrices give $c'=2a+2b+3c$, $c'=2a-2b+3c$, $c'=-2a+2b+3c$ respectively. In every case $c'\le 2(a+b)+3c\le 2\sqrt2\,c+3c=(3+2\sqrt2)c$ by Lemma 8.2. $\square$

**Corollary 8.4 (universal growth bound).** A node reached by a word of length $n$ has $c\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^n$. $\square$

The bound is attained up to a bounded factor. Along the all-$B$ branch the hypotenuses satisfy the Pell-type recursion $c_{n+1}=6c_n-c_{n-1}$ with $c_0=5$, $c_1=29$:
$$5,\;29,\;169,\;985,\;5741,\;33461,\;195025,\;1136689,\dots$$

**Proposition 8.5 (two-sided law on the $B$-spine).** For all $n\ge0$,
$$5^{\,n+1}\le c_n\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^n .$$

*Proof.* The upper bound is Corollary 8.4. For the lower bound, induction using $c_{n+1}=6c_n-c_{n-1}$ and $c_{n}\ge 5c_{n-1}$: indeed $6c_n-c_{n-1}\ge6c_n-c_n/5\ge5c_n$. $\square$

Numerically $c_n/(5(3+2\sqrt2)^n)\to0.995$, so the silver ratio squared is the exact growth constant of the tree, attained on the $B$-spine.

**Theorem 8.6 (depth-$n$ quality window).** For every word $p$ of length $n$ in $\{A,B,C\}$, the node $(a,b,c)$ it reaches satisfies
$$\log c\le \log 5+n\log(3+2\sqrt2),\qquad q(a,b,c)\cdot\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)=2\log c,$$
and hence
$$\frac23\;<\;q(a,b,c)\;\le\;\frac{2\bigl(\log5+n\log(3+2\sqrt2)\bigr)}{\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)} .$$

*Proof.* Combine Corollary 8.4, the definition of $q$ (Theorem 3.2, in cleared-denominator form), and Theorem 5.1. $\square$

**Interpretation.** In the ratio $q=2\log c/\log\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ the numerator is pinned by depth alone, uniformly across all $3^n$ words: it is $2\log c\in[\,2(n+1)\log5,\;2\log5+2n\log(3+2\sqrt2)\,]$ on the extremal branch and bounded above everywhere. All variation in the spectrum — all $3^n$ different qualities at depth $n$ — comes from the denominator. The tree cleanly factors the $abc$ problem into a geometric part, solved exactly by the silver ratio, and a multiplicative part, which is the conjecture.

## 9. Explicit spectrum data

The following values are exact rational comparisons via Theorem 3.3 (each is a comparison of two explicit integers), with decimals given for orientation.

| node | word | $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ | $q$ | hit? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(3,4,5)$ | (root) | $30$ | $0.946395$ | no |
| $(5,12,13)$ | $A$ | $390$ | $0.859834$ | no |
| $(7,24,25)$ | $AA$ | $210$ | $1.203969$ | **yes** |

**Theorem 9.1 (non-monotonicity under descent).** $q(5,12,13)<1<q(7,24,25)$ and $q(105,88,137)<1$, where each triple is the parent of the next. Hence the quality is not monotone along the tree's descent: there is no branch along which quality increases.

*Proof.* $\operatorname{rad}(5\cdot12\cdot13)=390>169=13^2$, so $q<1$; $\operatorname{rad}(7\cdot24\cdot25)=210<625=25^2$, so $q>1$; $\operatorname{rad}(105\cdot88\cdot137)=316470>18769=137^2$, so $q<1$. Apply Theorem 3.3. $\square$

**Theorem 9.2 (bracket for the best node found).** The node $(36207,18424,40625)$, reached from the root by the word $CCCACCBC$, satisfies
$$\frac54\;<\;q(36207,18424,40625)\;<\;\frac43 .$$

*Proof.* $\operatorname{rad}(36207\cdot18424\cdot40625)=19118190$. By Theorem 3.3 with $m/k=5/4$ we must check $19118190^{5}<40625^{8}$, and with $m/k=4/3$ we must check $40625^{6}<19118190^{4}$. Both are finite integer comparisons and both hold. $\square$

An exhaustive scan of all $159{,}139$ tree nodes with $c\le10^6$ finds no node of quality exceeding this one; the observed spectrum is contained in $[0.692,\,1.2659]$, with $0.43\%$ of nodes (in the range $c\le3\cdot10^5$) being hits and a unimodal distribution peaking near $q\approx0.73$. In particular, no node examined approaches the $abc$ record quality $\approx1.6299$, and the second- and third-best nodes ($q\approx1.2649$ at $(239,28560,28561)$, depth $118$; $q\approx1.2620$ at $(14553,144896,145625)$, depth $22$) sit at wildly different depths — further evidence that quality is not a function of position in any simple sense.

## 10. Algorithms

Three computational procedures underlie §9; all are elementary but worth stating because they show how completely the dictionary of §3 removes floating point from the problem.

**(A) Exact threshold test.** To decide $q(a,b,c)>m/k$: compute $R=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ by factoring $abc$; return the verdict of the exact big-integer comparison $R^m<c^{2k}$. Cost: one factorization plus $O(\log(mk))$ big-integer multiplications. No logarithms are computed and the answer is certified.

**(B) Breadth-first spectrum scan.** Maintain a queue seeded with $(3,4,5)$; pop a node, record its quality, push those of its three images under $M_A,M_B,M_C$ whose hypotenuse does not exceed the cutoff $X$. Correctness and termination follow from Theorem 4.2 (all nodes are genuine triples) and from the fact that each matrix strictly increases the hypotenuse. The number of nodes below $X$ is $\Theta(X)$-ish in practice (empirically $\approx0.16X$ for $X=10^6$); each costs one factorization.

**(C) Certified hits without factoring.** For the family of Theorem 7.3 the hypotenuse has astronomically many digits and cannot be factored. Instead one *certifies* the hit from the proof: with $n=d^{2^k}-1$, $a=2n+1$, $c=2n^2+2n+1$,
$$\operatorname{rad}(abc)\;\le\;B(d,k):=a\cdot 2\left\lfloor n/2^{k+1}\right\rfloor\cdot d\cdot c,$$
whence $q\ge 2\log c/\log B(d,k)$, a rigorous lower bound computable in $O(\text{polylog})$ big-integer operations. For $(d,k)=(3,6)$ this certifies $q\ge1.0109$ for a $62$-digit hypotenuse; for $(7,6)$, $q\ge1.0044$ for a $109$-digit one.

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 What the model solves

The Berggren tree delivers, in closed form, every ingredient of an $abc$ family except one:

* **Membership.** Every node is a certified primitive Pythagorean triple, hence an $abc$ triple, with legs $\ge3$ (Theorem 4.2).
* **Radical.** $\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$: the $abc$ radical of a node is the radical of the triple itself (Theorem 3.2).
* **Thresholds.** Every quality threshold is an exact integer inequality (Theorem 3.3).
* **Growth.** Depth $n$ pins $\log c$ up to a bounded additive constant, with growth constant $(1+\sqrt2)^2$ (Theorem 8.6).
* **Floor.** $q>2/3$, approached no faster than $1/\log c$ (Theorem 5.1, Corollary 5.2).
* **Hits.** Infinitely many, along an explicit family for every odd base, doubly exponentially sparse (Theorem 7.3, Theorem 7.5).

### 11.2 What the model does not solve, and why that is informative

The original ambition was an exactly computed supremum of the tree's quality — attained or approached along an identifiable branch — plus an exact distribution law at depth $n$. Neither is achieved, and the obstruction is structural rather than technical:

1. **The supremum is $abc$-strength.** By Theorem 3.3, $\sup q<2-\varepsilon$ over the tree is the assertion $\operatorname{rad}(abc)>c^{2/(2-\varepsilon)}$ for all nodes, i.e. a power-saving lower bound on the radical of a Pythagorean product. The gap furnished by unconditional means (Theorem 6.1) is $\Theta(1/\log c)$ and cannot be improved by that mechanism (Remark 6.2). Conversely, an effective $abc$ inequality immediately gives $q\le13/10$ (Theorem 6.3). The supremum question is therefore equivalent, up to constants, to the conjecture restricted to this family.
2. **The distribution is governed by radicals of $n(n+1)$.** On the $A$-spine, the depth-$n$ quality is a function of $\operatorname{rad}(n)$, $\operatorname{rad}(n+1)$ and $\operatorname{rad}(2n^2+2n+1)$. The distribution of $\operatorname{rad}(n(n+1))$ is precisely the object controlled by the standard $abc$ heuristics; no exact law is available, and an exact law would itself be a major theorem.

This is a genuinely useful negative: it *localizes* the difficulty. The geometric side of $abc$ — how the terms are generated, how they grow, how descent acts, how the radical simplifies — is exactly solvable here. The multiplicative side is untouched, and provably must be. In the language of exactly solvable models, the tree is not a solved instance of $abc$; it is a system in which every degree of freedom except the essential one has been integrated out.

### 11.3 Comparison with the general picture

Three quantitative comparisons put the family in context:

* The record over the tree ($q\approx1.2659$, hypotenuse $40625$) is far below the global record $q\approx1.6299$. Structured families appear to be *bad* at producing extreme hits — the extreme hits are sporadic coincidences, not the output of a mechanism.
* Hits occur at density $\approx0.4\%$ in the scanned range, while the provable family has density $O(\log\log X/X)$. There is a large gap between what we can prove and what is observed, entirely analogous to the gap in the general $abc$ hit tables.
* The tree's floor $2/3$ has no analogue for general $abc$ triples, where the quality can be arbitrarily close to $0$ (take $C$ squarefree and huge). The floor is a genuine feature of the Pythagorean shape — a consequence of $2ab\le c^2$ — and is a reminder that this family is *uniformly good* $abc$ material even though it is not *extremely* good.

## 12. Future directions

**1. Sharp lower edge.** Theorem 5.1 gives $q\ge 2\log c/(3\log c-\log2)$, and Corollary 5.2 the rate $2/3+\Theta(1/\log c)$. Is the constant $2\log2/9$ optimal? Equivalently: how often is $abc$ squarefree along the tree, and how close to $c^3/2$ can $\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ be? A sharp answer would need an unconditional lower bound for the density of nodes with $abc$ squarefree — a squarefree-values-of-a-quaternary-form problem.

**2. The high-quality region.** Characterize the set of words $w\in\{A,B,C\}^*$ whose nodes are hits. The data suggest no automaticity (hits occur at depths $6,8,22,118,\dots$ with no evident pattern), and the non-monotonicity theorem rules out branch-monotone descriptions. Is the language of hit-words non-regular? Non-context-free?

**3. Denser provable hit families.** Theorem 7.3 produces $O(\log\log X)$ hits below $X$. Every known unconditional construction of infinitely many $abc$ hits is similarly sparse. Can a Pythagorean family with $\gg X^{\delta}$ hits below $X$ be produced — e.g. by exploiting simultaneous power-richness of $n$, $n+1$ and $2n^2+2n+1$ along a two-parameter subfamily of the tree?

**4. Other spines.** The $A$-spine (almost-isosceles) and $B$-spine (Pell) are analyzed here. The $C$-spine and mixed periodic words $w^\infty$ give further one-parameter families with linear-recurrence hypotenuses; each has its own quality law of the shape $q_n\log\operatorname{rad}=2\log c_n$ with $c_n$ satisfying an explicit recursion. A uniform treatment for all eventually periodic words would give the "quality of a branch" as a function on the boundary of the tree.

**5. Distribution at depth $n$.** Conditional on standard heuristics for $\operatorname{rad}$ of polynomial values, predict the limiting distribution of $q$ over the $3^n$ nodes at depth $n$ — the numerics show a stable unimodal shape peaked near $0.73$ — and test the prediction against deep scans.

**6. Beyond squares.** The same collapse $\operatorname{rad}(a^rb^sc^t)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ holds for arbitrary exponents. Applying the dictionary to Fermat–Catalan-type equations $x^p+y^q=z^r$ with $1/p+1/q+1/r<1$ would extend the model, at the cost of losing the tree.

**7. Effective constants.** Theorem 6.3 uses the shape $C^{12}\le K\operatorname{rad}(ABC)^{13}$; other effective shapes give other explicit ceilings. Determining the best ceiling obtainable from currently known effective results (which are far weaker than the conjecture, of Baker-type strength) would give the first *unconditional numerical* upper bound for $\sup q$ over the tree, if any is achievable at all.

## 13. Conclusion

Reading $a^2+b^2=c^2$ as $a^2+b^2=c^2$ — that is, as an instance of $A+B=C$ — turns the oldest parametrized family in mathematics into an infinite family of $abc$ triples, and the ternary tree structure of primitive triples turns that family into something rare: an $abc$ family whose geometry is completely explicit. The collapse $\operatorname{rad}(a^2b^2c^2)=\operatorname{rad}(abc)$ makes the quality a ratio of two transparent quantities; the silver ratio $(1+\sqrt2)^2$ fixes the numerator as a function of depth; the inequality $2ab\le c^2$ fixes an unconditional floor of $2/3$ with an exact rate; and lifting the exponent produces infinitely many hits along an explicitly parametrized, doubly-exponentially sparse family.

What remains — whether the supremum of the spectrum is $1$, $13/10$, or anything below $2$; what the depth-$n$ distribution converges to — is exactly the content of the $abc$ conjecture, restricted to a family where nothing else is in the way. That is the value of the model: not that it settles the conjecture, but that it shows, with complete precision, what part of the conjecture is irreducible.

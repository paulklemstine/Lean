## Abstract

We study the fixed-point sets of elementary cellular automata on cyclic lattices, uniformly in the lattice size. Writing $V(g,n)$ for the set of configurations $s : \mathbb{Z}/n \to \mathbb{F}_2$ satisfying $s_i = g(s_{i-1}, s_i, s_{i+1})$ for all $i$, we obtain complete descriptions of $V(g,n)$ for large families of the $256$ elementary rules, valid for every $n \ge 1$ simultaneously.

Four kinds of result are proved. **(i) Propagation criteria.** A rule satisfying the three local conditions "$g(a,0,c) = 0 \Rightarrow c = 0$", "$g(1,1,1) \ne 1$" and "$g(0,0,0) = 0$" has $V(g,n) = \{0\}$ for every $n$; these conditions are equivalent to fixing four bits of the Wolfram rule number, so exactly $16$ rules are certified, Rule $110$ among them. A companion two-sided criterion ("zeros travel left, ones travel right") forces $V(g,n)$ to be exactly the two constant configurations; exactly $4$ rules satisfy it, namely $176$, $178$, $184$ and $186$, including the traffic rule $184$. **(ii) Symmetry.** Reflection and complementation are realised as conjugacies of the global dynamics, not merely as bijections of truth tables; consequently the fixed-point count and the entire exact-period spectrum are class functions for the resulting Klein four-group. Closing the singleton census under the group yields $48$ certified rules, and Rules $124$, $137$, $193$ inherit the Rule $110$ singleton theorem. **(iii) Periodic descent.** For $d \mid n$ the pullback along $\mathbb{Z}/n \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/d$ is an injective simulation embedding of dynamical systems; it gives $\#V(g,d) \le \#V(g,n)$ for all rules, exact equality when all fixed configurations are $d$-periodic, and preservation of exact orbit periods. Combined with a coprimality collapse this yields the exact arithmetic counting functions $\#V(150,n) = 4$ or $2$ according as $n$ is even or odd, $\#V(90,n) = 4$ or $1$ according as $3 \mid n$ or not, and $\#V(30,n) = 3$ or $1$ according as $n$ is even or odd. **(iv) Scheme-theoretic model.** Each local rule is realised by explicit interpolation polynomials whose evaluation is the global update map, and the fixed configurations are exactly the $\mathbb{F}_2$-points of the ideal generated by the update equations together with the Boolean relations $x_i^2 - x_i$.

We conclude with a negative result of methodological interest: the fixed-point statistic cannot detect computational universality. Rules $46$ and $110$ agree in all four bits governing the count, hence have identical fixed-point cardinality on every lattice, although they are classified differently in the standard qualitative taxonomy. In contrast, orbit-spectrum data is strictly finer: Rule $110$ has an exact two-cycle on every lattice of size divisible by $4$, Rule $51$ has no fixed configuration at all yet has every configuration on an exact $2$-orbit, and Rule $170$ exhibits exact orbit lengths growing linearly in the lattice size.

**Keywords:** elementary cellular automata, Rule 110, fixed-point variety, de Bruijn graph, periodic descent, Klein four-group symmetry, traffic rule 184, $\mathbb{F}_2$ polynomial dynamics.

## 1. Introduction

An elementary cellular automaton is specified by a *local rule* $g : \mathbb{F}_2^3 \to \mathbb{F}_2$, where $\mathbb{F}_2 = \{0,1\}$ is the field with two elements. On the cyclic lattice of $n$ sites the rule induces a *global update map*
$$F_g : \mathbb{F}_2^{\mathbb{Z}/n} \to \mathbb{F}_2^{\mathbb{Z}/n}, \qquad (F_g s)(i) = g\big(s(i-1),\, s(i),\, s(i+1)\big).$$
There are $2^8 = 256$ local rules, indexed since Wolfram by the number whose binary expansion lists the eight outputs. Despite the triviality of the specification, the family contains a computationally universal member (Rule $110$), a standard chaotic exemplar (Rule $30$), the additive rules $90$ and $150$ whose space-time diagrams are Sierpiński gaskets, and the traffic rule $184$.

The object of this paper is the set of *fixed configurations*
$$V(g,n) := \{ s \in \mathbb{F}_2^{\mathbb{Z}/n} : F_g s = s \},$$
which we call the *fixed-point variety* of $g$ at size $n$, for reasons made precise in §7: it is the set of $\mathbb{F}_2$-rational points of an explicit affine scheme over $\mathbb{F}_2$. Our aim is not to compute $\#V(g,n)$ for particular $n$ — that is a finite search — but to determine $\#V(g,\cdot)$ *as a function of $n$*, and to identify the structural mechanisms that make such uniform determinations possible.

Three mechanisms emerge, and they are logically independent:

1. **Local propagation, amplified by cyclicity.** If a single entry of the truth table forces a bit to travel around the lattice, one entry of the table determines the whole variety, at every size.
2. **Conjugacy.** Reflection and complementation are changes of coordinates for the dynamics; every dynamical invariant is therefore constant on their orbits, so one proof certifies four rules.
3. **Descent along divisibility.** When all fixed configurations are $d$-periodic and $d \mid n$, the problem at size $n$ *is* the problem at size $d$; when $\gcd(d,n) = 1$, $d$-periodicity degenerates to constancy. Between them these two cases handle all $n$.

We also record what these techniques *cannot* do, since that is the sharpest conclusion of the work: the fixed-point statistic is a coarse invariant that records the period of an associated recurrence and is blind to computational universality.

## 2. Setup and basic definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Cells, configurations, rules).** Let $\mathbb{F}_2$ be the two-element field, written additively (so $1+1 = 0$). For $n \ge 1$ let $\mathbb{Z}/n$ denote the cyclic group of lattice sites. A *configuration* is a function $s : \mathbb{Z}/n \to \mathbb{F}_2$; the set of configurations is written $\mathrm{Config}(n)$ and has $2^n$ elements. A *local rule* is a function $g : \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_2 \times \mathbb{F}_2 \to \mathbb{F}_2$.

**Definition 2.2 (Global update, fixed configurations).** The *global update map* of $g$ is $F_g : \mathrm{Config}(n) \to \mathrm{Config}(n)$ with $(F_g s)(i) = g(s(i-1), s(i), s(i+1))$, indices in $\mathbb{Z}/n$. A configuration $s$ is *fixed* if $F_g s = s$. We write $V(g,n)$ for the set of fixed configurations and $\#V(g,n)$ for its cardinality.

**Definition 2.3 (Wolfram rule numbers).** For $r \in \mathbb{N}$ define the local rule $\mathrm{rule}(r)$ by
$$\mathrm{rule}(r)(a,b,c) = \text{the } (4a+2b+c)\text{-th binary digit of } r,$$
and for a local rule $g$ define its rule number
$$\nu(g) = \sum_{a,b,c \in \mathbb{F}_2} [\,g(a,b,c) = 1\,]\; 2^{\,4a+2b+c}.$$

**Proposition 2.4 (Decoding is a bijection).** $\mathrm{rule}(\nu(g)) = g$ for every local rule $g$, $\nu(\mathrm{rule}(r)) = r$ for every $r < 256$, and $\nu(g) < 256$ always. Hence $r \mapsto \mathrm{rule}(r)$ is a bijection from $\{0,1,\dots,255\}$ onto the set of local rules, which therefore has exactly $256$ elements.

*Proof sketch.* All three statements are finite: the first quantifies over the $2^8$ rules and $2^3$ neighbourhoods, the second over the $256$ numbers. Each is an identity of binary expansions verified entry by entry. $\square$

We use the standard algebraic normal forms throughout:
$$g_{110}(a,b,c) = b+c+bc+abc, \quad g_{150} = a+b+c, \quad g_{90} = a+c,$$
$$g_{30} = a+b+c+bc, \quad g_{51} = 1+b, \quad g_{170} = c, \quad g_{240} = a,$$
each verified against its rule number by exhaustive comparison of truth tables.

## 3. Shifts and equivariance

**Definition 3.1.** The *left shift* and *right shift* are the maps $\sigma^-, \sigma^+ : \mathrm{Config}(n) \to \mathrm{Config}(n)$ given by $(\sigma^- s)(i) = s(i+1)$ and $(\sigma^+ s)(i) = s(i-1)$.

**Proposition 3.2.** $\sigma^-$ and $\sigma^+$ are mutually inverse $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear automorphisms of $\mathrm{Config}(n)$. Moreover $F_{\mathrm{rule}(170)} = \sigma^-$ and $F_{\mathrm{rule}(240)} = \sigma^+$ identically: rules $170$ and $240$ *are* the shifts.

*Proof sketch.* Linearity is pointwise; $\sigma^\pm$ are inverse because $(i+1)-1 = i$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$. The identification of rules $170$ and $240$ is immediate from their normal forms $g_{170}(a,b,c) = c$ and $g_{240}(a,b,c) = a$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.3 (Shift equivariance).** For every local rule $g$ and every configuration $s$,
$$F_g(\sigma^- s) = \sigma^-(F_g s), \qquad F_g(\sigma^+ s) = \sigma^+(F_g s).$$

*Proof sketch.* Evaluate both sides at $i$. The left-hand side is $g(s(i-1+1), s(i+1), s(i+1+1))$ and the right-hand side is $g(s(i+1-1), s(i+1), s(i+1+1))$; these agree because $i-1+1 = i = i+1-1$. Equivariance for $\sigma^+$ follows by conjugating with $\sigma^-$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4.** $V(g,n)$ is $\sigma^\pm$-stable, and shifting a configuration of exact period $k$ produces a configuration of exact period $k$. Iterating, $\ (\sigma^-)^{k} s = s(\,\cdot + k\,)$, and $F_g^{\,m} \circ \sigma^- = \sigma^- \circ F_g^{\,m}$ for all $m$.

**Lemma 3.5 (Transitivity of translation).** For $n \ge 1$ and any $i, j \in \mathbb{Z}/n$ there is $k \in \mathbb{N}$ with $i + k = j$; explicitly $k = (j-i)^{\mathrm{val}}$, the canonical representative in $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$.

Lemma 3.5 is the engine that converts a one-step local implication into a global statement about the whole lattice. It is used in every propagation theorem below.

## 4. The singleton criterion and its census

**Definition 4.1.** A local rule $g$ is *right-transitive at zero* if
$$\forall a,c \in \mathbb{F}_2, \quad g(a,0,c) = 0 \implies c = 0.$$

**Lemma 4.2 (Zero propagation).** Let $g$ be right-transitive at zero, let $s \in V(g,n)$ and suppose $s(i) = 0$. Then $s(i+1) = 0$.

*Proof.* Fixedness at $i$ reads $g(s(i-1), s(i), s(i+1)) = s(i) = 0$; substituting $s(i) = 0$ gives $g(s(i-1), 0, s(i+1)) = 0$, and right-transitivity yields $s(i+1) = 0$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.3.** If $g(1,1,1) \ne 1$ and $n \ge 1$, then every $s \in V(g,n)$ has a vanishing coordinate.

*Proof.* If not, then $s \equiv 1$; fixedness at $0$ gives $g(1,1,1) = 1$, a contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 4.4 (Singleton Theorem).** Suppose $g$ is right-transitive at zero, $g(1,1,1) \ne 1$, and $g(0,0,0) = 0$. Then for every $n \ge 1$,
$$V(g,n) = \{0\}, \qquad \text{hence} \qquad \#V(g,n) = 1 .$$

*Proof sketch.* Let $s$ be fixed. By Lemma 4.3 pick $i_0$ with $s(i_0) = 0$. By induction on $k$, using Lemma 4.2, $s(i_0 + k) = 0$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$. By Lemma 3.5 every site is of this form, so $s = 0$. Conversely $0$ is fixed because $g(0,0,0) = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.5 (Bit characterisation).** A local rule $g$ satisfies the three hypotheses of Theorem 4.4 if and only if
$$g(0,0,0) = 0, \quad g(0,0,1) = 1, \quad g(1,0,1) = 1, \quad g(1,1,1) = 0,$$
i.e. if and only if bits $0, 1, 5, 7$ of its rule number are $0, 1, 1, 0$ respectively.

*Proof sketch.* Right-transitivity is the conjunction of the two implications with $c = 1$, namely $g(0,0,1) \ne 0$ and $g(1,0,1) \ne 0$; over $\mathbb{F}_2$, "$\ne 0$" is "$=1$". The implications with $c = 0$ are vacuous. $\square$

**Corollary 4.6 (Census).** Exactly $16$ of the $256$ elementary rules satisfy the criterion, the four remaining bits being free. They are
$$34,\ 38,\ 42,\ 46,\ 50,\ 54,\ 58,\ 62,\ 98,\ 102,\ 106,\ 110,\ 114,\ 118,\ 122,\ 126,$$
and each has $\#V(g,n) = 1$ for every $n \ge 1$.

**Corollary 4.7 (Rule 110).** Rule $110$ has $g_{110}(a,0,c) = c$, hence is right-transitive at zero; $g_{110}(1,1,1) = 0$ and $g_{110}(0,0,0) = 0$. Therefore $V(110, n) = \{0\}$ and $\#V(110,n) = 1$ for every $n \ge 1$.

**Remark 4.8 (The first negative result).** Rule $46$ lies in the same census. Since Theorem 4.5 shows the criterion depends on only four bits, rules $46$ and $110$ agree in every bit relevant to the fixed-point count, and $\#V(46,n) = \#V(110,n) = 1$ for all $n$. No statistic derived from fixed-point counts can separate them, although the standard qualitative taxonomy assigns them to different classes. This is our first indication that fixed points do not see universality.

## 5. Symmetries as conjugacies

**Definition 5.1.** For a local rule $g$ define the *mirror rule* and the *complement-dual rule* by
$$(\mathrm{mir}\, g)(a,b,c) = g(c,b,a), \qquad (\mathrm{dual}\, g)(a,b,c) = 1 + g(1+a, 1+b, 1+c).$$
On configurations define *reflection* $(\rho s)(i) = s(-i)$ and *complementation* $(\kappa s)(i) = 1 + s(i)$.

**Proposition 5.2 (Klein four-group).** $\mathrm{mir}$ and $\mathrm{dual}$ are commuting involutions on the set of local rules; likewise $\rho$ and $\kappa$ are commuting involutions of $\mathrm{Config}(n)$, in particular injective. Thus $\{\mathrm{id}, \mathrm{mir}, \mathrm{dual}, \mathrm{mir}\circ\mathrm{dual}\}$ is a Klein four-group acting on the $256$ rules.

*Proof sketch.* $\mathrm{mir}(\mathrm{mir}\, g) = g$ holds definitionally. For $\mathrm{dual}$, expand and use $1 + (1+x) = x$, valid because $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{F}_2$. Commutation is again definitional since reflection acts on positions and complementation on values. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Conjugacy).** For every local rule $g$ and configuration $s$,
$$F_{\mathrm{mir}\, g}(\rho s) = \rho(F_g s), \qquad F_{\mathrm{dual}\, g}(\kappa s) = \kappa (F_g s).$$

*Proof sketch.* For the first, evaluate at $i$: the left side is $g(s(-(i+1)), s(-i), s(-(i-1)))$ and the right is $g(s(-i-1), s(-i), s(-i+1))$; these agree since $-(i+1) = -i-1$ and $-(i-1) = -i+1$. For the second, expand $\mathrm{dual}$ and cancel double complements. $\square$

Theorem 5.3 says the two operations are genuine *changes of coordinates* for the dynamics, not merely relabellings of truth tables. Every dynamical invariant transports.

**Corollary 5.4 (Invariance of the count).** For every $n$ and every $g$,
$$\#V(\mathrm{mir}\, g, n) = \#V(\mathrm{dual}\, g, n) = \#V(g,n).$$

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 5.3, $s \in V(g,n) \iff \rho s \in V(\mathrm{mir}\, g, n)$, so $V(\mathrm{mir}\, g, n) = \rho(V(g,n))$; $\rho$ is injective, so the cardinalities agree. Similarly for $\kappa$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.5 (Invariance of the orbit spectrum).** For every $s$,
$$\mathrm{per}\big(F_{\mathrm{mir}\, g},\, \rho s\big) = \mathrm{per}(F_g, s), \qquad \mathrm{per}\big(F_{\mathrm{dual}\, g},\, \kappa s\big) = \mathrm{per}(F_g, s),$$
where $\mathrm{per}$ denotes the *exact* (minimal) period. Thus the entire multiset of exact orbit lengths is a class function for the Klein four-group.

Corollary 5.5 is a consequence of the following abstract lemma, which we isolate because it is used again in §6.

**Lemma 5.6 (Injective semiconjugacy preserves exact periods).** Let $f : A \to A$, $u : B \to B$ and let $e : A \to B$ be injective with $e \circ f = u \circ e$. Then $\mathrm{per}(u, e(x)) = \mathrm{per}(f, x)$ for every $x \in A$ (both sides being $0$ when $x$ is not periodic).

*Proof sketch.* Induction gives $e(f^{k}(x)) = u^{k}(e(x))$. Hence $u^{k}(e(x)) = e(x)$ iff $e(f^k(x)) = e(x)$ iff $f^k(x) = x$, by injectivity. The two sets of periods coincide, so each minimal period divides the other. $\square$

**Definition 5.7.** A rule $g$ satisfies the *extended singleton criterion* if $g$, $\mathrm{mir}\, g$, $\mathrm{dual}\, g$ or $\mathrm{mir}(\mathrm{dual}\, g)$ satisfies the criterion of Theorem 4.4.

**Theorem 5.8 (Extended singleton theorem and census).** If $g$ satisfies the extended criterion, then $\#V(g,n) = 1$ for every $n \ge 1$. Exactly $48$ of the $256$ rules satisfy it — three times the $16$ detected by the purely local criterion, which is a subset.

*Proof sketch.* Combine Theorem 4.4 with Corollary 5.4 in each of the four cases. The census is a finite check over the $256$ rule numbers. $\square$

**Corollary 5.9 (The partners of Rule 110).** $\mathrm{rule}(124) = \mathrm{mir}(g_{110})$, $\mathrm{rule}(137) = \mathrm{dual}(g_{110})$ and $\mathrm{rule}(193) = \mathrm{mir}(\mathrm{dual}(g_{110}))$. Consequently
$$\#V(124,n) = \#V(137,n) = \#V(193,n) = 1 \quad \text{for every } n \ge 1,$$
even though none of these three rules satisfies the local criterion of Theorem 4.4. Rule $124$ additionally inherits the two-cycles of Rule $110$ established in §8: whenever $4 \mid n$ it has a configuration of exact period $2$.

**Remark 5.10.** Since counting is a class function, any classification statement about fixed points should be phrased on the $88$ symmetry classes rather than on rule numbers.

## 6. Periodic descent

Fix $d \mid n$ and let $\pi : \mathbb{Z}/n \to \mathbb{Z}/d$ be the canonical ring homomorphism.

**Definition 6.1.** The *pullback* $\mathrm{pull} : \mathrm{Config}(d) \to \mathrm{Config}(n)$ is $(\mathrm{pull}\, t)(i) = t(\pi(i))$. Its image consists exactly of the $d$-periodic configurations.

**Theorem 6.2 (Simulation embedding).** For every local rule $g$ and every $d \mid n$, the map $\mathrm{pull}$ is injective and intertwines the dynamics:
$$F_g(\mathrm{pull}\, t) = \mathrm{pull}(F_g\, t).$$
Consequently $\mathrm{pull}$ is a morphism of dynamical systems: $t \in V(g,d) \iff \mathrm{pull}\, t \in V(g,n)$, and by Lemma 5.6,
$$\mathrm{per}(F_g, \mathrm{pull}\, t) = \mathrm{per}(F_g, t).$$

*Proof sketch.* $\pi$ is a ring homomorphism, so $\pi(i \pm 1) = \pi(i) \pm 1$; the local rule therefore sees exactly the same neighbourhood after pullback. Injectivity holds because $\pi$ is surjective. $\square$

**Corollary 6.3 (Divisibility monotonicity).** For every rule $g$ and every $d \mid n$, $\#V(g,d) \le \#V(g,n)$. Likewise every exact orbit length occurring at size $d$ occurs at size $n$.

**Lemma 6.4 (Descent).** Suppose $s \in \mathrm{Config}(n)$ satisfies $s(i + d) = s(i)$ for all $i$, where $d \mid n$. Then $s = \mathrm{pull}\, t$ for some $t \in \mathrm{Config}(d)$.

*Proof sketch.* Set $t(x) = s(\bar{x})$ where $\bar x \in \{0,\dots,d-1\}$ is the canonical representative. Writing $\mathrm{val}(i) = (\mathrm{val}(i) \bmod d) + d\lfloor \mathrm{val}(i)/d \rfloor$ and iterating the $d$-periodicity hypothesis $\lfloor \mathrm{val}(i)/d\rfloor$ times gives $s(i) = t(\pi(i))$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.5 (Count invariance under descent).** Let $d \mid n$ and suppose every $s \in V(g,n)$ is $d$-periodic. Then
$$\#V(g,n) = \#V(g,d).$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 6.4 and Theorem 6.2, $V(g,n) = \mathrm{pull}(V(g,d))$; apply injectivity. $\square$

**Lemma 6.6 (Coprime collapse).** If $d$ is invertible modulo $n$ — equivalently $\gcd(d,n) = 1$ — then any $d$-periodic $s \in \mathrm{Config}(n)$ is constant.

*Proof sketch.* If $d v = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$, then for any $i,j$ set $u = v(j-i)$, so $d u = j - i$. Iterating $d$-periodicity $\mathrm{val}(u)$ times from $i$ gives $s(i + du) = s(i)$, i.e. $s(j) = s(i)$. $\square$

Theorem 6.5 and Lemma 6.6 together cover all $n$ once the "period" $d$ of a rule's fixed-point equation is known: sizes divisible by $d$ reduce to the single finite computation at size $d$, and sizes coprime to $d$ collapse to constants. For $d$ prime, as in all our applications, these two cases are exhaustive.

## 7. Exact counting functions

### 7.1 Rule 150

**Lemma 7.1.** $s \in V(150,n)$ if and only if $s(i-1) = s(i+1)$ for all $i$, i.e. if and only if $s$ is $2$-periodic. Every constant configuration is fixed.

*Proof.* The fixed-point equation is $s(i-1) + s(i) + s(i+1) = s(i)$, i.e. $s(i-1) + s(i+1) = 0$, i.e. $s(i-1) = s(i+1)$. Constants satisfy $c+c+c = c$ since $2c = 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2.** For every $n \ge 1$,
$$\#V(150,n) = \begin{cases} 4, & 2 \mid n,\\ 2, & 2 \nmid n.\end{cases}$$

*Proof sketch.* If $n$ is even, Theorem 6.5 with $d = 2$ gives $\#V(150,n) = \#V(150,2)$, and direct inspection of the four configurations of length $2$ shows all are fixed: $\#V(150,2) = 4$. If $n$ is odd, $2$ is invertible mod $n$, so by Lemma 6.6 every fixed configuration is constant; both constants are fixed and they are distinct, giving $2$. $\square$

### 7.2 Rule 90

**Lemma 7.3 ($3$-periodicity).** Every $s \in V(90,n)$ satisfies $s(i+3) = s(i)$.

*Proof sketch.* The fixed-point equation is the $\mathbb{F}_2$ Fibonacci recurrence $s(i-1) + s(i+1) = s(i)$, equivalently $s(i+1) = s(i) + s(i-1)$. Applying it at $i+1$ and $i+2$ and adding, the middle terms cancel because $2 = 0$, leaving $s(i+3) = s(i)$. (Equivalently: the Fibonacci recurrence over $\mathbb{F}_2$ has Pisano period $3$.) $\square$

**Theorem 7.4.** For every $n \ge 1$,
$$\#V(90,n) = \begin{cases} 4, & 3 \mid n,\\ 1, & 3 \nmid n.\end{cases}$$

*Proof sketch.* If $3 \mid n$, Theorem 6.5 with $d = 3$ reduces to $\#V(90,3) = 4$, a check over $8$ configurations. If $3 \nmid n$, then $3$ is invertible mod $n$, so by Lemmas 7.3 and 6.6 every fixed configuration is constant; the equation at a constant $c$ reads $c + c = c$, i.e. $c = 0$. Hence $V(90,n) = \{0\}$. $\square$

### 7.3 Rule 30

Rule $30$ is nonlinear, so no recurrence argument applies directly; nevertheless the same descent machinery works after a case analysis.

**Lemma 7.5 (Cleared fixed-point relation).** $s \in V(30,n)$ if and only if for all $i$,
$$s(i-1) + s(i+1) + s(i)\,s(i+1) = 0 .$$

*Proof.* The equation $s(i-1) + s(i) + s(i+1) + s(i)s(i+1) = s(i)$ simplifies by cancelling $s(i)$. $\square$

**Lemma 7.6 ($2$-periodicity).** Every $s \in V(30,n)$ satisfies $s(i+2) = s(i)$.

*Proof sketch.* Apply Lemma 7.5 at $i+1$: $s(i) + s(i+2) + s(i+1)s(i+2) = 0$. If $s(i+1) = 0$ this reads $s(i) = s(i+2)$ directly. If $s(i+1) = 1$ it gives $s(i) = 0$; applying the relation at $i+2$ then shows $s(i+2) = 1$ is impossible (it would force $1 = 0$), so $s(i+2) = 0 = s(i)$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.7.** For every $n \ge 1$,
$$\#V(30,n) = \begin{cases} 3, & 2 \mid n,\\ 1, & 2 \nmid n.\end{cases}$$

*Proof sketch.* For even $n$, descend to $d = 2$: the fixed configurations of length $2$ are $00$, $01$, $10$, giving $3$ (the configuration $11$ fails). For odd $n$, $2$ is invertible, so every fixed configuration is constant by Lemmas 7.6 and 6.6; a constant $c$ must satisfy $c + c + c^2 = 0$, i.e. $c^2 = 0$ since $2c = 0$, hence $c = 0$. $\square$

**Remark 7.8.** Rule $30$ is the standard chaotic exemplar, yet its fixed-point count is as tame and as periodic in $n$ as those of the additive rules. What the counts record is the period of the recurrence cutting out the fixed set — $2$, $3$, $2$ for rules $150$, $90$, $30$ — an invariant of the *linearisation* of the rule, not of its dynamical complexity.

### 7.4 A count equal to two: the traffic rule

**Lemma 7.9 (Leftward transitivity of the lattice).** Let $P$ be a property of sites such that $P(i) \Rightarrow P(i-1)$ for all $i$, and suppose $P(i_0)$ holds for some $i_0$. Then $P$ holds at every site.

*Proof sketch.* Induction gives $P(i_0 - k)$ for all $k \in \mathbb{N}$; every site is of that form by the mirror of Lemma 3.5. $\square$

**Definition 7.10 (Constant-pair criterion).** A local rule $g$ satisfies the *constant-pair criterion* if
1. *(zeros travel left)* $g(a,0,c) = 0 \Rightarrow a = 0$;
2. *(ones travel right)* $g(a,1,c) = 1 \Rightarrow c = 1$;
3. $g(0,0,0) = 0$ and $g(1,1,1) = 1$.

**Theorem 7.11 (Constant-Pair Theorem).** If $g$ satisfies the constant-pair criterion then for every $n \ge 1$
$$V(g,n) = \{\, \mathbf{0},\ \mathbf{1} \,\}, \qquad \#V(g,n) = 2,$$
where $\mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{1}$ are the two constant configurations.

*Proof sketch.* Let $s$ be fixed. If some coordinate vanishes, say $s(i) = 0$, then fixedness at $i$ gives $g(s(i-1), 0, s(i+1)) = 0$, so by condition 1, $s(i-1) = 0$; Lemma 7.9 propagates this around the lattice and $s = \mathbf{0}$. If no coordinate vanishes then $s = \mathbf{1}$. Conversely conditions 3 make both constants fixed, and they are distinct. $\square$

**Corollary 7.12 (Census).** Exactly four elementary rules satisfy the constant-pair criterion, namely $176$, $178$, $184$ and $186$. In particular the traffic rule $184$ has exactly two fixed configurations on every cyclic lattice, and so does its mirror image (by Corollary 5.4).

**Remark 7.13.** Condition 2 of the criterion is not used in the proof of $V \subseteq \{\mathbf 0, \mathbf 1\}$; it is retained because it is the natural dual of condition 1, it is the transparent statement of the traffic interpretation ("cars advance into empty space ahead"), and it is what makes the criterion invariant in spirit under the mirror symmetry. Under the traffic reading of Rule $184$ — $1$ for an occupied cell, $0$ for empty road, a car advancing exactly when the cell ahead is free — the theorem says that on a ring road the only stationary traffic states are the empty road and the complete jam, at every ring size.

**Remark 7.14 (Sufficiency, not necessity).** Both propagation criteria are sufficient conditions only. There exist rules with $\#V \equiv 2$ that fail the constant-pair criterion (for instance rules whose two fixed configurations form a nonconstant shift orbit), and enumeration shows exactly $78$ rules have $\#V(g,n) = 1$ for all $n \le 7$, of which only $48$ are certified for all $n$ by Theorem 5.8. Closing the remaining gap is Conjecture 10.1.

## 8. Orbits: what fixed points miss

**Theorem 8.1 (Universal existence of periodic orbits).** For every local rule $g$ and every $n \ge 1$ there exist $s \in \mathrm{Config}(n)$ and $k > 0$ with $F_g^{\,k}(s) = s$.

*Proof sketch.* The orbit $k \mapsto F_g^{\,k}(0)$ takes values in a finite set, so $F_g^{\,a}(0) = F_g^{\,b}(0)$ for some $a < b$; then $F_g^{\,a}(0)$ is periodic with period $b - a > 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.2 (Rule 51).** Rule $51$, $g(a,b,c) = 1+b$, is an involution on $\mathrm{Config}(n)$ with no fixed configuration whatsoever: $\#V(51,n) = 0$ for every $n \ge 1$. Every configuration has exact period exactly $2$.

*Proof sketch.* $F_{51}$ is pointwise complementation, hence involutive since $2 = 0$. It fixes no configuration since $1 + s(0) = s(0)$ is impossible. Hence the exact period divides $2$ and is not $1$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (Rule 170: unbounded orbit lengths).** Let $\delta$ be the configuration with $\delta(0) = 1$ and $\delta(i) = 0$ otherwise. Under Rule $170$ (the left shift), $\delta$ is $k$-periodic if and only if $n \mid k$; its exact period is therefore $n$.

*Proof sketch.* By Proposition 3.2 and Corollary 3.4, $F_{170}^{\,k} s = s(\cdot + k)$. Evaluating at $0$, $F_{170}^{\,k}\delta = \delta$ forces $\delta(k) = 1$, i.e. $k = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$. Conversely $n \mid k$ makes the translation trivial. $\square$

So the fixed-point count of Rule $170$ is the constant $2$ (its fixed configurations are exactly the constants) while its exact orbit lengths grow linearly in $n$: the fixed-point statistic is genuinely lossy.

**Theorem 8.4 (An exact two-cycle of Rule 110).** Let $t = 1110 \in \mathrm{Config}(4)$. Then $F_{110}^{\,2}(t) = t$ and $F_{110}(t) \ne t$, so $\mathrm{per}(F_{110}, t) = 2$. Consequently, by Theorem 6.2, for every $n$ with $4 \mid n$ there is a configuration $s \in \mathrm{Config}(n)$ with $\mathrm{per}(F_{110}, s) = 2$, and $s \ne 0$.

*Proof sketch.* The two finite facts about $t$ are direct computations on the $4$-site lattice. Transport to size $n$ is by the simulation embedding, which preserves exact periods. That $s \ne 0$ follows since $0$ is the unique fixed configuration and has exact period $1 \ne 2$. $\square$

Explicitly, on $\mathrm{Config}(4)$ the configurations satisfying $F_{110}^{\,2}(s) = s$ are $0000$, $1110$, $1011$, $1101$, $0111$, forming the fixed point together with two $2$-cycles $1110 \leftrightarrow 1011$ and $1101 \leftrightarrow 0111$. These are the smallest glider-like objects of Rule $110$, and their existence at *every* size divisible by $4$ is the content of Theorem 8.4.

**Theorem 8.5 (Separation results).** For every $n \ge 1$:
1. $\#V(90,n) \ne \#V(110,n)$ if and only if $3 \mid n$;
2. $\#V(110,n) < \#V(150,n)$ always;
3. $\#V(184,n) = 2 \ne 1 = \#V(110,n)$ always;
4. $\#V(46,n) = \#V(110,n)$ always, and likewise for the other $15$ rules of the census of Corollary 4.6 and for the three symmetry partners $124, 137, 193$.

Items 1–3 show the statistic is not useless; item 4 shows precisely what it cannot do. Universality is invisible to it.

## 9. The polynomial and scheme-theoretic model

The counting results above can be recast as statements about rational points of affine schemes over $\mathbb{F}_2$, which is the natural home for uniform-in-$n$ statements.

**Definition 9.1 (Rule polynomials).** For a local rule $g$ and a site $i \in \mathbb{Z}/n$ define
$$P_{g,i} \;=\; \sum_{a,b,c \in \mathbb{F}_2} g(a,b,c)\,\big(1 + a + X_{i-1}\big)\big(1 + b + X_i\big)\big(1 + c + X_{i+1}\big) \;\in\; \mathbb{F}_2[X_j : j \in \mathbb{Z}/n].$$
The factor $1 + a + X_j$ is the $\mathbb{F}_2$ indicator of the event $X_j = a$.

**Theorem 9.2 (Evaluation is the update map).** For every configuration $s$ and site $i$,
$$P_{g,i}(s) = (F_g s)(i).$$

*Proof sketch.* Expanding, the identity reduces to the finite statement
$$\sum_{a,b,c} g(a,b,c)(1+a+x)(1+b+y)(1+c+z) = g(x,y,z)$$
for all $x,y,z \in \mathbb{F}_2$, which holds because $(1+a+x)$ equals $1$ when $x = a$ and $0$ otherwise, so exactly one summand survives. $\square$

**Theorem 9.3 (Polynomial shift equivariance).** Renaming the variables by $X_j \mapsto X_{j+i}$ carries $P_{g,0}$ to $P_{g,i}$.

**Definition 9.4 (Fixed-point ideal).** Let
$$I(g,n) = \big( \{P_{g,i} - X_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{Z}/n} \;\cup\; \{X_i^2 - X_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{Z}/n} \big) \subseteq \mathbb{F}_2[X_j : j \in \mathbb{Z}/n].$$
The second family is the Boolean relation set, forcing the coordinates to be bits.

**Theorem 9.5 (Rational points).** A configuration $s$ satisfies $P(s) = 0$ for every $P \in I(g,n)$ if and only if $s \in V(g,n)$. That is, the $\mathbb{F}_2$-points of the fixed-point ideal are exactly the fixed configurations.

*Proof sketch.* Forwards: apply the hypothesis to the generator $P_{g,i} - X_i$ and use Theorem 9.2 to get $(F_g s)(i) = s(i)$. Backwards: each generator lies in the kernel of evaluation at $s$ — the update generators by Theorem 9.2, the Boolean generators because $c^2 = c$ for $c \in \mathbb{F}_2$ — and the kernel is an ideal, so it contains the span. $\square$

**Corollary 9.6.** $I(110,n) \ne (1)$ for every $n \ge 1$: the coordinate ring of the fixed-point scheme of Rule $110$ is nonzero, since $0 \in V(110,n)$ is a rational point.

In this language, Theorems 7.2, 7.4, 7.7 compute the number of $\mathbb{F}_2$-rational points of an explicit family of affine schemes whose defining equations depend on $n$ only through the cyclic index group, and the periodicity of the point-counts in $n$ is the arithmetic shadow of that dependence.

## 10. Algorithms

Three algorithms underlie the computational side of this work.

**A. Transfer-matrix point count.** The fixed configurations of $g$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n$ are exactly the closed walks of length $n$ in the *fixed de Bruijn graph* $D(g)$: vertices are the four overlapping pairs $(a,b) \in \mathbb{F}_2^2$, and there is an edge $(a,b) \to (b,c)$ precisely when $g(a,b,c) = b$. Writing $M(g)$ for its $4\times 4$ adjacency matrix,
$$\#V(g,n) = \operatorname{tr} M(g)^n .$$
This computes any single count in $O(\log n)$ matrix multiplications of fixed size $4$, versus $\Theta(n 2^n)$ for brute-force enumeration, and it explains structurally every phenomenon above: a constant count $1$ corresponds to a graph with exactly one cycle, and a count periodic in $n$ with period $p$ corresponds to a spectrum of roots of unity of order dividing $p$ together with nilpotent directions.

**B. Descent certification.** To prove a formula for $\#V(g,\cdot)$ valid for all $n$: (1) find the smallest $d$ such that every fixed configuration is $d$-periodic — over $\mathbb{F}_2$ this is the period of the recurrence, detectable from $M(g)$; (2) compute $\#V(g,d)$ by enumeration of $2^d$ configurations; (3) for $n$ with $d \mid n$, conclude $\#V(g,n) = \#V(g,d)$; (4) for $n$ coprime to $d$, solve the constant case by hand. For prime $d$ the two cases are exhaustive.

**C. Symmetry-closed census.** For each of the $256$ rule numbers, test the local singleton criterion on the rule and on its three symmetry partners; accept if any passes. The test inspects four truth-table entries per rule, so the census costs $O(256 \cdot 4 \cdot 4)$ elementary operations. It certifies $48$ rules.

## 11. Discussion

The results assemble into a single methodological point. There are three genuinely different reasons why $\#V(g,n)$ can be determined for all $n$ at once:

* a **local** reason (an entry of the truth table forces a bit to propagate, and cyclicity closes the argument);
* a **conjugacy** reason (the rule is a coordinate change away from one already understood);
* an **arithmetic** reason (the fixed-point equations are a recurrence of period $d$, and divisibility of $n$ by $d$ decides the count).

Fifty-two of the $256$ rules are covered by the first two mechanisms alone; the third handles the additive rules $90$ and $150$ and, more interestingly, the nonlinear Rule $30$.

Against this, the negative results are sharp. The count is a class function for a group of order four; it depends on Rule $110$ only through four bits of its table, which it shares with fifteen other rules including a rule of a different qualitative class; and it is constant in $n$ for the shift rule $170$, whose orbit lengths grow linearly. Any claim of a correlation between fixed-point statistics and a qualitative classification of automata must therefore specify at minimum: the boundary convention, the range of sizes, the exact statistic, and the classification data — and must survive the fact that no such statistic distinguishes Rule $46$ from Rule $110$ at any size.

The constructive alternative is the orbit spectrum. It is finer (it distinguishes Rules $51$ and $110$, which the fixed-point count also distinguishes, but also Rules $170$ and $240$ from constant-count rules at large $n$), it is still a class function for the symmetry group, and it transports along divisibility of lattice sizes by an *injective simulation embedding*. Since universality is a statement about unbounded space-time evolution, embeddings and orbit structures are the right invariants to pursue.

## 12. Future directions

The following conjectures are the sharpest questions the results raise; each is falsifiable by a finite computation or a single counterexample.

**Conjecture 12.1 (Finite certification of the singleton property).** For an elementary rule $g$ the following are equivalent: (i) $\#V(g,n) = 1$ for every $n \ge 1$; (ii) $\#V(g,n) = 1$ for $n = 1,\dots,7$; (iii) the only bi-infinite $g$-fixed word is the all-zero word. Enumeration shows that the set of rules satisfying (ii) has exactly $78$ elements; $48$ of them are certified by the symmetry-closed criterion, so the conjecture predicts exactly $30$ further rules whose unit count needs a non-local argument. The key insight is that $\#V(g,n) = \operatorname{tr} M(g)^n$ for the $4 \times 4$ fixed de Bruijn matrix of §10, so a count that stays $1$ forever means that graph has a unique cycle — a decidable property of a four-vertex graph. Hence a finite test must exist; the only question is how small it can be.

**Further programme.**

1. Derive additional named singleton theorems as concrete corollaries of the right-transitivity criterion, with periodic left and right shifts as explicit primitives.
2. Replace Boolean algebraic normal forms by polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_2$ throughout and prove equality of polynomial evaluation with the global update map in that setting.
3. Specify the fixed-point coordinate ring using both the update equations and the Boolean relations $x_i^2 - x_i$; study its Krull dimension separately from its number of $\mathbb{F}_2$-rational points.
4. Formalise all $256$ rule tables from rule numbers and prove that rule-number decoding is a bijection with the set of local rules.
5. Compute fixed-point counts for every rule and bounded lattice size, then compare those finite invariants with a precisely encoded qualitative classification. Any correlation claim should state the boundary convention, size range, statistic, and classification data explicitly.
6. Study periodic orbits of length greater than one. Universality concerns unbounded space-time evolution, so orbit structure and simulation embeddings are more relevant invariants than fixed points alone.
7. Formulate any sheaf-theoretic construction explicitly: give the site, topology, restriction maps, and the intended theorem relating global sections to configurations, before comparing section structure across rules.

## Abstract

We give a self-contained algebraic development of the Weil pairing on the $n$-torsion of an elliptic curve, and of the pairing-based BLS signature scheme built on it, in which every cryptographic property is derived from a minimal set of axioms: bi-additivity together with alternation, $e(P,P)=1$.

Our first group of results is structural. We show that on a group generated by two elements, alternation and bi-additivity *force* the determinant form: $e(a e_1 + b e_2, c e_1 + d e_2) = \zeta^{ad-bc}$ with $\zeta = e(e_1,e_2)$. Over the model group $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ with values in $\mu_n$ we obtain a complete classification: every alternating pairing is $\zeta^{u\det}$ for a unique scalar $u \in \mathbb{Z}/n$; it is nondegenerate if and only if $u \in (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$; and every nondegenerate alternating pairing is the pullback of the determinant pairing along a group automorphism (symplectic normal form). We prove a **rank obstruction**: a nondegenerate alternating pairing on cyclic torsion forces the torsion to be trivial, so rank two is not a convention but a theorem. Conversely, transporting the determinant form along any isomorphism $E[n] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ produces a genuine Weil pairing, establishing consistency and non-vacuity of the axioms.

Our second group of results is cryptanalytic. We prove the degree law $e(mP,mQ) = e(P,Q)^{m^2}$ and, more generally, that a torsion endomorphism with matrix $\binom{a\ b}{c\ d}$ scales the pairing by $ad-bc$. We prove the MOV embedding, that $a \mapsto e(aP,Q)$ is injective modulo $n$ when $\operatorname{ord} e(P,Q) = n$, and that decisional Diffie–Hellman is *decided* by a publicly computable pairing test — hence unusable as a security assumption — while the same test is vacuous on a single cyclic subgroup.

Our third group of results concerns BLS signatures. We first exhibit an **obstruction**: the natural symmetric model, which posits one generator $G$ and injectivity of $P \mapsto e(P,G)$, is satisfiable only when the $n$-torsion is trivial; every theorem proved in that model is vacuous. We then develop the full theory over a corrected, inhabited asymmetric model with two generators $g_1, g_2$ and the requirement $\operatorname{ord} e(g_1,g_2) = n$: correctness; an exact characterisation of accepted signatures as the congruence class $s \equiv sk\cdot h \pmod n$, hence uniqueness; an existential-unforgeability reduction in which a forgery on a programmed fresh message *equals* the computational Diffie–Hellman target; aggregate correctness and aggregate uniqueness; an aggregate-to-single forgery extraction, giving a tight reduction of aggregate unforgeability to computational Diffie–Hellman; soundness and completeness of weighted batch verification; an exact compression statement, $N^m \twoheadrightarrow N$; and an explicit rogue-key attack showing that the extraction hypothesis of registered keys cannot be dropped.

**Keywords.** Weil pairing; alternating bilinear form; symplectic normal form; elliptic curve torsion; BLS signatures; aggregate signatures; computational Diffie–Hellman; MOV reduction; rogue-key attack.

## 1. Introduction

Pairings occupy an unusual position in cryptography. They are simultaneously the enabling mechanism for a family of protocols with no known alternative — identity-based encryption, short signatures, succinct arguments — and the enabling mechanism for the most effective structural attack on the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. Both roles derive from a single equation,
$$e(aP, bQ) = e(P,Q)^{ab},$$
which converts an *unknown product of secret exponents* into a *publicly checkable equality*.

The Weil pairing on the $n$-torsion $E[n]$ of an elliptic curve is classically constructed from divisors: one takes functions $f_P, f_Q$ with divisors $n(P) - n(O)$ and $n(Q)-n(O)$ and forms $e_n(P,Q) = f_P(D_Q)/f_Q(D_P)$. That construction is geometric and its properties — bilinearity, alternation, Galois equivariance, nondegeneracy — are proved by function-field arguments.

The present paper takes the opposite route. We work axiomatically, with the weakest possible interface, and ask how much of the theory is *forced*. The answer is: essentially all of it. In particular the determinant, which never appears in the axioms, is a theorem; and the rank-two structure of the torsion, usually imported from the classical structure theorem, is also a theorem. This is not merely aesthetic. Because the derivations are so short, they can be checked against the security definitions they support — and doing so uncovers that the most natural way to state the BLS security model is inconsistent.

### 1.1 Contributions

1. **Forcing of the determinant form** (Theorem 3.5) and the resulting **complete classification** of alternating pairings on $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ (Theorems 4.2, 4.4, 4.6).
2. **Rank obstruction** (Theorem 5.2): nondegeneracy is incompatible with cyclic torsion.
3. **Existence** (Theorem 5.5): the axioms are inhabited by a pairing transported from the determinant model.
4. **Cryptanalysis**: degree law and endomorphism equivariance (Theorems 6.1, 6.3), MOV embedding (Theorem 6.5), and the collapse of decisional Diffie–Hellman (Theorem 6.7) with its cyclic-subgroup blind spot (Proposition 6.9).
5. **An obstruction theorem for the symmetric BLS model** (Theorem 7.2), showing that the injectivity axiom forces trivial torsion.
6. **The corrected asymmetric model** and the complete BLS theory over it: correctness, an exact verification criterion and uniqueness (Theorems 8.4, 8.5), the CDH reduction (Theorems 8.7, 8.8), aggregation (Theorems 9.2, 9.3), forgery extraction and the aggregate CDH reduction (Theorems 9.4, 9.5), batch verification (Theorem 9.7), compression (Theorem 9.9), and the rogue-key attack (Theorem 10.1).
7. **Exhaustive finite verification** of the key identities over small models (Section 11).

## 2. Setting and notation

Throughout, $A$ denotes an additively written abelian group, and $\mu$ a multiplicatively written commutative group. For a Weierstrass curve $W$ over a field $F$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, we write
$$E[n] = \{ P \in W(F) : nP = O \}$$
for the $n$-torsion subgroup, which is an abelian group under the chord-and-tangent law. We write $\mathbb{Z}/n$ for the integers modulo $n$ and $\mu_n$ for a cyclic group of order dividing $n$; concretely we may realise $\mu_n$ as $\mathbb{Z}/n$ written multiplicatively, an identification we use freely in the model computations.

$\operatorname{ord}(x)$ denotes the order of a group element. $a \equiv b \pmod n$ is written $a \equiv b \ [n]$.

## 3. Alternating pairings and the forcing of the determinant

### Definition 3.1 (Alternating pairing)

An **alternating pairing** on $A$ with values in $\mu$ is a map $e : A \times A \to \mu$ that is additive in each argument,
$$e(x+y, z) = e(x,z)\,e(y,z), \qquad e(x, y+z) = e(x,y)\,e(x,z),$$
and satisfies the **alternation** axiom $e(x,x) = 1$ for all $x \in A$.

Bi-additivity alone yields $e(0,y) = e(x,0) = 1$ and, by induction and passage to inverses, full $\mathbb{Z}$-bilinearity:
$$e(kx, y) = e(x,y)^k = e(x, ky) \quad (k \in \mathbb{Z}). \tag{3.2}$$

### Proposition 3.3 (Skew-symmetry)

*For all $x,y$: $\;e(y,x) = e(x,y)^{-1}$, and $e(x-y,z) = e(x,z)\,e(y,z)^{-1}$.*

**Proof.** Expand $1 = e(x+y, x+y)$ by bi-additivity into $e(x,x)e(x,y)e(y,x)e(y,y)$. The outer factors are $1$ by alternation, so $e(x,y)e(y,x)=1$. The second identity is (3.2) with $k=-1$ combined with additivity. $\square$

Note the logical direction: antisymmetry is *derived*, not assumed. Over groups of even order the two conditions are genuinely different, alternation being the stronger.

### Proposition 3.4 (Blindness on cyclic subgroups)

*For all $a,b \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $x \in A$, $\;e(ax, bx) = 1$. Consequently, if $A$ is cyclic then $e$ is identically $1$.*

**Proof.** By (3.2) twice, $e(ax,bx) = e(x,x)^{ab} = 1$. If $A = \langle g\rangle$, write $x = ag$, $y = bg$. $\square$

### Theorem 3.5 (The determinant is forced)

*Let $e_1, e_2 \in A$ and put $\zeta = e(e_1,e_2)$. Then for all integers $a,b,c,d$,*
$$e\big(a e_1 + b e_2,\; c e_1 + d e_2\big) \;=\; \zeta^{\,ad - bc}.$$

**Proof.** Expand both arguments by bi-additivity into four terms:
$$e(ae_1, ce_1)\; e(ae_1, de_2)\; e(be_2, ce_1)\; e(be_2, de_2).$$
The first and fourth are $1$ by Proposition 3.4. By (3.2) the second is $\zeta^{ad}$ and the third is $e(e_2,e_1)^{bc} = \zeta^{-bc}$ by Proposition 3.3. Multiplying gives $\zeta^{ad-bc}$. $\square$

### Corollary 3.6 (Rigidity)

*Two alternating pairings that agree on a pair $(e_1,e_2)$ agree on the entire subgroup $\langle e_1, e_2\rangle$.*

Theorem 3.5 is the technical heart of the paper. Everything that follows is an application of it, a specialisation of it, or a consequence of the fact that its exponent is a determinant.

## 4. Complete classification over $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$

Fix $n \geq 1$ and work on $A = (\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ with values in $\mu_n \cong \mathbb{Z}/n$ (multiplicatively written), with standard basis $\varepsilon_1 = (1,0)$, $\varepsilon_2 = (0,1)$.

### Definition 4.1 (Determinant pairing)

The **determinant form** is $\det(v,w) = v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \in \mathbb{Z}/n$, and the **determinant pairing** is $e_{\det}(v,w) = \zeta_0^{\det(v,w)}$ where $\zeta_0$ is the canonical generator of $\mu_n$. It is bi-additive (each slot is $\mathbb{Z}/n$-linear in coordinates), alternating ($\det(v,v)=0$), and $n$-torsion valued.

### Theorem 4.2 (Classification)

*Let $e$ be any alternating pairing on $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ with values in $\mu_n$, and let $u \in \mathbb{Z}/n$ be the exponent of its value on the standard basis, $e(\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2) = \zeta_0^{u}$. Then for all $v,w$,*
$$e(v,w) = \zeta_0^{\,u\,\det(v,w)}.$$

**Proof.** Write $v = v_1\varepsilon_1 + v_2\varepsilon_2$ and $w = w_1\varepsilon_1 + w_2\varepsilon_2$ with integer representatives. Theorem 3.5 gives $e(v,w) = e(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)^{v_1w_2 - v_2w_1}$; substituting $e(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2) = \zeta_0^u$ and reducing the exponent modulo $n$ (legitimate since $\zeta_0$ has order dividing $n$) gives the claim. $\square$

So the moduli space of alternating pairings on the model group is a single copy of $\mathbb{Z}/n$.

### Definition 4.3 (Nondegeneracy)

$e$ is **nondegenerate on the left** if $e(v,w) = 1$ for all $w$ implies $v = 0$; nondegeneracy on the right is defined symmetrically and, by skew-symmetry, is equivalent.

### Theorem 4.4 (Nondegeneracy criterion)

*With $u$ as in Theorem 4.2: $e$ is nondegenerate $\iff u \in (\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$.*

**Proof.** ($\Leftarrow$) If $u$ is invertible then $e(v,w)=1$ for all $w$ gives $u\det(v,w) = 0$, hence $\det(v,w)=0$ for all $w$; taking $w = \varepsilon_2$ gives $v_1 = 0$ and $w = \varepsilon_1$ gives $-v_2 = 0$, so $v=0$. This also establishes nondegeneracy of $e_{\det}$ itself ($u=1$).

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $u$ is not a unit, so $g = \gcd(\tilde u, n) > 1$ for an integer representative $\tilde u$. Put $k = n/g$, so $0 < k < n$ and $k\,u = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$ (because $g \mid \tilde u$ implies $n \mid k\tilde u$). Then $v = (k, 0)$ is nonzero, yet for every $w$, $u\det(v,w) = u\,k\,w_2 = 0$, so $e(v,w) = 1$. Hence $e$ is degenerate. $\square$

### Corollary 4.5 (Counting)

*The nondegenerate alternating pairings on $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ valued in $\mu_n$ are in bijection with $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^\times$; there are $\varphi(n)$ of them, and exactly $n-1$ when $n$ is prime. They are precisely the primitive powers of the determinant pairing.*

### Theorem 4.6 (Symplectic normal form)

*Let $e$ be nondegenerate alternating on $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$. Then there is a group automorphism $\psi$ of $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ with*
$$e(v,w) = e_{\det}(\psi v,\; w) \quad \text{for all } v,w.$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 4.4 the scalar $u$ is a unit; let $\psi(v) = (uv_1, uv_2)$, an automorphism with inverse given by $u^{-1}$. Then $\det(\psi v, w) = u v_1 w_2 - u v_2 w_1 = u \det(v,w)$, and Theorem 4.2 concludes. $\square$

Thus the Weil pairing is unique up to symplectic isomorphism: no essentially different alternating nondegenerate pairing exists on the torsion. A corollary of methodological value is that to identify any concrete construction (divisor-theoretic or otherwise) with the determinant pairing, one need only compute a *single* value on a basis pair; the rest is forced.

### Theorem 4.7 (Nondegeneracy pins the root of unity)

*If $e$ is alternating on $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$, $n$-torsion valued and nondegenerate, then $\zeta := e(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)$ has exact order $n$.*

**Proof.** Let $k = \operatorname{ord}\zeta$; $k \mid n$ since $\zeta^n = 1$. The element $v = (k, 0)$ satisfies, for all $w$, $e(v,w) = \zeta^{k w_2} = 1$ by Theorem 3.5. Nondegeneracy forces $v = 0$, i.e. $n \mid k$. Hence $k = n$. $\square$

This is the bridge to the cryptographic sections: "nondegenerate" and "the pairing value generates $\mu_n$" are the same hypothesis, and the latter is the one a protocol actually uses.

## 5. Rank obstruction and existence

### Definition 5.1 (Weil pairing)

A **Weil pairing** on $E[n]$ with values in $\mu$ is an alternating pairing $e$ on $E[n]$ that is $n$-torsion valued, $e(P,Q)^n = 1$, and nondegenerate on both sides.

### Theorem 5.2 (Rank obstruction)

*If $E[n]$ is cyclic, generated by $g$, and carries a Weil pairing, then $E[n] = 0$.*

**Proof.** By Proposition 3.4 the pairing is identically $1$ on a cyclic group, in particular $e(g, Q) = 1$ for all $Q$. Left nondegeneracy gives $g = 0$; since every element is a multiple of $g$, every element is $0$. $\square$

### Corollary 5.3

*If some $n$-torsion point is nonzero and $E[n]$ carries a Weil pairing, then $E[n]$ is not cyclic.*

This is the intrinsic reason pairing-based cryptography is inherently *two-dimensional*: nontrivial nondegenerate alternation cannot exist in rank one. In protocol language, symmetric (type-1) pairings require a distortion map precisely because $e$ restricted to $\langle P\rangle \times \langle P\rangle$ is trivial by Proposition 3.4.

### Lemma 5.4 (Transport)

*If $\varphi : A \to B$ is a group homomorphism and $e$ is an alternating pairing on $B$, then $(x,y) \mapsto e(\varphi x, \varphi y)$ is an alternating pairing on $A$. If $\varphi$ is an isomorphism and $e$ is nondegenerate, so is the transported pairing.*

**Proof.** Bi-additivity and alternation are immediate. For nondegeneracy: if $e(\varphi x, \varphi y) = 1$ for all $y$, then since $\varphi$ is surjective $e(\varphi x, w) = 1$ for all $w \in B$, so $\varphi x = 0$, so $x = 0$ by injectivity. $\square$

### Theorem 5.5 (Existence / non-vacuity)

*Let $W$ be a Weierstrass curve with $E[n] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ as abelian groups. Then $E[n]$ carries a Weil pairing with values in $\mu_n$.*

**Proof.** Transport the determinant pairing along any such isomorphism (Lemma 5.4). Alternation and $n$-torsion values are inherited; nondegeneracy on both sides follows from Theorem 4.4 with $u=1$ and Proposition 3.3. $\square$

The classical structure theorem supplies the hypothesis whenever $\operatorname{char} F \nmid n$. Theorem 5.5 matters because it certifies that the axiom system of Definition 5.1 is *consistent*: all subsequent theorems have models, and none is vacuous.

## 6. Cryptanalytic consequences

Throughout this section $e$ is an alternating pairing on an abelian group $A$.

### Theorem 6.1 (Degree law)

*For all $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $x,y \in A$: $\;e(mx, my) = e(x,y)^{m^2}$.*

**Proof.** Apply (3.2) in each slot: $e(mx,my) = e(x,my)^m = e(x,y)^{m^2}$. $\square$

The exponent $m^2$ is the degree of the multiplication-by-$m$ isogeny; the next theorem explains why.

### Theorem 6.3 (Endomorphism equivariance)

*On $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$, let $\varphi$ be the endomorphism with matrix $M = \binom{a\ \ b}{c\ \ d}$. Then*
$$\det(\varphi v, \varphi w) = (ad-bc)\,\det(v,w), \qquad e_{\det}(\varphi v, \varphi w) = e_{\det}(v,w)^{\det M}.$$

**Proof.** Direct expansion: $(av_1+bv_2)(cw_1+dw_2) - (cv_1+dv_2)(aw_1+bw_2)$ simplifies to $(ad-bc)(v_1w_2-v_2w_1)$. $\square$

Taking $M = mI$ recovers Theorem 6.1 with $\det M = m^2$, a consistency check between the abstract and coordinate pictures. Theorem 6.3 is the algebraic shadow of the classical fact $e(\varphi P, \varphi Q) = e(P,Q)^{\deg\varphi}$ for an isogeny $\varphi$.

### Theorem 6.5 (MOV embedding)

*Suppose $\zeta = e(P,Q)$ has exact order $n$. Then the map $a \mapsto e(aP,Q) = \zeta^a$ is injective on $\{0,\dots,n-1\}$. Consequently, if $b < n$ satisfies $\zeta^{b} = e(aP, Q)$ for a challenge $aP$ with $a<n$, then $bP = aP$: a discrete logarithm in $\mu$ yields the discrete logarithm on the curve.*

**Proof.** $e(aP,Q) = \zeta^a$ by (3.2), and $x \mapsto \zeta^x$ is injective on a set of representatives of size $\operatorname{ord}\zeta = n$. Injectivity gives $a=b$, hence $bP=aP$. $\square$

The security significance is standard but worth restating in this framework: the *existence* of a nondegenerate pairing with values in $\mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{q^k}^\times$ transports the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm into a finite field where subexponential index-calculus attacks apply. Pairing-friendly curves must therefore balance a small enough embedding degree $k$ for efficiency against a large enough $k$ for security.

### Theorem 6.7 (Decisional Diffie–Hellman is easy)

*Suppose $\zeta = e(P,Q)$ has exact order $n$. Then for all $a,b,c \in \mathbb{N}$,*
$$e(aP,\, bQ) = e(P,\, cQ) \iff ab \equiv c \ [n].$$
*Both sides of the test are computable from the group elements $aP$, $bQ$, $cQ$ alone.*

**Proof.** The left side is $\zeta^{ab}$ and the right side is $\zeta^{c}$ by (3.2); $\zeta^{x} = \zeta^{y}$ iff $x \equiv y \bmod \operatorname{ord}\zeta = n$. $\square$

### Corollary 6.8

*In a group equipped with a nondegenerate pairing, no security proof may rest on the decisional Diffie–Hellman assumption.* This is exactly why the signature scheme of Sections 8–9 is reduced to the **computational** Diffie–Hellman problem: given $a\,g_2$ and $h\,g_1$, produce $ah\,g_1$.

### Proposition 6.9 (The test is blind on a cyclic subgroup)

*For any $P$ and any $a,b,c$: $\;e(aP, bP) = 1 = e(P, cP)$, so the DDH test of Theorem 6.7 is uninformative when all inputs are drawn from $\langle P \rangle$.*

**Proof.** Proposition 3.4. $\square$

Thus the pairing attack, like the pairing protocol, requires two independent torsion directions — the same dichotomy as Theorem 5.2, seen from the attacker's side.

## 7. An obstruction in the naive signature model

Before developing the scheme, we record a negative result about the way it is tempting to axiomatise.

### Definition 7.1 (Symmetric model with injective verification)

A *symmetric BLS setup* consists of an alternating pairing $e$ on $E[n]$, a distinguished generator $G \in E[n]$, and the requirement that
$$P \longmapsto e(P, G)$$
be **injective**.

The injectivity requirement is attractive: it makes signature uniqueness immediate, since the verification equation determines $e(\sigma, G)$ and hence $\sigma$.

### Theorem 7.2 (Obstruction)

*In any symmetric BLS setup, $G = 0$ and moreover every $Q \in E[n]$ is $0$. Hence such a setup exists only over curves with trivial $n$-torsion, and every statement proved from these axioms is vacuous.*

**Proof.** By alternation $e(G,G) = 1$, and $e(0,G)=1$ by bi-additivity; injectivity gives $G = 0$. Then for any $Q$, $e(Q,G) = e(Q,0) = 1 = e(0,0) = e(0,G)$, and injectivity gives $Q=0$. $\square$

### Corollary 7.3

*If some $Q \in E[n]$ is nonzero, no symmetric BLS setup exists over $E[n]$.*

The failure is instructive. The axiom is a *strengthened nondegeneracy* — nondegeneracy says only that $P \mapsto e(P,\cdot)$ is injective as a map into the dual, while the axiom above fixes the second argument. Fixing the second argument at $G$ and simultaneously demanding alternation is contradictory, because the fixed argument is itself in the group and pairs trivially with itself. The general lesson: a security model that bundles an injectivity assumption with an alternating form should be checked for inhabitedness before anything is proved in it.

## 8. The corrected asymmetric setting and single-signer BLS

### Definition 8.1 (Pairing-based signature setting)

A **signature setting** consists of an abelian group $A$, a commutative group $\mu$, an alternating pairing $e$ on $A$ with values in $\mu$, two elements $g_1$ (signature generator) and $g_2$ (key generator) of $A$, and the two requirements
$$n\,g_1 = 0, \qquad \operatorname{ord}\big(e(g_1,g_2)\big) = n.$$
We write $\zeta = e(g_1, g_2)$ for the **root of unity** of the setting.

This is the asymmetric (type-3) configuration used in deployed systems. By Theorem 4.7, the order condition is exactly what nondegeneracy delivers in the model, so nothing has been lost; by Theorem 7.2, everything has been gained, because — unlike Definition 7.1 — this setting is inhabited (Theorem 8.10).

### Definition 8.2 (Scheme)

For $sk, h \in \mathbb{N}$:

- **public key** $\quad pk(sk) = sk \cdot g_2$;
- **hash point** $\quad H(h) = h \cdot g_1$ (the image of a message under a hash-to-curve map, with exponent $h$);
- **signature** $\quad \sigma(sk, h) = (sk\cdot h)\cdot g_1$;
- **verification** $\quad \mathrm{Ver}(pk, H, \sigma) :\iff e(\sigma, g_2) = e(H, pk)$.

### Lemma 8.3 (Pairing values)

*For all $a,b \in \mathbb{N}$: $\;e(a g_1, b g_2) = \zeta^{ab}$, $\;e(a g_1, g_2) = \zeta^{a}$, and $\;e(H(h), pk(sk)) = \zeta^{sk\cdot h}$.*

**Proof.** (3.2) in each slot. $\square$

### Theorem 8.4 (Correctness and exact verification criterion)

*For all $sk, h, s \in \mathbb{N}$:*
$$\mathrm{Ver}\big(pk(sk),\, H(h),\, s\cdot g_1\big) \iff s \equiv sk\cdot h \ [n].$$
*In particular $\mathrm{Ver}(pk(sk), H(h), \sigma(sk,h))$ holds.*

**Proof.** By Lemma 8.3 the verification equation reads $\zeta^{s} = \zeta^{sk\cdot h}$, and since $\operatorname{ord}\zeta = n$ this is equivalent to $s \equiv sk\, h \bmod n$. Correctness is the case $s = sk\cdot h$. $\square$

This is the property the naive model tried, and failed, to impose by fiat: within the signature group $\langle g_1\rangle$, verification is an exact test on the exponent.

### Theorem 8.5 (Uniqueness of signatures)

*If $\mathrm{Ver}(pk(sk), H(h), s\cdot g_1)$ then $s\cdot g_1 = \sigma(sk,h)$.*

**Proof.** By Theorem 8.4, $s \equiv sk\,h \bmod n$. Since $n g_1 = 0$, congruent exponents act identically: writing $c = qn + r$ gives $c\,g_1 = r\,g_1$, so $s\,g_1 = (s \bmod n) g_1 = (sk\,h \bmod n)g_1 = (sk\,h)g_1$. $\square$

BLS signatures are therefore *unique*, hence deterministic: there is nothing to randomise and no nonce to leak.

### Definition 8.6 (Fresh-message game)

A **fresh game** consists of a secret exponent $a$ (the signer's key and the CDH secret), a hash-exponent programming function $h(\cdot)$ on messages, a target message $m^\*$, a finite set $Q$ of messages already submitted to the signing oracle, and the freshness condition $m^\* \notin Q$. Its **CDH target** is
$$T = a \cdot H\big(h(m^\*)\big) = \big(a\cdot h(m^\*)\big)\, g_1.$$

Freshness is what makes the reduction sound: the reduction never has to answer a signing query on $m^\*$, and can answer all other queries because it chose their hash exponents.

### Theorem 8.7 (A forgery *is* the CDH solution)

*In a fresh game with secret $a$, if $s\cdot g_1$ is accepted for the public key $pk(a)$ and the programmed hash point $H(h(m^\*))$, then*
$$s\cdot g_1 = T.$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 8.5, $s\,g_1 = \sigma(a, h(m^\*)) = (a\,h(m^\*))g_1$, which is $T$ by associativity of scalar action. $\square$

The reduction is *tight in the strongest possible sense*: no factor need be stripped, no rewinding is used, and the forgery is literally the challenge answer.

### Theorem 8.8 (Existential unforgeability under CDH)

*Let $\mathcal{R} \subseteq A$ be the set of elements the adversary can produce ("attainable"). If $T \notin \mathcal{R}$, then there is no $s$ with $s\,g_1 \in \mathcal{R}$ and $\mathrm{Ver}(pk(a), H(h(m^\*)), s\,g_1)$.*

**Proof.** Such an $s$ would give $s\,g_1 = T$ by Theorem 8.7, contradicting $T \notin \mathcal{R}$. $\square$

### Remark 8.9

The predicate $\mathcal{R}$ is the formal stand-in for "computable by the adversary within its resources"; the theorem says exactly that breaking the scheme on a fresh, programmed message is *the same problem* as solving the CDH instance, not merely as hard as it.

### Theorem 8.10 (Inhabitedness)

*(i) For every $n \geq 1$, the group $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ with the determinant pairing, $g_1 = \varepsilon_1$, $g_2 = \varepsilon_2$, is a signature setting. (ii) Every Weierstrass curve with $E[n] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$ carries a signature setting, with the pairing of Theorem 5.5 and the images of the standard basis as generators.*

**Proof.** (i) $n\varepsilon_1 = 0$ holds in $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$, and $\operatorname{ord} e_{\det}(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2) = n$ by Theorem 4.7 applied to the nondegenerate determinant pairing. (ii) Transport along the isomorphism (Lemma 5.4); the pairing value on the transported basis equals the value on the standard basis, so the order condition is preserved. $\square$

Contrast Corollary 7.3: the corrected model has models, the naive one has none.

## 9. Aggregation

Fix a signature setting and an index type $\iota$.

### Definition 9.1 (Aggregate signature and aggregate verification)

Given a finite signer set $S \subseteq \iota$, public keys $pk_i$, hash points $H_i$ and signatures $\sigma_i$, the **aggregate** is $\sigma = \sum_{i\in S}\sigma_i$, and the **aggregate verification predicate** is
$$\mathrm{AggVer}(S, pk, H, \sigma) :\iff e(\sigma,\, g_2) = \prod_{i\in S} e(H_i,\, pk_i).$$

### Theorem 9.2 (Aggregate correctness)

*With $pk_i = pk(sk_i)$, $H_i = H(h_i)$ and honest $\sigma_i = \sigma(sk_i,h_i)$,*
$$\mathrm{AggVer}\Big(S,\ pk,\ H,\ \textstyle\sum_{i \in S}\sigma_i\Big)$$
*holds.*

**Proof.** By induction on $S$ and additivity in the first slot, $e(\sum_i c_i g_1, g_2) = \zeta^{\sum_i c_i}$. With $c_i = sk_i h_i$ the left side is $\zeta^{\sum_i sk_i h_i}$. The right side is $\prod_i \zeta^{sk_i h_i} = \zeta^{\sum_i sk_i h_i}$ by Lemma 8.3. $\square$

### Theorem 9.3 (Aggregate uniqueness)

*In the registered-key setting, if $\mathrm{AggVer}(S, pk, H, \sigma)$ holds for an aggregate candidate $\sigma$ in the signature group, then $\sigma = \sum_{i\in S}\sigma(sk_i,h_i)$.*

**Proof.** Both $\sigma$ and the honest aggregate satisfy the same equation, so their pairings against $g_2$ agree, so their exponents agree modulo $n = \operatorname{ord}\zeta$, so (as in Theorem 8.5) they are equal in $\langle g_1\rangle$. $\square$

### Theorem 9.4 (Aggregate-to-single forgery extraction)

*Let $i_0 \in S$, let $\sigma = t\,g_1$ satisfy $\mathrm{AggVer}(S, pk, H, \sigma)$, and suppose the co-signer secret keys $\{sk_i\}_{i\neq i_0}$ are known. Then*
$$\sigma' \;=\; \sigma \;-\; \sum_{i \in S\setminus\{i_0\}} \sigma(sk_i, h_i)$$
*is a valid single-signer signature: $\mathrm{Ver}\big(pk(sk_{i_0}),\, H(h_{i_0}),\, \sigma'\big)$. No assumption is made about how $\sigma$ was produced.*

**Proof.** Aggregate verification gives $\zeta^{t} = \zeta^{\sum_{i\in S} sk_i h_i}$. Splitting off the index $i_0$, $\sum_{i \in S} sk_ih_i = sk_{i_0}h_{i_0} + \Sigma$ with $\Sigma = \sum_{i\neq i_0} sk_i h_i$. The co-signer sum pairs to $\zeta^{\Sigma}$. By Proposition 3.3, subtraction in the first slot divides:
$$e(\sigma', g_2) = e(\sigma, g_2)\cdot \zeta^{-\Sigma} = \zeta^{t - \Sigma} = \zeta^{sk_{i_0}h_{i_0}} = e\big(H(h_{i_0}), pk(sk_{i_0})\big). \qquad \square$$

### Theorem 9.5 (Aggregate unforgeability reduces to CDH)

*In a fresh game with secret $a$ and target message $m^\*$, suppose $sk_{i_0} = a$ and $H_{i_0} = H(h(m^\*))$. If $\sigma$ satisfies $\mathrm{AggVer}(S, pk, H, \sigma)$, then*
$$\sigma - \sum_{i\in S\setminus\{i_0\}} \sigma(sk_i,h_i) \;=\; T,$$
*the CDH target. Consequently, if $T$ is unattainable and the attainable set is closed under subtracting the (known) co-signer contributions, then no attainable $\sigma$ satisfies the aggregate verification equation.*

**Proof.** Theorem 9.4 produces a valid single-signer signature on the programmed fresh message; Theorem 8.7 identifies it with $T$. The second statement is the contrapositive, using closure to place the extracted element in the attainable set. $\square$

### Remark 9.6

The reduction consumes exactly two hypotheses: *freshness* of the target message (so the signing oracle can be simulated) and *knowledge of the co-signers' keys* (so the extraction is computable). The second is the KOSK / proof-of-possession assumption, and Section 10 shows it is indispensable.

### Theorem 9.7 (Batch verification: soundness and completeness)

*For weights $r : \iota \to \mathbb{N}$, define*
$$\mathrm{Batch}(S,pk,H,\sigma,r) :\iff e\Big(\sum_{i\in S} r_i\,\sigma_i,\ g_2\Big) = \prod_{i\in S} e(H_i, pk_i)^{r_i}.$$
*Then $\mathrm{Batch}$ holds for **all** weight vectors $r$ if and only if $\mathrm{Ver}(pk_i, H_i, \sigma_i)$ holds for every $i \in S$.*

**Proof.** ($\Leftarrow$) By additivity and (3.2), $e(\sum_i r_i\sigma_i, g_2) = \prod_i e(\sigma_i,g_2)^{r_i}$; substitute each individual verification equation. ($\Rightarrow$) Take the indicator weights $r_j = [j = i_0]$: the sum collapses to $\sigma_{i_0}$ and the product to $e(H_{i_0}, pk_{i_0})$, yielding the individual equation. $\square$

### Remark 9.8

This is the deterministic core of randomised batch verification: in practice one samples the $r_i$ from a small set, and the equivalence above is what a cheating prover must defeat.

### Theorem 9.9 (Exact compression)

*Suppose the group $A$ is finite with $|A| = N$, and let $m \geq 1$. Then*
1. *the space of $m$-tuples of signatures has $N^m$ elements;*
2. *the set of values taken by the aggregation map $(\sigma_1,\dots,\sigma_m)\mapsto \sum_i \sigma_i$ has exactly $N$ elements.*

*Hence aggregation is precisely an $N^m \twoheadrightarrow N$ compression.*

**Proof.** (1) is counting functions. For (2), the image is contained in $A$, so has at most $N$ elements; conversely for any $g \in A$ the tuple $(g,0,\dots,0)$ aggregates to $g$, so the image is all of $A$. $\square$

The statement is worth making precisely because the naive reading ("the aggregate is one group element, so it's short") does not by itself exclude the possibility that only a thin subset of the group is reachable — which would signal hidden structure exploitable by an attacker. Theorem 9.9 says the image is the entire group: the compression is surjective, and the $m$-dependence has been fully absorbed into the public keys.

## 10. The rogue-key attack

### Definition 10.0 (Rogue key)

Given a victim public key $pk_1$ and any scalar $y$ chosen by the adversary, the **rogue public key** is
$$pk_2 = y\,g_2 - pk_1.$$
It is computable from $pk_1$ alone; the adversary knows no corresponding secret key, and indeed one may not exist in any useful sense.

### Theorem 10.1 (Plain aggregation is universally forgeable)

*For every $y$, every victim key $pk_1$ and every hash point $H$, the element $\sigma = y\cdot H$ satisfies the two-signer aggregate verification equation for the key pair $(pk_1, pk_2)$ on the common message $H$:*
$$e(y H,\, g_2) = e(H, pk_1)\cdot e(H, pk_2).$$

**Proof.** By additivity in the second slot,
$$e(H,pk_1)\,e(H, pk_2) = e\big(H,\ pk_1 + (y g_2 - pk_1)\big) = e(H,\ y\,g_2) = e(H,g_2)^y = e(yH,\ g_2). \qquad \square$$

The forger uses no secret key, performs no interaction with the victim, and produces a verifying aggregate that implicates the victim in signing $H$. The attack is not probabilistic and has no failure case: the identity is unconditional.

### Corollary 10.2

*The knowledge-of-secret-key hypothesis in Theorem 9.4, and hence in Theorem 9.5, cannot be removed.* Deployments must therefore require proofs of possession at registration, or use a defended aggregation rule (for instance, exponentiating each key by a hash of the key set), which restores the extraction argument by preventing the algebraic cancellation exploited above.

## 12. Discussion

**What is assumed and what is derived.** The development assumes only bi-additivity and $e(P,P)=1$. From these, skew-symmetry, cyclic blindness, the determinant form, the classification, the rank obstruction, the degree law, the MOV embedding and the DDH collapse are all theorems. The cryptographic layer adds exactly one genuinely new hypothesis — that the root of unity $\zeta = e(g_1,g_2)$ has exact order $n$ — and one computational assumption, the hardness of computational Diffie–Hellman. Nothing else is assumed.

**On the value of adversarial self-review.** Theorem 7.2 is the methodological centrepiece. A security model can be internally consistent as a piece of prose and still be uninhabited as a mathematical object; when it is uninhabited, every theorem proved in it is true and worthless. The failure mode here — an injectivity axiom that alternation immediately contradicts — is the kind that survives informal review precisely because each ingredient is individually standard. The remedy is cheap: exhibit a model (Theorem 8.10) before proving anything.

**On tightness.** The reduction of Theorems 8.7 and 9.5 is unusually clean: the forgery *equals* the CDH target. This is a consequence of signature uniqueness (Theorem 8.5), which in turn is a consequence of the exact-order hypothesis. Schemes with randomised signatures do not enjoy this and pay for it with looser reductions.

**Limitations.** (i) We treat the hash-to-curve map as programmable with known exponents, i.e. we work in the random-oracle idealisation; the exponent $h(m)$ is a modelling device, not something a real signer knows. (ii) The adversary's power is modelled abstractly by an "attainable set" predicate rather than by a concrete machine model; this is adequate for the algebraic content of the reduction but does not by itself quantify concrete security. (iii) The classification results are stated over $(\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$; a full treatment of higher-rank abelian varieties requires the symplectic induction sketched below. (iv) The divisor-theoretic Weil pairing is not constructed here; Theorem 4.6 shows only that *any* nondegenerate alternating construction must coincide with the determinant model up to a unit.

## 13. Future directions

**Conjecture A (symplectic structure implies square order).** Every finite abelian group carrying a nondegenerate alternating pairing into a commutative group has square cardinality, and admits a symplectic basis $(x_1,y_1,\dots,x_g,y_g)$ with $e(x_i, y_j) = \zeta_i^{\delta_{ij}}$ and $e(x_i,x_j) = e(y_i,y_j) = 1$.

The rank obstruction (Theorem 5.2) is precisely the rank-one base case of the induction that would prove this: choose $x$ of maximal order, use nondegeneracy to find $y$ with $e(x,y)$ of the same order, and split off the orthogonal complement of $\langle x,y\rangle$. The complement step is the one missing ingredient, and the structure theory of finite abelian groups supplies the decomposition against which to run the induction. The conjecture is falsifiable: exhibit a finite abelian group of non-square order carrying a nondegenerate alternating pairing, or show the induction step fails for $\mathbb{Z}/p \times \mathbb{Z}/p^2$.

**Conjecture B (divisor-theoretic pairing matches the determinant model).** The classical Weil pairing $e_n(P,Q) = f_P(D_Q)/f_Q(D_P)$ on a Weierstrass curve is, after any isomorphism $E[n] \cong (\mathbb{Z}/n)^2$, a unit multiple of the determinant pairing — so the transported pairing of Theorem 5.5 is not merely *a* pairing but *the* Weil pairing, up to a choice of primitive root of unity.

The symplectic normal form (Theorem 4.6) reduces this identification to a single computation: any construction that is bi-additive, alternating and nondegenerate is forced into determinant normal form, so only the value on *one* basis pair need be computed from divisors. This converts a global geometric identification into a local, finite one.

**Further directions.** (i) Quantify the embedding-degree trade-off implied by Theorem 6.5 in the classification framework, relating $\operatorname{ord}\zeta$ to the field of definition of $\mu_n$. (ii) Extend the aggregation theory to defended aggregation rules (key-hash exponentiation) and prove the analogue of Theorem 9.4 without the knowledge-of-secret-key hypothesis. (iii) Develop threshold and multi-signature variants within the same axiomatic frame, where uniqueness of signatures (Theorem 8.5) should yield unusually simple robustness arguments. (iv) Investigate whether the exact compression statement (Theorem 9.9) can be refined to a statement about the *distribution* of aggregates under honest generation, which is the quantity relevant to privacy rather than to bandwidth.

---

## 14. Conclusion

A single axiom — $e(P,P) = 1$ — imposed on a bi-additive map determines the entire theory. It forces the determinant form, and with it the classification of pairings up to a unit; it forces the torsion to have rank two if the pairing is to be nontrivial; it makes decisional Diffie–Hellman easy and thereby dictates that pairing-based schemes be reduced to the computational variant; it makes signatures unique and reductions tight; and it is what quietly destroys the most natural symmetric formulation of the signature model, whose injectivity axiom it contradicts outright. The corrected asymmetric setting, with two generators and an exact-order condition, is inhabited, supports the full theory including aggregation, and shows exactly where the registered-key hypothesis is load-bearing: remove it, and a rogue key breaks the scheme in one line.

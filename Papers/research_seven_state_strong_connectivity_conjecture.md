**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-09-13

## Abstract

We study the reachability structure of the finite transition system obtained by reading the rules of first-species (note-against-note) two-voice counterpoint as the transition relation of a state machine, restricted to a canonical set of seven interval representatives in which the lower voice is fixed at pitch $0$. The conjecture that this seven-state system is strongly connected — that the reflexive–transitive closure of the one-step motion relation is total — is **false**. We prove that the *register*, a four-valued invariant obtained by cutting the consonance ladder $\{0,3,4,7,8,9,12\}$ at its gaps of size three, is a complete invariant of one-step motion: a legal canonical motion from interval $i$ to interval $j$ exists if and only if $i$ and $j$ share a register. Consequently the one-step relation is already an equivalence relation, transitive closure is idempotent on the nose, exactly $15$ of the $49$ ordered pairs are reachable and $34$ are not, and the unison cannot reach the octave. We establish four further results. (i) *Universality:* every quantity conserved by one-step motion factors through the register, so no finer invariant exists, and the thin category generated by the system is a groupoid whose skeleton is the discrete four-object category. (ii) *A sharp threshold:* parameterizing the admissible melodic step width by $w$, the seven states are strongly connected if and only if $w \ge 3$, so the historical stepwise width $w = 2$ lies exactly one semitone below criticality. (iii) *A structural explanation:* for an arbitrary finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ with edges $|b-a| \le w$, connectivity holds if and only if every pair of consecutive elements of $S$ differs by at most $w$; the consonance ladder has maximal consecutive gap $3$, which recovers the threshold conceptually and makes it computable for any tuning system. (iv) *Repair:* in the ambient two-voice system with a free bass, all seven consonances are mutually reachable, witnessed by an explicit contrary-motion spine, and the distance from a unison to an octave is exactly three motions. The obstruction is therefore an artifact of the frozen-bass canonical realization, not of first-species counterpoint.

**Keywords:** counterpoint, consonance ladder, reachability, complete invariant, gap graph, connectivity threshold, groupoid skeleton, discrete dynamics.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Counterpoint as a transition system

First-species counterpoint, codified by Fux in *Gradus ad Parnassum* (1725), governs two voices sounding one note against one note. Its constraints are local and finitary:

1. every simultaneity must be a consonance;
2. each voice must move melodically by a small amount (classically a step, at most a whole tone);
3. a perfect consonance may not be approached by similar motion (both voices moving the same direction), which outlaws parallel and direct fifths and octaves.

These are conditions on *pairs* of consecutive simultaneities, so they define a binary relation on the set of simultaneities: a transition system. A counterpoint exercise is a path in that system, and questions of compositional freedom become questions of reachability.

The most basic such question is strong connectivity. If the system is strongly connected, every harmonic configuration can eventually be turned into every other, and the rules constrain only the *route*, never the *destination*. If it is not, then some destinations are unreachable, and the rule book contains a hidden conservation law.

### 1.2 The conjecture and its fate

To reduce the (infinite) set of two-voice simultaneities to a finite state space one selects one representative per interval class. The canonical choice fixes the lower voice at pitch $0$ and places the upper voice at the interval. This yields seven states, one for each simple consonance. The conjecture under examination asserts that the reflexive–transitive closure of the resulting one-step relation is total on these seven states.

We refute it. Freezing the bass means that only the upper voice can move, so a legal motion can change the vertical interval by at most a whole tone. The consonance ladder has three gaps of size three, and each is an impassable cut. Section 3 develops the resulting invariant and its consequences; Sections 4–6 develop the threshold, the general gap-graph criterion, and the free-bass repair.

### 1.3 Contributions

* A complete four-valued invariant (the *register*) for canonical one-step motion, together with the observation that the one-step relation is therefore already an equivalence relation and closure is idempotent (Section 3).
* An exact census: $15$ reachable and $34$ unreachable ordered pairs among $49$; total isolation of unison and of octave (Section 3.3).
* Universality of the register invariant, and the identification of the generated thin category as a groupoid with discrete four-object skeleton (Section 3.4).
* A sharp connectivity threshold at melodic width $w = 3$, with the subcritical and supercritical regimes both proved (Section 4).
* A general connectivity criterion for integer gap graphs, uniform in the underlying finite set and in the width, from which the threshold follows conceptually and which computes the critical width of any tuning system (Section 5).
* The free-bass repair: mutual reachability of all seven consonances via an explicit contrary-motion spine, and an exact unison-to-octave distance of three (Section 6).

## 2. Definitions

Throughout, pitches are integers measured in semitones; ordinary integer arithmetic models transposition and melodic displacement.

**Definition 2.1 (Dyad).** A *dyad* is an ordered pair $x = (x_{\mathrm{lo}}, x_{\mathrm{hi}}) \in \mathbb{Z}^2$ recording the pitches of the lower and upper voice. We write $\mathrm{dyad}(b,u)$ for the dyad with lower voice $b$ and upper voice $u$.

**Definition 2.2 (Vertical interval).** The *vertical interval* of a dyad is
$$I(x) = x_{\mathrm{hi}} - x_{\mathrm{lo}} \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Definition 2.3 (Simple consonances).** The *simple consonances* are the seven intervals
$$\mathcal{C} = \{\,0,\;3,\;4,\;7,\;8,\;9,\;12\,\},$$
namely unison, minor third, major third, perfect fifth, minor sixth, major sixth, octave. A dyad $x$ is *consonant* when $I(x) \in \mathcal{C}$. The *perfect* consonances are $\{0,7,12\}$; the remainder are imperfect. We write $\sigma(i)$ for the semitone value of a named consonance $i$, so that $\sigma$ is a bijection from the seven names onto $\mathcal{C}$.

**Definition 2.4 (Stepwise motion).** A transition $x \to y$ between dyads is *stepwise* when each voice moves by at most a whole tone:
$$|y_{\mathrm{lo}} - x_{\mathrm{lo}}| \le 2 \quad\text{and}\quad |y_{\mathrm{hi}} - x_{\mathrm{hi}}| \le 2 .$$

**Definition 2.5 (Similar motion).** The transition $x \to y$ is a *similar motion* when both voices move in the same direction, i.e. $y_{\mathrm{lo}} - x_{\mathrm{lo}}$ and $y_{\mathrm{hi}} - x_{\mathrm{hi}}$ are both strictly positive or both strictly negative.

**Definition 2.6 (Permitted motion).** A transition $x \to y$ is a *permitted (first-species) motion*, written $x \rightsquigarrow y$, when

1. $x$ is consonant,
2. $y$ is consonant,
3. $x \to y$ is stepwise, and
4. it is **not** the case that $y$ is perfect and $x \to y$ is a similar motion.

Clause (4) is the prohibition on direct and parallel perfect consonances.

**Definition 2.7 (Reachability).** *Reachability* $x \Rightarrow y$ between dyads is the reflexive–transitive closure of $\rightsquigarrow$: there are $n \ge 0$ and dyads $x = z_0 \rightsquigarrow z_1 \rightsquigarrow \cdots \rightsquigarrow z_n = y$. We write $x \Rightarrow_n y$ when a path of exactly $n$ permitted motions exists.

**Definition 2.8 (Canonical realization).** The *canonical representative* of a simple consonance $i$ is the dyad $\kappa(i) = \mathrm{dyad}(0, \sigma(i))$, with the lower voice frozen at pitch $0$. The *canonical motion* relation on the seven consonance names is
$$\mathrm{CM}(i,j) \iff \kappa(i) \rightsquigarrow \kappa(j),$$
and *canonical reachability* $\mathrm{CR}$ is its reflexive–transitive closure taken **inside the seven-state set**.

The distinction in Definition 2.8 is the crux of the paper: $\mathrm{CR}$ only permits intermediate states that are themselves canonical, whereas $\Rightarrow$ permits any dyads whatsoever.

**Lemma 2.9 (Explicit form of canonical motion).** For all consonance names $i,j$,
$$\mathrm{CM}(i,j) \iff |\sigma(j) - \sigma(i)| \le 2 .$$

*Proof.* Both $\kappa(i)$ and $\kappa(j)$ are consonant by construction, so clauses (1) and (2) hold automatically. The lower voice does not move, so the lower-voice displacement is $0$, hence the transition is never a similar motion and clause (4) holds vacuously. Clause (3) reduces to $|\sigma(j) - \sigma(i)| \le 2$ on the upper voice. $\square$

Lemma 2.9 already shows that the frozen bass removes two of the four clauses from play: canonical counterpoint is governed by a bare melodic bound.

## 3. The register invariant and the refutation

### 3.1 Registers

**Definition 3.1 (Register).** The *register* $\rho(i) \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ of a simple consonance is
$$\rho(\text{unison}) = 0, \quad \rho(\text{minor third}) = \rho(\text{major third}) = 1,$$
$$\rho(\text{perfect fifth}) = \rho(\text{minor sixth}) = \rho(\text{major sixth}) = 2, \quad \rho(\text{octave}) = 3 .$$

Equivalently, the registers are the blocks
$$\{0\},\qquad \{3,4\},\qquad \{7,8,9\},\qquad \{12\}$$
obtained by cutting the consonance ladder at each consecutive gap exceeding $2$. The ladder's consecutive gap sequence is
$$3,\;1,\;3,\;1,\;1,\;3,$$
so exactly three cuts are made, producing four blocks of sizes $1,2,3,1$.

**Theorem 3.2 (Register completeness).** For all simple consonances $i,j$,
$$\mathrm{CM}(i,j) \iff \rho(i) = \rho(j).$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 2.9 the left side is $|\sigma(j) - \sigma(i)| \le 2$. Within a block, consecutive elements differ by $1$ and the extreme elements of the largest block ($7$ and $9$) differ by $2$, so the bound holds throughout each block. Across blocks, the minimum distance between distinct blocks is realized at a cut and equals $3$: $|3-0| = 3$, $|7-4| = 3$, $|12-9| = 3$, and all other cross-block distances are larger. Hence the bound fails across blocks. Both directions are verified by finite inspection of the $7\times 7$ table. $\square$

**Corollary 3.3 (The one-step relation is an equivalence).** $\mathrm{CM}$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.

*Proof.* Each property is transported from the corresponding property of equality of registers via Theorem 3.2. $\square$

Corollary 3.3 is already surprising: the ambient dyad relation $\rightsquigarrow$ is *not* transitive (chaining two stepwise motions can produce a leap), yet the canonical restriction is. Transitivity is precisely what makes closure vacuous.

### 3.2 Saturation and the counterexample

**Theorem 3.4 (Register is a complete reachability invariant).** For all $i,j$,
$$\mathrm{CR}(i,j) \iff \rho(i) = \rho(j).$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) Induct on the length of the path. The empty path preserves the register trivially; if a path from $i$ to $b$ preserves the register and $\mathrm{CM}(b,c)$, then $\rho(b) = \rho(c)$ by Theorem 3.2, and transitivity of equality finishes the step. ($\Leftarrow$) If $\rho(i) = \rho(j)$ then $\mathrm{CM}(i,j)$ by Theorem 3.2, and a one-step path is a path. $\square$

**Theorem 3.5 (Idempotence of closure).** $\mathrm{CR} = \mathrm{CM}$: the reflexive–transitive closure of the canonical motion relation adds no new pairs.

*Proof.* Compose Theorems 3.2 and 3.4. $\square$

As an immediate by-product, canonical reachability is decidable by evaluating a four-valued function twice, rather than by any search.

**Theorem 3.6 (Counterexample: unison $\not\Rightarrow$ octave).** There is no finite chain of canonical motions from the unison to the octave.

*Proof.* $\rho(\text{unison}) = 0 \ne 3 = \rho(\text{octave})$; apply Theorem 3.4. $\square$

**Theorem 3.7 (Refutation of the conjecture).** It is not the case that $\mathrm{CR}(i,j)$ holds for all pairs $i,j$ of simple consonances; the seven-state canonical system is not strongly connected.

*Proof.* Instantiate at $(\text{unison},\text{octave})$ and apply Theorem 3.6. $\square$

**Theorem 3.8 (Isolation of the extremes).** The unison and the octave are each totally isolated: if $\mathrm{CR}(\text{unison}, i)$ or $\mathrm{CR}(i, \text{unison})$ then $i$ is the unison, and likewise for the octave.

*Proof.* Their registers $0$ and $3$ are singletons; apply Theorem 3.4. $\square$

Musically: with a frozen bass, an exercise beginning in unison is a unison forever, and one beginning in octaves is an octave forever.

### 3.3 Exact census

**Theorem 3.9 (Census).** Exactly $15$ of the $49$ ordered pairs of simple consonances are joined by a finite chain of canonical motions, and exactly $34$ are not; the two counts partition the $49$ pairs. Moreover the set of reachable pairs coincides exactly with the tabulated set of one-step canonical motions.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 3.4 the reachable pairs are the fibred square of $\rho$, i.e. the union over registers of $B \times B$. The block sizes are $1,2,3,1$, so the count is $1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 1^2 = 15$, and $49 - 15 = 34$. Coincidence with the one-step table is Theorem 3.5. $\square$

**Theorem 3.10 (Component count).** The seven states fall into exactly four strongly connected components, namely the four registers. Since strong connectivity requires a single component and $4 \ne 1$, the component count is itself an obstruction.

### 3.4 Universality and categorical shape

**Theorem 3.11 (Universality of the register).** Let $f$ be any function on the seven simple consonances, valued in any set, such that $f(i) = f(j)$ whenever $\mathrm{CM}(i,j)$. Then $f(i) = f(j)$ whenever $\rho(i) = \rho(j)$; that is, $f$ factors through $\rho$.

*Proof.* If $\rho(i) = \rho(j)$ then $\mathrm{CM}(i,j)$ by Theorem 3.2, so $f(i) = f(j)$ by hypothesis. $\square$

Thus $\rho$ is the finest conserved quantity: the four-block decomposition is canonical, not an artefact of a particular choice of invariant.

Consider now the thin category $\mathcal{K}$ whose objects are the seven consonances and which has a unique arrow $i \to j$ precisely when $\mathrm{CR}(i,j)$.

**Theorem 3.12 (Categorical shape).** $\mathcal{K}$ is a groupoid; it is not a partial order; its skeleton has exactly four objects and is discrete.

*Proof sketch.* Symmetry of $\mathrm{CR}$ (Corollary 3.3 via Theorem 3.4) makes every arrow invertible, hence $\mathcal{K}$ is a groupoid. Antisymmetry fails because the minor and major thirds are distinct objects that are mutually reachable, so $\mathcal{K}$ is not skeletal and not a poset. Mutual reachability is an equivalence relation whose quotient is in bijection with the four registers: the map induced by $\rho$ on the quotient is inverted by choosing the representatives unison, minor third, perfect fifth, octave, since each representative has the correct register and is mutually reachable with every element of its block. Between distinct registers there are no arrows at all, so the induced relation on the quotient is equality and the skeleton is discrete on four objects. $\square$

Strong connectivity is exactly the statement that the skeleton is terminal (one object). Here it has four, so the failure is maximal subject to each register being internally connected.

## 4. A sharp threshold in the melodic step width

The frozen-bass dynamics is governed by the single bound $|\sigma(j)-\sigma(i)| \le 2$. Replacing $2$ by a parameter yields a one-parameter family and exposes a phase transition.

**Definition 4.1 (Width-$w$ motion).** For $w \in \mathbb{N}$ set
$$\mathrm{GM}_w(i,j) \iff |\sigma(j) - \sigma(i)| \le w,$$
and let $\mathrm{GR}_w$ be its reflexive–transitive closure on the seven states.

**Lemma 4.2 (Monotonicity and symmetry).** If $w \le w'$ then $\mathrm{GM}_w \subseteq \mathrm{GM}_{w'}$, hence $\mathrm{GR}_w \subseteq \mathrm{GR}_{w'}$. Each $\mathrm{GM}_w$ is symmetric, because $|\sigma(i)-\sigma(j)| = |\sigma(j)-\sigma(i)|$; consequently each $\mathrm{GR}_w$ is symmetric.

**Lemma 4.3 (Calibration).** $\mathrm{GM}_2 = \mathrm{CM}$: at the historical stepwise width, the parameterized model reproduces canonical motion exactly. (Immediate from Lemma 2.9.)

**Theorem 4.4 (Subcritical regime).** For every $w \le 2$, the unison does not reach the octave under $\mathrm{GR}_w$; in particular no stepwise rule is strongly connected.

*Proof.* By monotonicity $\mathrm{GM}_w \subseteq \mathrm{GM}_2 = \mathrm{CM}$, so $\mathrm{GR}_w \subseteq \mathrm{CR}$, and Theorem 3.6 applies. $\square$

**Theorem 4.5 (Supercritical regime).** For every $w \ge 3$ and all $i,j$, $\mathrm{GR}_w(i,j)$ holds.

*Proof sketch.* At $w = 3$ each consecutive rung of the ladder is a legal hop, since the gaps are $3,1,3,1,1,3 \le 3$. Chaining them gives the spine
$$0 \to 3 \to 4 \to 7 \to 8 \to 9 \to 12,$$
so the unison reaches every consonance. By symmetry (Lemma 4.2) every consonance reaches the unison, and composing through the unison connects any $i$ to any $j$. Monotonicity lifts the conclusion from $3$ to every $w \ge 3$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.6 (Sharp threshold).** For every $w \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$\bigl(\forall i,j:\ \mathrm{GR}_w(i,j)\bigr) \iff w \ge 3 .$$

*Proof.* Combine Theorems 4.4 and 4.5. $\square$

The threshold is one semitone above the historical width. The rule "move by step" and the rule "sing only consonances" are individually innocuous; their conjunction disconnects the state space, and admitting leaps of a minor third repairs it exactly.

## 5. The structural reason: connectivity of integer gap graphs

Theorem 4.6 was obtained by finite computation at the endpoints. It has a conceptual explanation, uniform in the underlying set of intervals, which makes the result portable to arbitrary tuning systems.

**Definition 5.1 (Gap graph).** Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ be finite and $w \in \mathbb{N}$. A *hop* is a pair $a, b \in S$ with $|b - a| \le w$; write $\mathrm{Step}_{S,w}(a,b)$. Let $\mathrm{Conn}_{S,w}$ be the reflexive–transitive closure of $\mathrm{Step}_{S,w}$ within $S$.

**Definition 5.2 (Consecutive gap bound).** $S$ satisfies the *consecutive gap bound* at width $w$ when, for all $x < y$ in $S$ with no element of $S$ strictly between them, $y - x \le w$.

**Theorem 5.3 (Gap criterion).** For every finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ and every $w \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$\bigl(\forall a,b \in S:\ \mathrm{Conn}_{S,w}(a,b)\bigr) \iff S \text{ satisfies the consecutive gap bound at width } w .$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Leftarrow$, sufficiency) Strong induction on $(b-a)$ for $a < b$ in $S$. If $|b-a| \le w$, a single hop suffices. Otherwise, either some $z \in S$ lies strictly between $a$ and $b$ — in which case both $(a,z)$ and $(z,b)$ have strictly smaller separation and the induction hypothesis plus transitivity finishes — or no such $z$ exists, making $a,b$ consecutive, so the gap bound gives $b - a \le w$, contradicting the case assumption. The cases $a = b$ and $a > b$ follow from reflexivity and from symmetry of hops.

($\Rightarrow$, necessity) Suppose $x < y$ are consecutive in $S$ with $y - x > w$. We show the gap is an impassable cut: every $v$ with $\mathrm{Conn}_{S,w}(x,v)$ satisfies $v \le x$. Induct on the path. The base case is $v = x$. For the inductive step, let $u \le x$ and $\mathrm{Step}_{S,w}(u,v)$; if $v > x$ then, since $x$ and $y$ are consecutive and $v \in S$, we must have $v \ge y$, whence $v - u \ge y - x > w$, contradicting the hop bound. Applying the cut to $v = y$ gives $y \le x$, contradicting $x < y$. Hence connectivity forces the gap bound. $\square$

Note that neither direction is a finite check: the statement is uniform in $S$ and $w$.

**Definition 5.4 (Consonance ladder).** $L = \{0,3,4,7,8,9,12\} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, the image of the seven simple consonances under $\sigma$.

**Proposition 5.5.** $L$ satisfies the consecutive gap bound at width $3$, and at no smaller width: the pair $0 < 3$ is consecutive in $L$ and $3 - 0 = 3$.

**Theorem 5.6 (Ladder threshold).** $\bigl(\forall a,b \in L: \mathrm{Conn}_{L,w}(a,b)\bigr) \iff w \ge 3$.

*Proof.* Theorem 5.3 plus Proposition 5.5, using that the consecutive gap bound is monotone in $w$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.7 (Reconciliation).** Width-$w$ motion on the seven named consonances corresponds exactly to hops of the ladder gap graph: $\mathrm{GM}_w(i,j) \iff \mathrm{Step}_{L,w}(\sigma(i),\sigma(j))$, because $\sigma$ lands in $L$ by definition. Transporting reachability along $\sigma$ and using that $\sigma$ is onto $L$, Theorem 5.6 yields a second, structural proof of Theorem 4.6. The two derivations agree.

**Corollary 5.8 (Diagnosis).** At the historical width $w = 2$ the ladder gap graph is disconnected, because $L$ has consecutive gaps of size $3$. The register blocks of Definition 3.1 are exactly the connected components of $\mathrm{Conn}_{L,2}$, and the cut points are exactly the three wide gaps.

**Remark 5.9 (Portability).** Theorem 5.3 holds for arbitrary finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, so the *critical melodic width* of any pitch system is a computable invariant of its consonance set: it is the maximum consecutive gap of that set. For $n$-tone equal temperaments, just-intonation subsets rendered in cents, or non-octave scales such as Bohlen–Pierce, the critical width can be read off by sorting the consonance list and taking a maximum of successive differences — an $O(|S| \log |S|)$ computation.

## 6. The free bass: repair and exact distance

All results so far concern the seven canonical states with the bass pinned at $0$. The ambient system permits both voices to move, and there the picture reverses.

**Lemma 6.1 (Symmetry of permitted motion).** If $x \rightsquigarrow y$ then $y \rightsquigarrow x$.

*Proof sketch.* Consonance of the two endpoints is a symmetric pair of conditions. Stepwise-ness is symmetric, because negating each displacement preserves its absolute value. For clause (4), similar motion is symmetric as a relation on pairs (both displacements change sign together, so "same strict sign" is preserved), so a violation of clause (4) for $y \to x$ would produce a violation for $x \to y$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.2.** Dyad reachability $\Rightarrow$ is symmetric.

**Definition 6.3 (Contrary-motion spine).** Define dyads
$$\begin{aligned}
&\mathrm{sp}(\text{unison}) = (0,0), && \mathrm{sp}(\text{minor third}) = (-1,2), && \mathrm{sp}(\text{major third}) = (-1,3),\\
&\mathrm{sp}(\text{perfect fifth}) = (-2,5), && \mathrm{sp}(\text{minor sixth}) = (-2,6), && \mathrm{sp}(\text{major sixth}) = (-2,7),\\
&\mathrm{sp}(\text{octave}) = (-3,9). &&&&
\end{aligned}$$

**Lemma 6.4 (The spine realizes the intervals).** $I(\mathrm{sp}(i)) = \sigma(i)$ for every simple consonance $i$: the vertical intervals are $0,3,4,7,8,9,12$ respectively.

**Lemma 6.5 (The spine is legal).** Each consecutive transition
$$(0,0) \to (-1,2) \to (-1,3) \to (-2,5) \to (-2,6) \to (-2,7) \to (-3,9)$$
is a permitted motion.

*Proof sketch.* All seven dyads are consonant by Lemma 6.4. Each transition displaces the lower voice by $0$ or $-1$ and the upper voice by $+1$ or $+2$, so all are stepwise. The perfect consonances occurring as targets are the fifth $(-2,5)$ and the octave $(-3,9)$; both are approached with the lower voice descending and the upper voice ascending, i.e. by contrary motion, so clause (4) is satisfied. The remaining targets are imperfect, where clause (4) is vacuous. $\square$

**Theorem 6.6 (Free-bass connectivity).** For all simple consonances $i,j$ there exist dyads $x,y$ with $I(x) = \sigma(i)$, $I(y) = \sigma(j)$ and $x \Rightarrow y$. All seven consonances are mutually reachable once the bass may move.

*Proof.* Take $x = \mathrm{sp}(i)$, $y = \mathrm{sp}(j)$. Chaining Lemma 6.5 shows $\mathrm{sp}(\text{unison}) \Rightarrow \mathrm{sp}(k)$ for every $k$; Corollary 6.2 reverses the first leg; compose through the unison. $\square$

**Theorem 6.7 (Dichotomy).** Strong connectivity fails on the canonical frozen-bass states and simultaneously holds in the ambient free-bass dyad system.

The obstruction is therefore created solely by the canonical realization. Quantitatively, a free bass doubles the per-step interval mobility from $2$ to $4$ semitones, which exceeds the maximal ladder gap $3$ — exactly the condition Theorem 5.3 requires.

### 6.1 Metric refinement

Connectivity says nothing about cost. For the extremal pair the cost is determined exactly.

**Definition 6.8.** Write $x \Rightarrow_n y$ for reachability by a path of exactly $n$ permitted motions.

**Lemma 6.9 (Per-step interval bound).** If $x \rightsquigarrow y$ then $|I(y) - I(x)| \le 4$.

*Proof.* $I(y) - I(x) = (y_{\mathrm{hi}} - x_{\mathrm{hi}}) - (y_{\mathrm{lo}} - x_{\mathrm{lo}})$, and each displacement is bounded by $2$ in absolute value. $\square$

**Lemma 6.10 (Potential bound).** If $x \Rightarrow_n y$ then $|I(y) - I(x)| \le 4n$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$ using Lemma 6.9 and the triangle inequality. $\square$

**Theorem 6.11 (Lower bound).** For every $n \le 2$ and all dyads $x,y$ with $I(x) = 0$ and $I(y) = 12$, there is no path $x \Rightarrow_n y$.

*Proof.* Lemma 6.10 gives $12 \le 4n \le 8$, a contradiction. $\square$

The bound is uniform: it constrains *every* realization of a unison and *every* realization of an octave, not just the canonical ones.

**Theorem 6.12 (Upper bound).** The maximally contrary path
$$(0,0) \to (-2,2) \to (-4,4) \to (-6,6)$$
consists of three permitted motions and realizes the intervals $0 \to 4 \to 8 \to 12$.

*Proof sketch.* Each transition moves the lower voice by $-2$ and the upper voice by $+2$, so all are stepwise and all are contrary motions; the intervals $0,4,8,12$ are consonances; the only perfect target is the final octave, approached by contrary motion, so clause (4) holds. $\square$

**Theorem 6.13 (Exact free-bass distance).** The distance from a unison sonority to an octave sonority in the free-bass system is exactly three permitted motions: a three-step path exists, and no path of length at most two exists for any realizing dyads.

*Proof.* Theorems 6.11 and 6.12. $\square$

**Corollary 6.14 (The spine is not geodesic).** The six-step contrary spine of Definition 6.3 connects unison to octave, but the same interval change is achievable in three steps. The spine's extra length is the price of visiting *every* consonance en route, which is a strictly stronger requirement.

## 7. Algorithms

Three computations summarize the development; all are elementary, and we record their complexities for completeness.

**Algorithm 7.1 (Register partition).** Given a sorted consonance ladder $L = (\ell_0 < \cdots < \ell_{m-1})$ and a width $w$, scan once and start a new block whenever $\ell_{k+1} - \ell_k > w$. The blocks are precisely the connected components of the gap graph (Theorem 5.3). Cost: $O(m)$ after sorting.

**Algorithm 7.2 (Critical width).** Given an arbitrary finite pitch set $S$, sort it and return $\max_k (\ell_{k+1} - \ell_k)$. By Theorem 5.3 this is the least $w$ for which the gap graph on $S$ is connected. Cost: $O(|S| \log |S|)$.

**Algorithm 7.3 (Reachability census).** Given the ladder and width, compute the block sizes $k_1,\dots,k_r$ by Algorithm 7.1; the number of reachable ordered pairs is $\sum_t k_t^2$ and the number of unreachable ordered pairs is $m^2 - \sum_t k_t^2$. For $L$ and $w = 2$ this returns $15$ and $34$ (Theorem 3.9). Cost: $O(m)$. Note that no transitive-closure computation is required — that is exactly the content of Theorem 3.5.

A brute-force check via repeated closure (e.g. an $O(m^3)$ Warshall pass, or breadth-first search from each state) returns the same answer and serves as an independent validation of Theorem 3.5.

## 8. Discussion

### 8.1 What kind of failure this is

The conjecture failed, but the failure is informative rather than merely negative, for three reasons.

*First*, the failure is caused by a **conserved quantity**, not by an accident of small numbers. The register is preserved by every legal step, so no amount of path-searching can change it. Universality (Theorem 3.11) makes this a structural statement: nothing subtler is available.

*Second*, the failure is **sharp**. It sits one semitone below a phase transition (Theorem 4.6) that is governed by a general principle valid for arbitrary integer sets (Theorem 5.3). A refutation that merely exhibited an unreachable pair would be far less useful than one that locates the exact parameter value at which the phenomenon disappears.

*Third*, the failure is **local to the model**. Free the bass and connectivity returns (Theorem 6.6), with an exact cost of three steps for the extremal pair (Theorem 6.13). In other words, the conjecture was false in letter and true in spirit; the guarded version — "the consonances are mutually reachable in the two-voice system, and the seven-state frozen-bass restriction is disconnected with exactly four components" — is both true and more informative.

### 8.2 Musical interpretation

The frozen bass is a modelling convenience: it selects one representative per interval so that the state space becomes finite. Its cost is precisely half the melodic mobility, because only one voice remains free. Since the consonance ladder has a maximal gap of $3$ and a single voice can move only $2$, the ladder disconnects.

This aligns with practice. Counterpoint pedagogy warns against static bass lines and prizes contrary motion; the theorems say that with a static bass the consonance space is literally inert (unisons stay unisons forever, Theorem 3.8) and that contrary motion is exactly the device that traverses the ladder (Lemma 6.5, Theorem 6.12). The prohibition on similar motion into a perfect consonance never binds in the frozen-bass model and binds mildly in the free-bass model — both of the spines exhibited approach their perfect consonances contrarily, so the prohibition costs nothing in reachability terms.

### 8.3 Scope and limitations

The model is deliberately minimal: two voices, note-against-note, a fixed consonance set, an absolute-value melodic bound, and a single prohibition on similar motion into perfect consonances. It omits modal scale membership, cadence formulas, range constraints, voice-crossing prohibitions, and any treatment of dissonance (suspensions, passing tones), all of which belong to later species. Adding constraints can only shrink the reachability relation, so the refutation (Theorem 3.7) is robust: any model refining this one still fails strong connectivity on the frozen-bass states, since the register remains conserved. The positive results (Theorems 4.6, 6.6, 6.13) are less robust in principle, because added constraints could invalidate the exhibited paths; we note that the exhibited paths use only stepwise contrary motion between consonances, the most conservative material available.

## 9. Future work

**Register spectrum of arbitrary consonance systems.** Connectivity of a pitch-interval system under a melodic-step bound is governed entirely by the consecutive gaps of its interval ladder, so any tuning system's counterpoint graph has a computable critical width equal to its maximal gap. Since the gap criterion (Theorem 5.3) is proved for arbitrary finite $S \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$, instantiating it for $n$-tone equal temperaments, just-intonation subsets, or Bohlen–Pierce scales is immediate and carries musicological payoff: one obtains, for each tuning, the smallest melodic leap that makes its consonance space navigable.

**Bass mobility as the true connectivity parameter.** The frozen bass, not the consonance set, creates the obstruction. Admitting bass displacement of at most $b$ per step makes the achievable interval change at most $2 + b$, so connectivity should hold as soon as $b \ge 1$, with the contrary spine as the extremal witness. A clean two-parameter phase diagram in $(w, b)$ — melodic width against bass mobility — would subsume both Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 6.6 as boundary cases.

**Metric geometry of the free-bass system.** Theorem 6.13 computes one distance exactly. The full distance matrix between consonance classes, and the diameter of the reachability graph restricted to consonant dyads within a fixed pitch range, are natural next targets; the potential argument of Lemma 6.10 gives generic lower bounds, and maximally contrary paths give matching upper bounds in many cases.

**Higher species and longer memory.** Second- and later-species counterpoint introduce dissonance under metrical control, which makes the transition relation depend on more than the immediately preceding sonority. Modelling this as a transition system on windows of states, and asking again for strong connectivity, would test whether the register phenomenon is an artifact of memorylessness.

**Three and more voices.** With $v$ voices the state is a tuple of pitches and the "interval" becomes a vector of pairwise intervals. The natural conjecture is that the critical melodic width is again the maximal gap of the relevant consonance set, but the interaction between simultaneous pairwise constraints (a chord must be consonant in several coordinates at once) may raise it.

## 10. Conclusion

Read as a transition system, first-species counterpoint restricted to seven frozen-bass interval representatives is not strongly connected. The obstruction is a complete four-valued invariant, the register, which cuts the consonance ladder $\{0,3,4,7,8,9,12\}$ at its three gaps of size three, yielding the blocks $\{0\}$, $\{3,4\}$, $\{7,8,9\}$, $\{12\}$. A legal motion exists exactly between same-register intervals, so the one-step relation is already an equivalence relation, transitive closure adds nothing, $15$ of $49$ ordered pairs are reachable and $34$ are not, the unison and the octave are totally isolated, and the generated thin category is a groupoid with discrete four-object skeleton. The invariant is universal: nothing finer is conserved.

The phenomenon has a sharp parameter. Reachability under melodic width $w$ is total if and only if $w \ge 3$, and this follows from a general criterion — a gap graph on a finite integer set is connected exactly when its consecutive gaps do not exceed the width — which makes the critical width a computable invariant of any tuning system.

Finally, the obstruction is an artifact of freezing the bass. With both voices free, all seven consonances are mutually reachable via an explicit contrary-motion spine, and a unison reaches an octave in exactly three motions, no fewer. The refuted conjecture is thus replaced by a guarded statement that is sharp in every parameter it mentions.

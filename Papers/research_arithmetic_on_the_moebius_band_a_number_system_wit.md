# Arithmetic on the Möbius Band: Refutation of the "Möbius Integers" and the Twist Ring $\mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^2-1)$

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-08-21

## Abstract

We study a proposal to build a number system on the Möbius band $M = ([0,1]\times\mathbb{R})/\!\big((0,y)\sim(1,-y)\big)$, in which the point $(x,y)$ represents the number $y(2x-1)$ and the integers are embedded by $n \mapsto \big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac1{2n},\,|n|\big)$. The proposal predicts (i) that the image $\mathbb{Z}_M$ of $\mathbb{Z}$ carries a ring structure induced from coordinatewise operations on $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, (ii) that $1$ and $-1$ are identified at the seam so that $\mathbb{Z}_M$ is a one-point compactification of $\mathbb{Z}$, (iii) that $(1,0)\cdot(0,1)=(0,0)$ exhibits zero divisors, and (iv) that orientation appears as a "twist prime" in a unique factorisation of the form $\pm p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$.

We establish exactly one confirmation and four refutations. The value map $\mathrm{val}(x,y) = y(2x-1)$ *is* well defined on $M$ — it is a nowhere-vanishing-off-the-core section of the twisted line bundle — and the seam identification $[(0,-1)] = [(1,1)]$ is genuine. However: neither coordinatewise addition nor coordinatewise multiplication descends to $M$; the value of the embedded integer $n$ is exactly $\operatorname{sign}(n)$, so the embedding collapses $\mathbb{Z}$ onto three values; the embedding is injective into $M$ and its image is unbounded, so $1$ and $-1$ are *not* identified and $\mathbb{Z}_M$ is not compact; and the alleged nonzero factor $(1,0)$ equals $0$ in $M$ (and is not a Möbius integer).

We then relocate the surviving intuition into a genuine algebraic object: the **twist ring** $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}} = \mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^2-1)$, realised concretely as the subring $\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}^2 : u\equiv v \bmod 2\}$ of $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ via $a+bt \mapsto (a+b, a-b)$. We prove: the norm $N(a+bt) = a^2-b^2$ is multiplicative and detects units; the unit group is $\{\pm1,\pm t\}\cong(\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$, so the twist is a unit and hence never prime; $z$ is a zero divisor iff $N(z)=0$ iff $a=\pm b$, the algebraic image of the seam; no element has norm $\pm 2$, whence $2$ is irreducible while every odd integer $2k+1 = ((k+1)+kt)((k+1)-kt)$ splits, giving the classification "$n\in\mathbb{Z}$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ iff $n=\pm2$"; consequently $6 = 2(2+t)(2-t)$ has three irreducible factors, $-6$ is that product times the unit $-1$, and $0 = (1+t)(1-t)$; the ring has no nontrivial idempotents, hence $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}} \not\cong \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ despite $t^2=1$.

Finally we connect the algebra to the geometry through sections. Antiperiodic functions ($f(x+1)=-f(x)$) and periodic functions form a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded multiplicative system whose grading group is exactly the one generated by $t$; the holonomy $f(x+n) = (-1)^n f(x)$ is computed by $t^n$; and every continuous section of the Möbius bundle has a zero in every window $[a,a+1]$, so the odd part contains no units. The unified conclusion is that orientation is a **grading**, acting by units, and not a prime.

**Keywords:** Möbius band, non-orientability, twist ring, group ring $\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z}/2]$, hyperbolic norm form, zero divisors, irreducibility, antiperiodic functions, holonomy.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The proposal

The Möbius band is the simplest non-orientable surface, and its defining feature is a holonomy of order two: transport an orientation once around the core circle and it reverses; transport it twice and it returns. Signs in arithmetic behave the same way. It is therefore natural to ask whether one can *identify* the two phenomena — build a number system on the Möbius band in which the geometric twist becomes the arithmetic minus sign, and in which orientation itself functions as an irreducible element, a "twist prime".

Concretely, the proposal we test is the following.

**Model.** Let $M$ be the quotient of $[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}$ (equivalently, of $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, with points off the seam identified only with themselves) by the relation generated by
$$(0,y) \sim (1,-y).$$

**Value.** The point $(x,y)$ is to represent the number $\mathrm{val}(x,y) = y(2x-1)$, so that $x$ contributes a sign-like factor and $y$ a scale.

**Möbius integers.** $\mathbb{Z}_M$ is the image of the map
$$\mathrm{emb}(n) = \Big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac1{2n},\ |n|\Big), \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

**Claims.** (C1) $\mathrm{val}$ descends to $M$. (C2) $\mathbb{Z}_M$ is a ring under the operations induced from coordinatewise $+$ and $\cdot$ on $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$. (C3) $1$ and $-1$ are identified at the twist point, making $\mathbb{Z}_M$ a one-point compactification of $\mathbb{Z}$. (C4) $\mathbb{Z}_M$ is not a domain, because $(1,0)\cdot(0,1) = (0,0)$ with neither factor zero. (C5) Every nonzero Möbius integer factors as $\pm p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$ with the overall sign being a "twist prime"; e.g. $6 = 2_+3_+$ and $-6 = 2_+3_+\cdot(-1)$.

### 1.2 Results

Section 2 confirms (C1) and refutes (C2)–(C4) and the underlying premise of (C5). Section 3 constructs the correct home of the idea, the twist ring, and proves a complete unit/irreducibility/zero-divisor analysis, including the corrected form of the factorisation test. Section 4 connects the algebra to the analysis of sections of the Möbius line bundle. Section 5 gives algorithms; Sections 6–8 discuss applications, limitations, and future work.

Three structural principles emerge and organise the whole paper.

1. **Twist = grading, not sign, not prime.** Everything (C5) wanted from "orientation is prime" is realised by a degree-one element $t$ with $t^2=1$, which is necessarily a *unit*. Orientation data is a grading group, and grading groups act by units.
2. **The zero-divisor locus is the seam.** In the twist ring, $z$ is a zero divisor iff $N(z)=0$, i.e. iff $a=\pm b$: exactly two lines, the algebraic image of the identification $(0,y)\sim(1,-y)$.
3. **Splitting is governed by $a^2-b^2$.** Odd integers split; $2$ stays irreducible; no element has norm $\pm2$. The arithmetic of the twist ring is the arithmetic of the hyperbolic quadratic form, not of a quadratic *field*.

## 2. The Möbius band and the proposed number system

### 2.1 The band as a quotient

**Definition 2.1 (seam relation).** For $p,q \in \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ declare $p \mathrel{R} q$ iff
$$p = q \quad\text{or}\quad \big(p_1=0,\ q_1=1,\ q_2=-p_2\big) \quad\text{or}\quad \big(p_1=1,\ q_1=0,\ q_2=-p_2\big).$$

**Proposition 2.2.** $R$ is an equivalence relation.

*Proof sketch.* Reflexivity is the first clause. Symmetry swaps the second and third clauses and uses $-(-y)=y$. For transitivity, the only interesting composites are seam-then-seam: if $p_1=0,q_1=1,q_2=-p_2$ and $q_1=1,r_1=0,r_2=-q_2$, then $r=(0,p_2)=p$; and the mixed composite "$p_1=0,q_1=1$" followed by "$q_1=0$" is vacuous because $1\ne0$. $\square$

Write $M = (\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})/R$ and $[p]$ for the class of $p$. By construction $[p]=[q]$ iff $p\mathrel{R}q$. The subspace $\{0\le x\le 1\}$ maps onto the usual Möbius band; the *seam* is the image of $\{x\in\{0,1\}\}$, the *core* is the image of $\{y=0\}$.

### 2.2 Confirmed: the value map descends

**Definition 2.3.** $\mathrm{val}(x,y) := y(2x-1)$.

**Theorem 2.4 (the value map is well defined).** If $p \mathrel{R} q$ then $\mathrm{val}(p) = \mathrm{val}(q)$. Hence there is a unique function $\overline{\mathrm{val}} : M \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\overline{\mathrm{val}}([p]) = \mathrm{val}(p)$.

*Proof.* Only the seam clauses need checking. If $p=(0,y)$ and $q=(1,-y)$ then $\mathrm{val}(p) = y(2\cdot0-1) = -y$ and $\mathrm{val}(q) = (-y)(2\cdot1-1) = -y$. The other clause is symmetric. $\square$

This is precisely the statement that $x \mapsto 2x-1$ is a *section of the Möbius line bundle*: a fibrewise-linear rule that reverses sign across the seam, so that the reversal of the fibre coordinate is compensated exactly.

**Theorem 2.5 (the twist point).** $[(0,-1)] = [(1,1)]$.

*Proof.* Take $p=(0,-1)$, $q=(1,1)$; then $p_1=0$, $q_1=1$, $q_2 = 1 = -(-1) = -p_2$. $\square$

So the geometric content of the proposal is sound. What follows is not.

### 2.3 Refuted: no induced ring operations

**Theorem 2.6 (no induced addition).** There is no map $f : M\times M \to M$ with $f([p],[q]) = [p+q]$ for all $p,q\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$.

*Proof.* Note $[(0,1)] = [(1,-1)]$ by the seam clause. If such $f$ existed, then
$$[(0,1)+(0,1)] = f([(0,1)],[(0,1)]) = f([(1,-1)],[(1,-1)]) = [(1,-1)+(1,-1)],$$
i.e. $[(0,2)] = [(2,-2)]$. But $(0,2) \ne (2,-2)$, and neither seam clause applies because $(2,-2)$ has first coordinate $2 \notin \{0,1\}$. Contradiction. $\square$

**Theorem 2.7 (no induced multiplication).** There is no map $f:M\times M\to M$ with $f([p],[q]) = [p\cdot q]$ (coordinatewise product).

*Proof.* With the same seam identity, existence of $f$ forces $[(0,1)\cdot(0,1)] = [(1,-1)\cdot(1,-1)]$, i.e. $[(0,1)] = [(1,1)]$. The two points differ, and the seam clause relating first coordinates $0$ and $1$ demands second coordinates negatives of each other, whereas both equal $1$. Contradiction. $\square$

**Remark 2.8.** These failures are structural, not accidental. The gluing is a *fibrewise linear* identification: it identifies the fibre over $0$ with the fibre over $1$ by $y \mapsto -y$, and says nothing about the base. Coordinatewise operations mix base coordinates in a way the quotient cannot see; moreover $\{0,1\}$ is not closed under $+$ or $\cdot$ in a manner compatible with the relation. Any algebra on $M$ must therefore be fibrewise (which is exactly what a line bundle supports: addition of sections and multiplication by *periodic* scalars — see Section 4).

### 2.4 Refuted: the value map collapses $\mathbb{Z}$

**Theorem 2.9 (the embedding records only the sign).** For all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$,
$$\mathrm{val}\big(\mathrm{emb}(n)\big) = \operatorname{sign}(n) = \begin{cases} 1, & n>0,\\ 0, & n=0,\\ -1,& n<0.\end{cases}$$

*Proof.* For $n=0$ the definition gives $\mathrm{emb}(0) = (\tfrac12 + \tfrac10 \text{ interpreted as } \tfrac12, 0)$ with fibre coordinate $0$, so the value is $0$. For $n\ne 0$,
$$\mathrm{val}(\mathrm{emb}(n)) = |n|\Big(2\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac1{2n}\big)-1\Big) = |n|\cdot\frac1n = \frac{|n|}{n},$$
which is $1$ for $n>0$ and $-1$ for $n<0$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.10 (collapse).** $\overline{\mathrm{val}}([\mathrm{emb}(2)]) = \overline{\mathrm{val}}([\mathrm{emb}(3)]) = 1$. The proposed number system cannot distinguish $2$ from $3$.

The mechanism is transparent: the magnitude $|n|$ placed in the fibre is cancelled exactly by the $1/(2n)$ placed in the base. The map $\mathrm{emb}$ is a parametrisation of a curve on which $\mathrm{val}$ is locally constant, not a numeral system.

### 2.5 Refuted: no identification, no compactification

**Lemma 2.11 (seam detection).** $(\mathrm{emb}\,n)_1 = 0 \iff n = -1$, and $(\mathrm{emb}\,n)_1 = 1 \iff n = 1$.

*Proof.* For $n\ne 0$, $\tfrac12+\tfrac1{2n} = 0 \iff n = -1$ and $\tfrac12+\tfrac1{2n} = 1 \iff n = 1$; the case $n=0$ gives first coordinate $\tfrac12$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.12 (injectivity in the plane).** $\mathrm{emb}(m) = \mathrm{emb}(n) \iff m = n$.

*Proof sketch.* Equality of second coordinates gives $|m| = |n|$; for $m,n\ne0$ equality of first coordinates gives $1/m = 1/n$, hence $m=n$; and $|m|=|n|=0$ forces $m=n=0$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.13 ($1$ and $-1$ are not identified).** $[\mathrm{emb}(1)] \ne [\mathrm{emb}(-1)]$.

*Proof.* $\mathrm{emb}(1) = (1,1)$ and $\mathrm{emb}(-1) = (0,1)$. They are distinct points, so equality in $M$ would require a seam clause; the clause relating first coordinates $0$ and $1$ demands that the two fibre coordinates be negatives of each other, but both equal $1$ and $1 \ne -1$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.14 (injectivity into the band).** $n \mapsto [\mathrm{emb}(n)]$ is injective $\mathbb{Z}\to M$.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $[\mathrm{emb}(m)] = [\mathrm{emb}(n)]$. Either $\mathrm{emb}(m) = \mathrm{emb}(n)$, whence $m=n$ by Lemma 2.12; or a seam clause applies, which by Lemma 2.11 forces $\{m,n\} = \{1,-1\}$ and then requires $|1| = -|{-1}|$, i.e. $1=-1$, absurd. $\square$

**Theorem 2.15 (unboundedness, hence non-compactness).** For every $C>0$ there is $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $\|\mathrm{emb}(n)\| > C$; consequently the set $\{\mathrm{emb}(n) : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is not compact.

*Proof.* Choose a natural number $k > \max(C,0)$; the fibre coordinate of $\mathrm{emb}(k)$ is $k > C$, and the sup-norm dominates it. A compact set in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is bounded. $\square$

Thus $\mathbb{Z}_M \cong \mathbb{Z}$ as a set, with no point at infinity. The twist that was supposed to merge $\pm1$ is precisely what keeps them apart, since it reverses the fibre coordinate while $\mathrm{emb}$ makes both fibre coordinates positive ($=|n|$).

### 2.6 Refuted: the zero-divisor witness

**Theorem 2.16 (the alleged nonzero factor is zero).** $[(1,0)] = [(0,0)]$.

*Proof.* Take $p = (1,0)$, $q=(0,0)$: then $p_1=1$, $q_1=0$, and $q_2 = 0 = -0 = -p_2$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.17.** $(1,0)$ is not a Möbius integer: $(1,0) \notin \{\mathrm{emb}(n)\}$.

*Proof.* $|n| = 0$ forces $n=0$, whose first coordinate is $\tfrac12 \ne 1$. $\square$

Hence the identity $(1,0)\cdot(0,1)=(0,0)$ exhibits no zero divisor in $M$: one factor is the zero of the band. Geometrically, the seam pinches the zero section $\{y=0\}$ into a circle — the core — and $(1,0)$ lies on it.

**Summary of Section 2.** Of the five claims, (C1) holds; (C2), (C3), (C4) fail; and (C5) has no subject matter, since $\mathbb{Z}_M$ carries no induced multiplication and its value map does not even separate $2$ from $3$.

## 3. The twist ring $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}} = \mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^2-1)$

The surviving intuition is that going once around the band applies an involution. We now attach that involution to an algebraic object where it is well defined.

### 3.1 Definition and a concrete model

**Definition 3.1.** $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}} := \mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^2-1)$, whose elements are written uniquely as $a+bt$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$, with
$$(a+bt)+(c+dt) = (a+c)+(b+d)t, \qquad (a+bt)(c+dt) = (ac+bd)+(ad+bc)t.$$
This is the integral group ring $\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z}/2]$.

**Definition 3.2 (character coordinates).** Let $\chi_{\pm}$ be the two ring maps $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}\to\mathbb{Z}$ sending $t\mapsto \pm1$, and set
$$\Phi(a+bt) := (\chi_+(a+bt), \chi_-(a+bt)) = (a+b,\ a-b).$$

**Proposition 3.3 (model).** $\Phi$ is an injective ring homomorphism with image
$$E := \{(u,v)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z} : u \equiv v \pmod 2\},$$
a subring of $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$. Under $\Phi$, addition and multiplication are componentwise, and $\Phi^{-1}(u,v) = \tfrac{u+v}2 + \tfrac{u-v}2 t$.

*Proof sketch.* $\Phi$ is a ring map because $\chi_\pm$ are. Since $(a+b)-(a-b) = 2b$ is even, the image lies in $E$; conversely if $u\equiv v$, the integers $a=(u+v)/2$, $b=(u-v)/2$ are well defined and map to $(u,v)$. Injectivity: $a+b$ and $a-b$ determine $a,b$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. Closure of $E$ under $+,\times,-$ follows from parity arithmetic: $u\equiv v$ and $u'\equiv v'$ give $uu'\equiv vv'$ and $u+u'\equiv v+v'$ mod $2$. $\square$

All subsequent computations use this model, in which the ring axioms are inherited for free.

### 3.2 The twist is a unit, not a prime

**Definition 3.4.** The **twist** is $t = 0 + 1\cdot t$, with $\Phi(t) = (1,-1)$.

**Theorem 3.5.** $t^2 = 1$, $t \ne 1$, $t \ne -1$, and $t$ is a unit (its own inverse). Consequently $t$ is **not** prime and not irreducible.

*Proof.* $\Phi(t)^2 = (1,1) = \Phi(1)$, and $(1,-1)\notin\{(1,1),(-1,-1)\}$. A unit is by definition excluded from being prime or irreducible. $\square$

**Theorem 3.6 (holonomy).** For $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $t^n = 1$ if $n$ is even and $t^n = t$ if $n$ is odd; in particular $t^n=1 \iff n$ even. The holonomy homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}\to \mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}^{\times}$, $n\mapsto t^n$, has kernel $2\mathbb{Z}$, matching $\sigma^2=\mathrm{id}$ for the deck transformation $\sigma(x,y) = (x+1,-y)$ of the orientation double cover of the band.

*Proof.* Induction using $t^2=1$; distinctness of $t$ and $1$ gives the "only if". $\square$

### 3.3 The norm and the unit group

**Definition 3.7.** $N(a+bt) := \chi_+(a+bt)\,\chi_-(a+bt) = (a+b)(a-b) = a^2-b^2$.

**Theorem 3.8 (multiplicativity).** $N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$ for all $z,w$.

*Proof.* In the model, $N$ is the product of the two coordinates, and multiplication is componentwise. $\square$

**Theorem 3.9 (units).** $z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is a unit iff $N(z) = \pm1$. Explicitly,
$$\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}^{\times} = \{1,-1,t,-t\} \cong (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2 .$$

*Proof sketch.* If $zw=1$ then $N(z)N(w)=1$, so $N(z)=\pm1$. Conversely $N(z)=\pm1$ means the product of the coordinates $(u,v)$ of $z$ is $\pm1$, so $u,v\in\{\pm1\}$ and $z^2 = (u^2,v^2) = (1,1) = 1$, making $z$ its own inverse. In terms of $a+bt$: $(a+b)(a-b)=\pm1$ forces $(a,b)\in\{(1,0),(-1,0),(0,1),(0,-1)\}$. The group is elementary abelian of order $4$ since every element squares to $1$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.10.** The sign $-1$ and the twist $t$ are *independent* units; in particular the twist is not the sign. For example $6t \ne 6$ and $6t \ne -6$, since $\Phi(6t) = (6,-6)$ while $\Phi(\pm6) = \pm(6,6)$.

### 3.4 Zero divisors: the seam, algebraically

**Theorem 3.11.** $(1+t)(1-t) = 1-t^2 = 0$, and $1\pm t \ne 0$. Hence $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is a commutative ring that is not an integral domain.

**Theorem 3.12 (zero-divisor locus).** For $z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ the following are equivalent: (i) there exists $w\ne0$ with $zw=0$; (ii) $N(z)=0$; (iii) writing $z = a+bt$, $a = b$ or $a = -b$.

*Proof.* In the model $z=(u,v)$, and $zw = (uw_1, vw_2)$. If $u=0$, take $w = 1+t \mapsto (2,0)$, which is nonzero and gives $zw = (0\cdot2,\ v\cdot0) = 0$; symmetrically, if $v=0$ take $w = 1-t \mapsto (0,2)$. Conversely if $zw=0$ with $w\ne0$, some coordinate of $w$ is nonzero and the corresponding coordinate of $z$ must vanish, so $N(z) = uv = 0$. Finally $uv = (a+b)(a-b) = a^2-b^2 = 0 \iff a=\pm b$. $\square$

The locus $\{a=b\}\cup\{a=-b\}$ is a union of two lines through the origin: the algebraic image of the identification $(0,y)\sim(1,-y)$, which likewise glues each fibre line to its reflection.

### 3.5 The parity obstruction and irreducibility

**Theorem 3.13 (no norm $\pm2$).** For every $z \in \mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$, $N(z)\notin\{2,-2\}$.

*Proof.* Write $\Phi(z) = (u,v)$ with $u\equiv v \pmod 2$. If both are odd, $N(z)=uv$ is odd. If both are even, $4 \mid uv$. Neither case allows $|uv| = 2$. $\square$

Equivalently: $a^2-b^2 = \pm2$ has no integer solutions, since $a^2-b^2 \equiv 0,1,3 \pmod 4$.

**Theorem 3.14 (prime norm $\Rightarrow$ irreducible).** If $|N(z)|$ is a rational prime, then $z$ is irreducible.

*Proof.* $z$ is not a unit since $|N(z)|\ne1$. If $z=xy$ then $|N(x)|\,|N(y)| = |N(z)|$ is prime, so one factor has $|N|=1$ and is a unit by Theorem 3.9. $\square$

**Theorem 3.15 (norm $4$ $\Rightarrow$ irreducible).** If $N(z)=4$ then $z$ is irreducible.

*Proof.* $z$ is not a unit. If $z = xy$ then $|N(x)|\,|N(y)| = 4$ with neither factor equal to $2$ (Theorem 3.13); the only factorisations of $4$ into positive integers avoiding $2$ are $1\cdot4$ and $4\cdot1$, so some factor has $|N|=1$ and is a unit. $\square$

**Corollary 3.16.** $2$ and $-2$ are irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ ($N(\pm2) = 4$). Likewise $2\pm t$ are irreducible, since $N(2\pm t) = 3$.

**Theorem 3.17 (every odd integer splits).** For every $k\in\mathbb{Z}$,
$$2k+1 = \big((k+1) + kt\big)\big((k+1) - kt\big),$$
and for $k \notin \{0,-1\}$ (i.e. $2k+1 \ne \pm1$) neither factor is a unit.

*Proof.* Multiply out: $(k+1)^2 - k^2 = 2k+1$ and the $t$-coefficient $-(k+1)k + k(k+1) = 0$. Non-unitness follows from the explicit unit list of Theorem 3.9: $(k+1,\pm k)$ is one of $(\pm1,0),(0,\pm1)$ only if $k\in\{0,-1\}$. $\square$

For example $3 = (2+t)(2-t)$, $5 = (3+2t)(3-2t)$, $7 = (4+3t)(4-3t)$.

**Theorem 3.18 (classification of irreducible rational integers).** For $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, the element $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is irreducible **iff** $n = \pm 2$.

*Proof sketch.* ($\Leftarrow$) Corollary 3.16. ($\Rightarrow$) Let $n$ be irreducible. If $n$ is even, write $n=2m$. If $m=0$ then $n=0$, which is reducible by $0=(1+t)(1-t)$ (indeed $0$ is never irreducible). If $m = \pm1$ then $n=\pm2$. Otherwise $n = 2\cdot m$ with both factors non-units, contradicting irreducibility. If $n$ is odd, then $n=\pm1$ are units (excluded), and otherwise $n = 2k+1$ with $k\notin\{0,-1\}$ splits nontrivially by Theorem 3.17. $\square$

So the twist ring inverts our intuition about $\mathbb{Z}$: **the only irreducible rational integer is $2$**, the unique prime that the hyperbolic form $a^2-b^2$ cannot represent (up to sign) because of the parity obstruction. Every odd prime becomes a product of two conjugate irreducibles.

### 3.6 The factorisation test, answered

**Theorem 3.19 (the three test cases).**
1. $6 = 2\,(2+t)(2-t)$ with all three factors irreducible: $6$ has **three** irreducible factors in $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$, not two.
2. $-6 = (-1)\cdot 2\,(2+t)(2-t)$, where $-1$ is a **unit**, not a prime; and $-1 \ne t$, so the sign is not the twist. Moreover $6t \notin\{6,-6\}$.
3. $0 = (1+t)(1-t)$ with both factors nonzero: even zero admits a nontrivial factorisation, impossible in $\mathbb{Z}$.

*Proof.* (1) $2\cdot(2+t)(2-t) = 2\cdot(4-1) = 6$; irreducibility from Corollary 3.16. (2) Multiply (1) by $-1$; $(-1)^2=1$ so $-1$ is a unit; $\Phi(-1) = (-1,-1) \ne (1,-1) = \Phi(t)$. (3) Theorem 3.11. $\square$

This is the corrected version of claim (C5). The overall "sign" of a factorisation is a unit, as in $\mathbb{Z}$; but the unit group is now the Klein four-group $\{\pm1,\pm t\}$, and the extra generator $t$ is the orientation label. Factorisation is *not* unique even up to units in the naive sense — the presence of zero divisors means $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is not a UFD — but Theorem 3.18 gives a complete description on the rational-integer locus.

### 3.7 Nonsplitness: no nontrivial idempotents

Over $\mathbb{Q}$, the relation $t^2=1$ splits the ring: $e_\pm = \tfrac12(1\pm t)$ are orthogonal idempotents with $e_++e_-=1$, and $\mathbb{Q}[t]/(t^2-1)\cong\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the halving is unavailable, and this is not a technicality:

**Theorem 3.20 (idempotent rigidity).** If $e\in\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ satisfies $e^2=e$ then $e = 0$ or $e=1$.

*Proof.* In coordinates $e=(u,v)$ with $u\equiv v \pmod 2$, and $u^2=u$, $v^2=v$ give $u,v\in\{0,1\}$. The parity condition rules out $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$, leaving $(0,0)=0$ and $(1,1)=1$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.21 (nonsplitting).** $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}} \not\cong \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ as rings.

*Proof.* $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ contains the idempotent $(1,0)\notin\{0,1\}$; a ring isomorphism preserves idempotents and $0,1$, contradicting Theorem 3.20. $\square$

The congruence $u\equiv v \pmod 2$ is thus an *integral gluing datum*: the two "orientation eigenspaces" cannot be separated over $\mathbb{Z}$, only after inverting $2$. This is the arithmetic fingerprint of non-orientability, and it is a $2$-adic phenomenon — the same $2$ that obstructs norm values $\pm2$ and keeps $2$ irreducible.

## 4. Sections of the Möbius line bundle: grading, holonomy, and forced zeros

We now show that the twist ring genuinely computes the geometry, by working with sections rather than points.

**Definition 4.1.** A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is *periodic* if $f(x+1)=f(x)$ for all $x$, and *antiperiodic* if $f(x+1) = -f(x)$. Write $P$ for the periodic functions and $A$ for the antiperiodic ones. Periodic functions are sections of the trivial line bundle over the circle $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$; antiperiodic functions are sections of the Möbius line bundle, since crossing the seam reverses the fibre.

### 4.1 The $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading

**Theorem 4.2 (grading rules).** For $f,g$ as indicated, pointwise products satisfy
$$P\cdot P\subseteq P, \qquad P\cdot A \subseteq A, \qquad A\cdot A\subseteq P.$$

*Proof.* If $f,g\in A$ then $f(x+1)g(x+1) = (-f(x))(-g(x)) = f(x)g(x)$. If $f\in P,g\in A$ then the product picks up exactly one sign. If $f,g\in P$ the product is periodic. $\square$

**Theorem 4.3 (directness).** $P\cap A = \{0\}$.

*Proof.* If $f$ is both, then $f(x) = f(x+1) = -f(x)$, so $2f(x)=0$. $\square$

So $P\oplus A$ is a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded multiplicative system whose grading group is the group $\{1,t\}$ generated by the twist. The group ring of that grading group is exactly $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$; Theorems 3.5 and 4.2 are the same statement in two languages.

**Theorem 4.4 (the odd part is not a ring).** $A$ is not closed under multiplication: $f(x) = \cos(\pi x)$ is antiperiodic, but $f^2$ is not.

*Proof.* $\cos(\pi(x+1)) = -\cos(\pi x)$. If $f^2$ were antiperiodic, then at $x=0$ we would get $\cos^2(\pi) = -\cos^2(0)$, i.e. $1 = -1$. $\square$

Consequently, Möbius sections form a *module* over the periodic functions, never an algebra — an intrinsic reason why (C2) had to fail: the twisted objects are not closed under the multiplication one hopes to impose.

### 4.2 Holonomy computed by the twist ring

**Theorem 4.5 (shift law).** If $f\in A$ then $f(x+n) = (-1)^n f(x)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$, applying antiperiodicity at $x+n$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.6 (bridge).** For $f\in A$, $n\in\mathbb{N}$, $x\in\mathbb{R}$,
$$f(x+n) = \varepsilon_n f(x), \qquad \varepsilon_n = \begin{cases}+1 & \text{if } t^n = 1 \text{ in } \mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}},\\ -1 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

*Proof.* Combine Theorem 4.5 with Theorem 3.6: $t^n=1$ iff $n$ is even iff $(-1)^n=1$. $\square$

The sign accumulated by walking $n$ times around the band is *literally* the image of $t^n$ under the sign character. The algebra is not an analogy; it is the holonomy group ring.

### 4.3 Forced zeros: non-orientability made analytic

**Theorem 4.7 (a zero in every window).** Let $f$ be continuous and antiperiodic. Then for every $a\in\mathbb{R}$ there exists $x\in[a,a+1]$ with $f(x)=0$.

*Proof.* $f(a+1) = -f(a)$, so $0$ lies between $f(a)$ and $f(a+1)$ (in one order or the other). By the intermediate value theorem on $[a,a+1]$, $f$ attains $0$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.8.** (i) There is $x\in[0,1]$ with $f(x)=0$. (ii) The zero set is unbounded: for every $C$ there is $x\ge C$ with $f(x)=0$. (iii) For every $n\in\mathbb{N}$ and every $k<n$, the window $[k,k+1]\subseteq[0,n]$ contains a zero; so $[0,n]$ contains at least $n$ zeros.

**Theorem 4.9 (no nowhere-vanishing section).** There is no continuous $f$ with $f(x+1)=-f(x)$ and $f(x)\ne0$ for all $x$. Equivalently, the Möbius line bundle over the circle is nontrivial.

**Theorem 4.10 (no units in the odd part).** If $f$ is continuous and antiperiodic, there is no $g$ with $f(x)g(x)=1$ for all $x$. By contrast the even part contains units, e.g. the constant function $1$. Hence all units of the graded system live in degree $0$.

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 4.7: at a zero $x_0$ of $f$ we would need $0 = 1$. $\square$

Theorem 4.10 is the analytic counterpart of Theorem 3.5, and together they sharpen the moral. In the twist ring the orientation class *is* a unit (so it cannot be a prime); in the ring of continuous functions the odd part contains *no* units (so orientation cannot be realised as multiplication by an invertible section). Both say: orientation is a **grading**. A grading labels; it does not generate.

## 5. Algorithms

The twist ring is entirely computable, using the character coordinates $(u,v) = (a+b, a-b)$ with $u\equiv v\pmod2$.

### 5.1 Arithmetic and norm

Addition and multiplication in $(a,b)$-coordinates:
$$(a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,\,b+d), \qquad (a,b)\cdot(c,d) = (ac+bd,\ ad+bc),$$
each $O(1)$ integer operations (or $O(M(\log n))$ bit operations for $n$-bit inputs, where $M$ is integer multiplication). The norm $N(a,b) = a^2-b^2 = (a+b)(a-b)$ costs one multiplication in character coordinates.

### 5.2 Unit and zero-divisor tests

$z$ is a unit iff $N(z)\in\{1,-1\}$; $z$ is a zero divisor iff $N(z)=0$. Both are $O(1)$ after one norm evaluation. When $N(z)=0$, an explicit annihilator is $1+t$ if $\chi_+(z)=0$ and $1-t$ if $\chi_-(z)=0$.

### 5.3 Factorisation of a rational integer

Given $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, produce a factorisation into irreducibles of $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$:

1. If $n=0$, return the zero-divisor factorisation $(1+t)(1-t)$.
2. If $n=\pm1$, return the unit $n$.
3. Write $n = \pm 2^{e} m$ with $m$ odd, $m>0$. Emit the sign as a unit and $e$ copies of the irreducible $2$.
4. Factor $m$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ into odd primes $p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}$ (cost: any integer-factorisation routine; trial division is $O(\sqrt m)$).
5. Replace each odd prime $p = 2k_p+1$ by the conjugate pair $\big((k_p+1)+k_p t\big)\big((k_p+1)-k_p t\big)$, each irreducible because its norm is $\pm p$.

The number of irreducible factors emitted for $n = \pm2^e m$ is $e + 2\Omega(m)$, where $\Omega$ counts odd prime factors with multiplicity: **odd primes double, the prime $2$ does not**. For $n=6=2\cdot3$ this yields $2\cdot(2+t)\cdot(2-t)$, three factors.

### 5.4 Certified splitting of an odd prime

Given odd $p = 2k+1$, output $(k+1)+kt$ and $(k+1)-kt$ together with the certificate $N((k+1)\pm kt) = (k+1)^2-k^2 = p$, which proves irreducibility of each factor by Theorem 3.14 whenever $p$ is prime. Cost: $O(1)$.

### 5.5 Locating forced zeros of a Möbius section

Given a continuous antiperiodic $f$ and a window $[a,a+1]$, bisection converges to a zero: evaluate at the midpoint, keep the subinterval on which the endpoint values have opposite signs (one always does, since $f(a+1)=-f(a)$). After $k$ steps the zero is localised to width $2^{-k}$; cost $O(k)$ evaluations. This is a constructive form of Theorem 4.7.

## 6. Applications and interpretation

**Orientation as a grading, not a generator.** The central lesson generalises: whenever a finite-order geometric symmetry is imported into algebra, it appears in the *unit group* and induces a grading, not among the irreducible elements. Formally, a $G$-grading of a ring is the same as a coaction of the group ring $\mathbb{Z}[G]$, and $G$ sits inside $\mathbb{Z}[G]^\times$. Searching for a "twist prime" is therefore a category error that the twist ring makes visible.

**A calibrated non-domain.** $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is a small, fully explicit commutative ring with zero divisors, four units, a multiplicative norm, and a complete classification of irreducible rational integers. It is a useful test case for algorithms and statements about factorisation outside integral domains — an environment where $0$ itself factors nontrivially and where $2$ is the *only* irreducible rational integer.

**Signal processing and parity-encoded data.** Antiperiodic signals ($f(x+1) = -f(x)$) are ubiquitous: half-wave symmetry in AC waveforms, odd harmonics only, and the "$\pi$-shifted" structure of certain modulation schemes. Theorem 4.7 says such a signal must cross zero at least once per period — a hard, model-free guarantee — while Theorem 4.2 says the algebra of such signals is graded, so products of two odd-parity signals are even. The twist ring is the finite bookkeeping device for that parity, and the norm $a^2-b^2$ is exactly the product of the even-channel and odd-channel amplitudes.

**Two-channel decompositions in learning systems.** Any construction that splits data into a symmetric channel and an antisymmetric channel — even/odd feature decompositions, sign-equivariant parameterisations, $\pm1$-valued gauge variables — is a representation of the group of order two and therefore a $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$-module. Theorem 3.20 warns that the two channels cannot be separated *integrally*: the decomposition into eigenchannels requires dividing by $2$, so quantised or integer-constrained implementations retain a genuine coupling. The forced zeros of Theorem 4.7 give the analytic version: an antisymmetric response cannot be uniformly bounded away from zero.

**Physics analogy, corrected.** The conjecture's intuition — that orientation should behave like spin — is right in the sense that both are $\mathbb{Z}/2$ labels realised by an order-two symmetry acting by units, and both need two laps to return ($t^2=1$ after two crossings; $4\pi$ rotation for a spinor). It is wrong in the sense that spin is not a *constituent*, and neither is orientation.

## 7. Discussion and limitations

**What exactly was refuted.** We refuted (C2)–(C4) *as stated*, in the specific model given. In particular, Theorem 2.6 and Theorem 2.7 refute the existence of *any* operation compatible with coordinatewise operations on representatives; they do not, of course, say that $M$ admits no algebraic structure whatever. Indeed $M$ supports fibrewise addition of sections and a module structure over the periodic functions, which is Section 4.

**Repairs that do not work.** Replacing $\mathrm{emb}$ by another curve cannot rescue (C3): any injective map into $M$ landing off the seam is automatically non-identifying, and to glue $n$ with $-n$ one needs the fibre coordinates to be *negatives*, whereas $|n|$ makes them equal. Likewise no reparametrisation restores (C4): the seam identifies $(1,0)$ with $(0,0)$ for the trivial reason that $-0=0$, so the entire core circle is a single "zero direction" locus in whatever sense the value map assigns.

**Non-uniqueness of factorisation.** $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ is not a UFD; it is not even a domain. Theorem 3.18 classifies irreducible rational integers, but a general element $a+bt$ with $N(a+bt)$ composite may admit genuinely different factorisations, and elements on the seam lines ($a=\pm b$) are zero divisors. A complete factorisation theory would be a theory of the hyperbolic form $a^2-b^2$ modulo the parity condition, and is best organised through the two characters.

**Scope of the section results.** Theorems 4.7–4.10 are stated for real-valued continuous sections of the rank-one Möbius bundle. Higher-rank twisted bundles behave differently: an antiperiodic map $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^d$ with $d\ge2$ need not vanish (e.g. $x\mapsto(\cos\pi x,\sin\pi x)$ is antiperiodic and nowhere zero), so the rank threshold is exactly $1$ for forced vanishing.

## 8. Future directions

**Twisted group-ring factorisation spectrum.** For a finite abelian group $G$, the multiset of irreducible factors of a rational prime $p$ in $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ should be computed by the splitting of $p$ in the ring of integers of each cyclotomic character factor, together with a uniform "norm $\pm2$ obstruction" generalising Theorem 3.13. The key insight is that the form $a^2-b^2$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z}/2]$ is the field norm glued along the character decomposition, so the local obstruction should become a congruence condition on $p$ modulo $\exp(G)$ times a $2$-adic defect. The $\mathbb{Z}/2$ case proved here (Theorems 3.13, 3.14, 3.17, 3.18) is a template that generalises mechanically to $\mathbb{Z}/n$.

**Idempotent rigidity of non-orientable group rings.** Conjecturally, $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ has no idempotents other than $0$ and $1$ for every finite abelian $G$ of even order, whereas $\mathbb{Q}[G]$ splits completely; hence $\mathbb{Z}[G]$ is not a product of rings of integers although the two become isomorphic after inverting $|G|$. The key insight is that the congruence $u\equiv v \pmod 2$ cutting $\mathbb{Z}_{\mathrm{tw}}$ out of $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral gluing datum that no idempotent can respect — exactly the argument of Theorems 3.20 and 3.21, which is short and purely congruence-theoretic and should apply to each pair of conjugate characters.

**Rank threshold for nowhere-vanishing twisted sections.** The scalar statement is settled here: every continuous section of the rank-one Möbius bundle vanishes in each window of length one (Theorem 4.7). The natural next step is a sharp rank threshold: for twisted $\mathbb{R}^d$-bundles over the circle with holonomy a given involution $\sigma\in O(d)$, characterise exactly when nowhere-vanishing sections exist — the answer should depend only on whether $\sigma$ fixes a vector, i.e. on the $(+1)$-eigenspace being nonzero, with the rank-one Möbius case ($\sigma=-1$, eigenspace $0$) as the extremal obstruction.

**Quantitative zero counting.** Theorem 4.7 gives at least one zero per unit window. For smooth sections one expects refined counts (a Sturm-type theory for antiperiodic boundary conditions), relating the number of zeros in $[0,n]$ to the spectrum of an associated operator with antiperiodic boundary conditions.

**Graded number theory.** More broadly, one can ask for the "arithmetic of a grading": given a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded ring with an integral gluing congruence, what is the right analogue of unique factorisation? Theorem 3.18 suggests a clean answer for $\mathbb{Z}[\mathbb{Z}/2]$ — irreducible rational integers are exactly $\pm2$ — and the general pattern deserves to be worked out.

## 9. Conclusion

The proposal to found arithmetic on the Möbius band contains one correct and genuinely illuminating idea — that $y(2x-1)$ is a well-defined quantity on the band, i.e. a section of the twisted line bundle — surrounded by four false claims: there are no induced coordinatewise ring operations; the proposed embedding of $\mathbb{Z}$ records only the sign of an integer; it identifies nothing and compactifies nothing; and its proposed zero divisor has a factor that is already zero.

The intuition behind the proposal, however, is exactly right, and it lands on the twist ring $\mathbb{Z}[t]/(t^2-1)$: a commutative ring with zero divisors whose unit group $\{\pm1,\pm t\}$ contains the orientation class, whose norm $a^2-b^2$ obeys a parity obstruction making $2$ the unique irreducible rational integer, in which $6 = 2(2+t)(2-t)$, $-6$ is the same product times a unit, and $0 = (1+t)(1-t)$, and which — despite $t^2=1$ — does not split as $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}$. On the analytic side, the same $\mathbb{Z}/2$ appears as the grading of periodic and antiperiodic functions, whose holonomy is computed by powers of $t$ and whose odd part contains no units because every continuous antiperiodic function vanishes in every unit window.

Orientation is not a prime. It is a unit of order two, and what it generates is a grading.

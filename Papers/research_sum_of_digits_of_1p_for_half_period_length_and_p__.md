# Digit Sums of Periodic Base-$b$ Expansions of $1/p$: The Full-Reptend and Half-Period Laws

## Abstract

Let $p$ be a prime, let $b \ge 2$ be an integer with $p \nmid b$, and consider the eventually periodic base-$b$ expansion of $1/p$. The length of its period equals the multiplicative order $\operatorname{ord}_p(b)$. We prove two exact digit-sum identities governing the two most symmetric regimes. First, when $b$ is a primitive root modulo $p$ — that is, $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = p-1$ — the digits of one period sum to $(b-1)(p-1)/2$. Second, when $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$ and $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the digits sum to $(b-1)(p-1)/4$. The proofs rest on a single structural observation: the digit sum is a linear image of the sum of the remainder orbit. All base-dependent complexity is thereby transferred to a purely group-theoretic quantity — the sum of the residues in the cyclic subgroup generated by $b$ — which is computed by a triangular-number identity in the full-period case and by a negation-symmetry pairing argument in the half-period case, the latter enabled precisely by $-1$ being a quadratic residue when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. We situate both identities as extremal instances of a unified order-indexed digit-sum law, connect them to Midy's theorem, and discuss the average-digit corollary and directions for generalization.

**Keywords:** digit sum, repeating decimal, multiplicative order, primitive root, full-reptend prime, quadratic residue, Midy's theorem, cyclic group.

**MSC 2020:** 11A63 (radix representation, digital problems), 11A07 (congruences; primitive roots), 11B50 (sequences and sets).

## 1. Introduction

The decimal $1/7 = 0.\overline{142857}$ has a striking property known to recreational number theorists for centuries: its six repeating digits sum to $27 = 9 \cdot 3$, its two halves $142$ and $857$ add to $999$, and cyclic shifts of the block reproduce the expansions of $2/7, 3/7, \dots$. Behind these coincidences lies the arithmetic of the multiplicative group modulo $7$, in which $10$ is a primitive root.

This paper isolates and proves the exact digit-sum laws underlying such phenomena, in an arbitrary base $b$, for the two most symmetric period lengths. Throughout, $p$ is prime, $b \ge 2$ is an integer with $p \nmid b$, and we study one period of the base-$b$ expansion of $1/p$.

Our contributions are:

1. **A remainder-to-digit reduction (Section 3).** Summing the elementary long-division identity over one period shows the digit sum $D$ and the remainder-orbit sum $S$ satisfy the single equation $p\,D = (b-1)\,S$. This transfers the entire problem to computing $S$.

2. **The full-period law (Section 4).** When $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = p-1$, the remainder orbit is all of $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$, so $S = p(p-1)/2$ and $D = (b-1)(p-1)/2$.

3. **The half-period law (Section 5).** When $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$ and $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the orbit is a negation-symmetric index-$2$ subgroup, so $S = p(p-1)/4$ and $D = (b-1)(p-1)/4$.

4. **Unification, corollaries, and generalizations (Sections 6–7).** A single order-indexed law, the relationship to Midy's theorem, the average-digit corollary, and conjectural extensions.

## 2. Setup and definitions

Fix a prime $p$ and an integer base $b \ge 2$ with $p \nmid b$. Base-$b$ long division of $1/p$ is governed by a remainder sequence and a digit sequence.

**Definition 2.1 (Remainders).** The $k$-th remainder is
$$r_k \;=\; b^k \bmod p, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$
In particular $r_0 = 1$ (assuming $p \ge 2$).

**Definition 2.2 (Digits).** The $(k+1)$-st base-$b$ digit of $1/p$ is
$$d_k \;=\; \left\lfloor \frac{b\,r_k}{p} \right\rfloor.$$

**Definition 2.3 (Period).** The period length is the multiplicative order $L = \operatorname{ord}_p(b)$, the least positive integer with $b^L \equiv 1 \pmod p$. Since $p$ is prime and $p \nmid b$, this order exists and divides $p-1$.

**Definition 2.4 (Period sums).** Over one period we define the **remainder sum** and **digit sum**
$$S \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} r_k, \qquad D \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} d_k.$$

The quantity $D$ is the object of interest: the sum of the digits in one repeating block of $1/p$ in base $b$.

**Remark 2.5.** The recurrence $r_{k+1} = (b\,r_k) \bmod p$ follows immediately from $b^{k+1} = b \cdot b^k$ and the multiplicativity of reduction modulo $p$. Thus the remainders form the forward orbit of $1$ under multiplication by $b$, which is exactly the cyclic subgroup $\langle b \rangle \le (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ once we run through a full period.

## 3. The remainder-to-digit reduction

The engine of both theorems is a single identity obtained by summing the division algorithm over one period.

**Lemma 3.1 (Division identity).** For every $k$,
$$b\,r_k \;=\; p\,d_k + r_{k+1}.$$

*Proof.* By definition $d_k = \lfloor b\,r_k / p \rfloor$ and $r_{k+1} = (b\,r_k) \bmod p$. The Euclidean division of $b\,r_k$ by $p$ reads $b\,r_k = p \lfloor b\,r_k/p \rfloor + (b\,r_k \bmod p)$, which is exactly the claim. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 3.2 (Period closure).** With $L = \operatorname{ord}_p(b)$ we have $r_L = 1 = r_0$.

*Proof.* $r_L = b^L \bmod p$, and $b^L \equiv 1 \pmod p$ by definition of the order; since $1 < p$, its residue is $1$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 3.3 (Shift invariance of the remainder sum).** The shifted remainder sum over one period equals $S$:
$$\sum_{k=0}^{L-1} r_{k+1} \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} r_k \;=\; S.$$

*Proof.* The left-hand sum is $\sum_{k=1}^{L} r_k = S - r_0 + r_L$. By Lemma 3.2, $r_L = r_0 = 1$, so the two boundary terms cancel and the sum equals $S$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Proposition 3.4 (Reduction).** The digit sum and remainder sum satisfy
$$p\,D \;=\; (b-1)\,S.$$

*Proof.* Sum the identity of Lemma 3.1 over $k = 0, \dots, L-1$:
$$b \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} r_k \;=\; p \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} d_k + \sum_{k=0}^{L-1} r_{k+1}.$$
The left side is $b\,S$, the first right-hand term is $p\,D$, and by Lemma 3.3 the last term is $S$. Hence $b\,S = p\,D + S$, i.e. $p\,D = (b-1)\,S$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Proposition 3.4 is decisive: **the digit sum is a rescaling of the remainder-orbit sum.** Every subsequent theorem is obtained by computing $S$ for a particular orbit and cancelling the factor $p$.

## 4. The full-period (primitive-root) law

**Theorem 4.1 (Full-Period Digit-Sum Law).** Let $p$ be prime, $b \ge 2$ with $p \nmid b$, and suppose $b$ is a primitive root modulo $p$, i.e. $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = p-1$. Then the digits of one period of the base-$b$ expansion of $1/p$ sum to
$$D \;=\; \frac{(b-1)(p-1)}{2}.$$

*Proof.* Because $b$ is a primitive root, the map $k \mapsto b^k \bmod p$ is a bijection from $\{0, 1, \dots, p-2\}$ onto the nonzero residues $\{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$. (Injectivity: two powers $b^i \equiv b^j$ with $0 \le i, j < p-1$ force $i = j$ since the order is $p-1$; surjectivity then follows by counting.) Therefore the remainder orbit is a permutation of $\{1, \dots, p-1\}$ and
$$S \;=\; \sum_{x=1}^{p-1} x \;=\; \frac{p(p-1)}{2}.$$
Substituting into Proposition 3.4 gives $p\,D = (b-1)\cdot \frac{p(p-1)}{2}$. Cancelling $p > 0$ yields the doubled identity $2D = (b-1)(p-1)$, hence $D = (b-1)(p-1)/2$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Example 4.2.** For $p = 7$, $b = 10$: $10$ is a primitive root mod $7$ (its powers modulo $7$ are $3,2,6,4,5,1$). The prediction is $\frac{9 \cdot 6}{2} = 27$, matching $1+4+2+8+5+7$ for $0.\overline{142857}$. Similarly $p = 17$ gives $72$ and $p = 19$ gives $81$.

**Remark 4.3 (Integrality).** Since $p$ is odd, $p - 1$ is even, so $(b-1)(p-1)/2$ is an integer for every $b$. The proof establishes the doubled identity $2D = (b-1)(p-1)$ directly, so integrality is automatic.

## 5. The half-period law

We now treat $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$. The new ingredient is a symmetry of the orbit that holds precisely when $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

**Lemma 5.1 (Negation symmetry of the index-$2$ subgroup).** Let $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ be prime and let $H \le (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ be the unique subgroup of index $2$ (the subgroup of nonzero quadratic residues, of size $(p-1)/2$). Then $H$ is closed under negation: $x \in H \iff -x \in H$.

*Proof.* $H$ is exactly the set of nonzero squares modulo $p$. When $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $-1$ is a quadratic residue (by Euler's criterion, $(-1)^{(p-1)/2} = 1$ since $(p-1)/2$ is even), so $-1 \in H$. As $H$ is a subgroup, $x \in H$ implies $(-1)x = -x \in H$; the converse is identical. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.2 (Identification of the orbit).** If $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$, then the remainder orbit $\{b^0, \dots, b^{(p-3)/2}\} \bmod p$ equals the unique index-$2$ subgroup $H$.

*Proof.* The orbit is the cyclic subgroup $\langle b \rangle$ generated by $b$ in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which has order equal to $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$. A cyclic group has at most one subgroup of each order, and $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ is cyclic of order $p-1$; hence $\langle b \rangle$ is *the* subgroup of order $(p-1)/2$, namely $H$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.3 (Remainder sum via pairing).** Under the hypotheses $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$, the remainder sum is
$$S \;=\; \frac{p(p-1)}{4}.$$

*Proof.* By Lemma 5.2 the orbit is $H$, and by Lemma 5.1 the involution $x \mapsto p - x$ maps $H$ to itself with no fixed point (a fixed point would need $2x \equiv 0$, impossible in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ for odd $p$). Thus $H$ partitions into $|H|/2 = (p-1)/4$ pairs $\{x, p-x\}$, each summing to $p$. Hence
$$S \;=\; \sum_{x \in H} x \;=\; p \cdot \frac{p-1}{4} \;=\; \frac{p(p-1)}{4}. \qquad\blacksquare$$

**Theorem 5.4 (Half-Period Digit-Sum Law).** Let $p$ be prime with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, let $b \ge 2$ with $p \nmid b$, and suppose $\operatorname{ord}_p(b) = (p-1)/2$. Then the digits of one period of the base-$b$ expansion of $1/p$ sum to
$$D \;=\; \frac{(b-1)(p-1)}{4}.$$

*Proof.* Combine Proposition 3.4 with Lemma 5.3: $p\,D = (b-1)S = (b-1) \cdot p(p-1)/4$. Cancelling $p$ gives $D = (b-1)(p-1)/4$. Integrality holds because $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ makes $4 \mid p-1$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Example 5.5.** For $p = 13$, $b = 10$: the powers of $10$ modulo $13$ are $10, 9, 12, 3, 4, 1$, so $\operatorname{ord}_{13}(10) = 6 = (13-1)/2$, and $13 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. The prediction is $\frac{9 \cdot 12}{4} = 27$, matching $0+7+6+9+2+3$ for $1/13 = 0.\overline{076923}$. For $p = 89$, $b = 10$ (order $88/2 = 44$, $89 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$) the prediction $\frac{9 \cdot 88}{4} = 198$ matches the digit sum of the forty-four-digit period of $1/89$; for $p = 157$ (order $156/2 = 78$, $157 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$) the prediction $\frac{9 \cdot 156}{4} = 351$ matches.

**Remark 5.6 (Necessity of $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$).** The congruence is exactly what makes $-1$ a quadratic residue and hence forces negation symmetry (Lemma 5.1). When $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, the index-$2$ subgroup is *not* closed under negation, the pairing $\{x, p-x\}$ leaves $H$, and the clean value $S = p(p-1)/4$ fails; the quarter formula does not hold in general. The parity of $(p-1)/2$ is the switch controlling the symmetry.

## 6. Unification and corollaries

### 6.1 A single order-indexed law

Proposition 3.4 makes no assumption on the order. It yields the master formula, valid for every prime $p$ and base $b$ with $p \nmid b$:

**Corollary 6.1 (Master digit-sum formula).** With $d = \operatorname{ord}_p(b)$ and $s = \sum_{x \in \langle b \rangle} x$ the sum of the elements of the cyclic subgroup generated by $b$,
$$D \;=\; \frac{(b-1)\,s}{p}.$$

Theorems 4.1 and 5.4 are the two instances in which the orbit is *negation-symmetric*, giving $s = p\,d/2$ and collapsing the formula to $D = (b-1)d/2$:
- full period: $d = p-1$, so $D = (b-1)(p-1)/2$;
- half period ($p \equiv 1 \bmod 4$): $d = (p-1)/2$, so $D = (b-1)(p-1)/4$.

Negation symmetry is equivalent to $-1 \in \langle b \rangle$, a checkable divisibility condition on $\operatorname{ord}_p(b)$ and the residue of $p$.

### 6.2 Relation to Midy's theorem

**Corollary 6.2 (Midy complement).** In the full-period case with $L = p-1$ even, write the period as two halves of length $L/2$. The $j$-th digit of the second half is the base-$b$ complement $(b-1) - d_j$ of the $j$-th digit of the first half; consequently the two halves, read as integers, sum to $b^{L/2} - 1$.

*Proof sketch.* Since $b$ is a primitive root and $-1 = b^{L/2}$ has order $2$, the remainder $r_{k + L/2} = b^{L/2} r_k \equiv -r_k \equiv p - r_k \pmod p$. Feeding $r$ and $p - r$ into the digit map $d = \lfloor b\,r/p\rfloor$ gives digits summing to $b - 1$ (the two floors $\lfloor b r/p\rfloor$ and $\lfloor b(p-r)/p\rfloor$ add to $b-1$ whenever $p \nmid br$). Column-wise complementation makes the halves sum to $\underbrace{(b-1)\cdots(b-1)}_{L/2} = b^{L/2}-1$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

This exhibits the digit-sum laws and Midy's theorem as two expressions of the same negation symmetry.

### 6.3 Average digit

**Corollary 6.3 (Central mean).** In both symmetric regimes the average digit over one period equals $(b-1)/2$.

*Proof.* Full period: $D/(p-1) = \frac{(b-1)(p-1)/2}{p-1} = (b-1)/2$. Half period: $D/\big((p-1)/2\big) = \frac{(b-1)(p-1)/4}{(p-1)/2} = (b-1)/2$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

In base $10$ the mean digit is exactly $4.5$, the midpoint of $\{0, \dots, 9\}$ — a direct consequence of each digit's complement also appearing in the period.

## 7. Algorithms

We record the two elementary algorithms used for verification and exploration.

**Algorithm A (Period and digit sum by long division).** Given $p, b$, produce one period of digits of $1/p$ and their sum by iterating $r \mapsto (b r) \bmod p$ and emitting $\lfloor b r / p\rfloor$, halting when $r$ returns to $1$. Runs in $O(L)$ multiplications where $L = \operatorname{ord}_p(b) \le p-1$.

**Algorithm B (Orbit-sum verification).** Compute $\operatorname{ord}_p(b)$ and the subgroup sum $s = \sum_{x \in \langle b\rangle} x$, then output $(b-1)s/p$ and compare against Algorithm A. This validates Corollary 6.1 and, when the order matches $p-1$ or $(p-1)/2$ with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, Theorems 4.1 and 5.4.

Both are linear in $L$ and require only exact integer arithmetic.

## 8. Discussion and future work

The reduction $p\,D = (b-1)\,S$ shows that the arithmetic of the digits of $1/p$ is entirely controlled by a single group-theoretic invariant — the sum over the remainder orbit. Consequently, questions about digit sums become questions about orbit sums in $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$, where the tools of quadratic residues and cyclic-group structure apply cleanly.

Three directions extend the present results.

**A single order-indexed digit-sum law.** For any prime $p$, base $b \ge 2$ with $p \nmid b$, and period $d = \operatorname{ord}_p(b)$, the digit sum equals $(b-1)s/p$, where $s$ is the sum of the elements of the cyclic subgroup generated by $b$. When that subgroup contains $-1$ (equivalently, is closed under negation), $s = p\,d/2$ and the digit sum collapses to $(b-1)d/2$. The half-period and full-reptend results are the two extreme instances; the negation-closure criterion $-1 \in \langle b\rangle$ is a clean, checkable condition, making the general statement testable across all intermediate orders.

**Midy blocks of arbitrary equal length.** Whenever $-1 = b^{d/2}$ occurs within one period ($d$ even, half-power equal to $-1$), splitting the period into two halves yields blocks whose digitwise sum is constant $b-1$ in every position, so the two half-values sum to $b^{d/2}-1$. More generally, if $b^{d/m}$ runs through $m$-th roots of unity, the $m$ equal sub-blocks have completely determined column sums, upgrading the classical two-block Midy phenomenon to an $m$-block phenomenon governed by the order of $b$ relative to $d$.

**Average digit value stabilizes at $(b-1)/2$.** In the two symmetric regimes the average digit is exactly $(b-1)/2$. We conjecture the average digit of one period of $1/p$ in base $b$ tends to $(b-1)/2$ for every base as $p \to \infty$, with a bias term controlled by how far the orbit of $b$ is from negation symmetry. Negation symmetry of the remainder orbit is precisely the mechanism that makes each digit's complement appear, pinning the mean at the central value.

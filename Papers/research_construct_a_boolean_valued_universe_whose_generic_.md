## Abstract

The modal logic of forcing studies the operators $\Box p$ ("$p$ holds in every forcing extension") and $\Diamond p$ ("$p$ holds in some forcing extension") over the network of universes generated by forcing. Abstract accounts of this network postulate a frame that is reflexive, transitive and directed, together with two classes of distinguished assertions — *buttons*, which once true remain true, and *switches*, which can be turned on and off indefinitely — and derive from these postulates that the logic contains $\mathsf{S4.2}$. This paper removes the postulates. We construct a Boolean-valued propositional universe whose generic quotients *are* the worlds of a concrete button–switch frame, and we derive, rather than assume, forcing closure, directedness, the button law, and the existence of two opposite Continuum-Hypothesis branches.

The development has four layers. **Layer A** develops Boolean-valued semantics over an arbitrary Boolean algebra: Boolean values of propositional assertions, a *forcing closure* theorem stating that every theorem of classical propositional logic receives value $\top$, generic filters, the *truth lemma* identifying truth in the generic quotient with membership of the Boolean value in the filter, and a *branching theorem* producing two opposite generic quotients from a single undecided Boolean value. **Layer B** introduces the control frame of $n$ irreversible buttons and $m$ free switches, proves reflexivity, transitivity and directedness, and refutes the axioms $5$, $\mathrm{B}$ and $.3$ on it. **Layer C** proves the *realization theorem*: with the powerset of the space of switch settings as Boolean algebra and principal filters as generic objects, truth at the control world $(S,g)$ coincides with $g \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_S$. Buttons and CH-branching then follow from Boolean-valued monotonicity and from the branching theorem. **Layer D** adds the converse *ground* modality, proving the mixed validity $p \to \Box \check{\Diamond} p$ on the same frame on which the unimodal Brouwer axiom fails.

We complement the realization with an exact calibration of the frame's modal strength ($.2$ is equivalent to directedness; the frame is Euclidean iff it has no buttons; two independent buttons refute linearity), a sound Hilbert calculus for $\mathsf{S4.2}$ yielding independence of $5$ and $.3$, an exact characterization of the multiverse-invariant fragment as the button-free fragment, a necessary-and-sufficient Boolean criterion for a background assertion to survive into both branches, a bounded-morphism transfer theory showing that switches are semantically free and that the logic of the control frame sits strictly inside the logic of chains, and the exact count $3^n \cdot 4^m$ of accessibility pairs.

**Keywords:** forcing, set-theoretic multiverse, Boolean-valued models, generic filter, modal logic of forcing, $\mathsf{S4.2}$, buttons and switches, Continuum Hypothesis, bounded morphism, ground model.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The multiverse and its modal logic

Forcing produces, from a universe $V$ of sets and a partial order of conditions, an extension $V[G]$ containing a new *generic* object $G$. Iterating the construction generates a vast network of universes. The **set-theoretic multiverse** is this network, viewed as a structure in its own right, and its natural language is modal: for a sentence $p$ of set theory, write

- $\Box p$ for "$p$ holds in every forcing extension of the current universe";
- $\Diamond p$ for "$p$ holds in some forcing extension of the current universe".

The **modal logic of forcing** is the set of modal schemes valid under this interpretation. Three structural properties of forcing give lower bounds immediately:

1. Trivial forcing gives $\Box p \to p$ (axiom $T$; reflexivity).
2. An iteration of forcings is a forcing, giving $\Box p \to \Box \Box p$ (axiom $4$; transitivity).
3. Two forcing extensions of a universe embed into a common extension, obtained by forcing with the product, giving $\Diamond \Box p \to \Box \Diamond p$ (axiom $.2$; directedness).

Together with the normal base logic $K$ this is the system $\mathsf{S4.2}$. Upper bounds require *control*: one must exhibit set-theoretic assertions whose modal behaviour is rich enough to refute everything outside the target logic. The standard vocabulary is:

- a **button** is an assertion $p$ with $p \to \Box p$ valid at every world — once true, permanently true;
- a **switch** is an assertion $p$ with $\Box(\Diamond p \wedge \Diamond \neg p)$ valid — its truth value can always be toggled either way.

The Continuum Hypothesis is the archetypal switch. Assertions expressing that some previously definable object has become non-definable, or that some cardinal has been collapsed, are archetypal buttons: forcing adds objects and never removes them, so these cannot be undone.

### 1.2 The gap this paper closes

An abstract treatment can *postulate* a reflexive, transitive, directed frame carrying buttons and switches, and can derive the modal consequences. But a postulate is not a construction, and the resulting theory is vulnerable to the objection that no single semantics need validate all of the postulated laws at once. The obligation is concrete and falsifiable:

> **Realization problem.** Exhibit one satisfaction relation, arising from an honest Boolean-valued construction, for which every frame law (reflexivity, transitivity, directedness), the button law, the switch law, and both Continuum-Hypothesis branch conditions are *theorems*.

This paper discharges that obligation at the propositional level, and then uses the resulting concrete object to settle several adjacent questions exactly. Throughout, "assertion" means a formula of the propositional language described in §2.1; the restriction to propositional syntax is what makes every claim below provable outright, with no set-theoretic hypotheses whatsoever.

### 1.3 Results

- **Forcing closure** (Theorem 2.4): every theorem of classical propositional logic has Boolean value $\top$ in every Boolean-valued universe.
- **Truth lemma** (Theorem 2.8): $p$ holds in the generic quotient by $U$ iff $\llbracket p \rrbracket \in U$.
- **Branching theorem** (Theorem 2.12): an assertion with Boolean value strictly between $\bot$ and $\top$ is true in one generic quotient and false in another.
- **Frame laws** (Proposition 3.2): the control frame is reflexive, transitive and directed, hence sound for $\mathsf{S4.2}$; the axioms $5$ and $\mathrm{B}$ fail on it (Proposition 3.6).
- **Realization theorem** (Theorem 4.3): control worlds are generic quotients; truth at $(S,g)$ is membership of $g$ in the stage-$S$ Boolean value.
- **Derived buttons** (Theorem 4.6) and **derived CH branches** (Theorem 4.9), from Boolean-valued monotonicity and from the branching theorem.
- **Exact calibration** (Theorems 5.1–5.3): $.2$ holding as a schema forces directedness; the frame is Euclidean iff it has no buttons; two independent buttons refute $.3$.
- **Soundness and independence** (Theorems 6.2–6.4): a Hilbert calculus for $\mathsf{S4.2}$ is sound on all directed preorders; $5$ and $.3$ are not derivable in it.
- **Invariant fragment theorem** (Theorem 7.3): invariance along the button order characterizes exactly the button-free fragment.
- **Branch preservation criterion** (Theorem 8.1): a background $b$ survives into a $p$-branch and a $\neg p$-branch iff $b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket \ne \bot$ and $b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket^{\complement} \ne \bot$; button-definable backgrounds always survive, switch-dependent ones need not.
- **Bimodal separation** (Theorem 9.4): $p \to \Box \check{\Diamond} p$ is valid while $p \to \Box \Diamond p$ fails, on one frame.
- **Transfer and counting** (Theorems 10.3, 10.6, 11.2): switches are semantically free; the logic of the control frame is strictly inside the logic of chains; the $n$-button, $m$-switch frame has $2^{n+m}$ worlds and exactly $3^n 4^m$ accessibility pairs.

## 2. Layer A: Boolean-valued semantics and generic quotients

### 2.1 Syntax

Fix a set $\mathcal{A}$ of **atoms**. The language $\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A})$ of propositional set-theoretic assertions is generated by

$$p ::= a \;\mid\; \bot \;\mid\; p \to q \qquad (a \in \mathcal{A}).$$

The signature $\{\bot, \to\}$ is functionally complete for classical logic, so nothing is lost. We abbreviate

$$\neg p := (p \to \bot), \qquad \top := \neg \bot, \qquad p \vee q := (\neg p \to q), \qquad p \wedge q := \neg(p \to \neg q).$$

### 2.2 Boolean values

Let $B$ be a Boolean algebra with operations $\wedge, \vee, {}^{\complement}, \bot, \top$ and Heyting implication $a \Rightarrow b := a^{\complement} \vee b$; recall $a \wedge (a \Rightarrow b) \le b$ and $c \le a \Rightarrow b$ iff $c \wedge a \le b$.

**Definition 2.1 (Boolean value).** Given an **assignment** $v : \mathcal{A} \to B$, the Boolean value $\llbracket \cdot \rrbracket_v : \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{A}) \to B$ is defined recursively by
$$\llbracket a \rrbracket_v = v(a), \qquad \llbracket \bot \rrbracket_v = \bot, \qquad \llbracket p \to q \rrbracket_v = \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \Rightarrow \llbracket q \rrbracket_v .$$

**Lemma 2.2 (Defined connectives).** $\llbracket \neg p \rrbracket_v = \llbracket p \rrbracket_v^{\complement}$, $\llbracket \top \rrbracket_v = \top$, $\llbracket p \vee q \rrbracket_v = \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \vee \llbracket q \rrbracket_v$ and $\llbracket p \wedge q \rrbracket_v = \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \wedge \llbracket q \rrbracket_v$.

*Proof.* Immediate from $a \Rightarrow \bot = a^{\complement}$, $a \Rightarrow b = a^{\complement} \vee b$, De Morgan, and $\bot^{\complement} = \top$. $\square$

So the Boolean value operation is a homomorphism from the Lindenbaum algebra of the language into $B$, once the atoms are assigned.

### 2.3 Forcing closure

Let $\vdash$ denote derivability in the Hilbert calculus with the three axiom schemes

$$\text{(A1)}\; p \to (q \to p), \qquad \text{(A2)}\; \bigl(p \to (q \to r)\bigr) \to \bigl((p \to q) \to (p \to r)\bigr), \qquad \text{(A3)}\; \neg \neg p \to p,$$

closed under modus ponens. This calculus is complete for classical propositional logic.

**Lemma 2.3 (Boolean forms of the axioms).** In any Boolean algebra and for all $a,b,c$:
$$a \Rightarrow (b \Rightarrow a) = \top, \qquad \bigl(a \Rightarrow (b \Rightarrow c)\bigr) \Rightarrow \bigl((a \Rightarrow b) \Rightarrow (a \Rightarrow c)\bigr) = \top, \qquad a^{\complement\complement} \Rightarrow a = \top .$$

*Proof.* Each is an application of the residuation law $c \le a \Rightarrow b \iff c \wedge a \le b$, used repeatedly to strip the implications, followed by a lattice inequality. For the first, the goal becomes $\top \wedge a \wedge b \le a$. For the second, the goal becomes
$$\top \wedge (a \Rightarrow (b \Rightarrow c)) \wedge (a \Rightarrow b) \wedge a \le c,$$
and the left-hand side is below $\bigl((a \Rightarrow (b\Rightarrow c)) \wedge a\bigr) \wedge \bigl((a \Rightarrow b) \wedge a\bigr) \le (b \Rightarrow c) \wedge b \le c$. The third is $a^{\complement\complement} = a$ together with $a \Rightarrow a = \top$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.4 (Forcing closure).** *If $\vdash p$ then $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v = \top$, for every Boolean algebra $B$ and every assignment $v$.*

*Proof.* Induction on the derivation. The axiom cases are Lemma 2.3 (with Lemma 2.2 for the double negation in A3). For modus ponens, suppose $\llbracket p \to q \rrbracket_v = \top$ and $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v = \top$. Then
$$\top = \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \wedge (\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \Rightarrow \llbracket q \rrbracket_v) \le \llbracket q \rrbracket_v,$$
so $\llbracket q \rrbracket_v = \top$. $\square$

Theorem 2.4 is the propositional core of the statement that the forcing relation is closed under logical consequence: no Boolean-valued universe can disagree with logic.

### 2.4 Generic filters and the truth lemma

**Definition 2.5 (Generic filter).** A **generic filter** on $B$ is a set $U \subseteq B$ such that

1. $\top \in U$ (nontriviality);
2. $\bot \notin U$ (properness);
3. $a \in U$ and $a \le b$ imply $b \in U$ (upward closure);
4. $a, b \in U$ imply $a \wedge b \in U$ (closure under meets);
5. for every $a \in B$, $a \in U$ or $a^{\complement} \in U$ (genericity/decisiveness).

Equivalently, $U$ is an ultrafilter on $B$. Condition (5) is what makes the quotient two-valued.

**Lemma 2.6.** For a generic filter $U$: (i) $a^{\complement} \in U \iff a \notin U$; (ii) $(a \Rightarrow b) \in U \iff (a \in U \Rightarrow b \in U)$.

*Proof.* (i) If $a^{\complement}$ and $a$ were both in $U$ then $\bot = a \wedge a^{\complement} \in U$, contradicting properness; conversely use (5). (ii) If $a \Rightarrow b \in U$ and $a \in U$ then $a \wedge (a \Rightarrow b) \in U$ and this is $\le b$, so $b \in U$. Conversely, if $a \in U$ implies $b \in U$: when $a \in U$, we get $b \in U$ and $b \le a \Rightarrow b$; when $a \notin U$ we have $a^{\complement} \in U$ by (i) and $a^{\complement} \le a \Rightarrow b$. $\square$

**Definition 2.7 (Two-valued satisfaction and generic quotient).** For $w : \mathcal{A} \to \{\text{true},\text{false}\}$ define satisfaction $w \models p$ by the usual clauses. The **generic quotient** of the Boolean-valued universe $(B,v)$ by a generic filter $U$ is the two-valued world
$$\mathrm{quot}(v,U)(a) := \bigl( v(a) \in U \bigr).$$

**Theorem 2.8 (Truth lemma).** *For every assertion $p$,*
$$\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p \iff \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \in U .$$

*Proof.* Induction on $p$. Atoms hold by definition of the quotient. For $\bot$ both sides are false, the right by properness. For $p \to q$, satisfaction is $(\mathrm{quot} \models p) \Rightarrow (\mathrm{quot} \models q)$, which by induction is $(\llbracket p \rrbracket \in U) \Rightarrow (\llbracket q \rrbracket \in U)$, which by Lemma 2.6(ii) is $\llbracket p \rrbracket \Rightarrow \llbracket q \rrbracket \in U$, i.e. $\llbracket p \to q\rrbracket \in U$. $\square$

**Corollary 2.9.** If $\vdash p$ then $\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p$ for every $v$ and every generic filter $U$: logic survives every collapse.

### 2.5 Forcing

**Definition 2.10 (Forcing).** A **condition** $b \in B$ **forces** $p$, written $b \Vdash_v p$, when $b \le \llbracket p \rrbracket_v$.

**Proposition 2.11.** (i) *Monotonicity:* if $b \Vdash_v p$ and $c \le b$ then $c \Vdash_v p$. (ii) *Modus ponens:* if $b \Vdash_v p \to q$ and $b \Vdash_v p$ then $b \Vdash_v q$. (iii) *Generic truth:* if $b \in U$ and $b \Vdash_v p$ then $\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p$.

*Proof.* (i) Transitivity of $\le$. (ii) $b \le \llbracket p \rrbracket \wedge (\llbracket p \rrbracket \Rightarrow \llbracket q \rrbracket) \le \llbracket q \rrbracket$. (iii) $U$ is upward closed, so $\llbracket p \rrbracket \in U$; apply Theorem 2.8. $\square$

### 2.6 Branching

**Definition.** $B$ is **rich** if every $b \ne \bot$ belongs to some generic filter. (This is the Boolean prime ideal principle for $B$.)

**Theorem 2.12 (Branching theorem).** *Let $B$ be rich and suppose $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \notin \{\bot, \top\}$. Then there exist generic filters $U, V$ with*
$$\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p \quad\text{and}\quad \mathrm{quot}(v,V) \not\models p .$$

*Proof.* Since $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \ne \bot$, richness gives $U \ni \llbracket p \rrbracket_v$; by Theorem 2.8, $\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p$. Since $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \ne \top$ we have $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v^{\complement} \ne \bot$, so richness gives $V \ni \llbracket p \rrbracket_v^{\complement}$; by Lemma 2.6(i) $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \notin V$, so by Theorem 2.8 $\mathrm{quot}(v,V) \not\models p$. $\square$

Two branches of the multiverse arise from *one* Boolean-valued universe and *one* undecided value. Independence phenomena are, at this level, exactly the statement that a Boolean algebra has elements other than $0$ and $1$.

**Proposition 2.13 (Powerset algebras are rich, choice-freely).** *For any set $\Omega$, the powerset algebra $\mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ is rich: the principal filter $U_x = \{ s \subseteq \Omega : x \in s \}$ at a point $x$ is generic, and any nonempty $b$ contains a point.*

*Proof.* $U_x$ contains $\Omega$, omits $\varnothing$, is upward closed, closed under intersections, and decides every $s$ since either $x \in s$ or $x \in \Omega \setminus s$. $\square$

Working with powerset algebras therefore avoids any appeal to the axiom of choice for the branching theorem — a point worth making, since the two branches are the substance of the realization.

## 3. Layer B: the control frame of buttons and switches

Fix a type $\mathrm{Btn}$ of buttons and a type $\mathrm{Sw}$ of switches.

**Definition 3.1 (Control frame).** A **control world** is a pair $w = (S, g)$ where $S$ is a finite subset of $\mathrm{Btn}$ (the buttons pushed so far) and $g : \mathrm{Sw} \to \{0,1\}$ is a setting of all switches. The **forcing accessibility** relation is
$$(S,g) \sqsubseteq (T,h) \quad :\iff \quad S \subseteq T .$$
Extensions may push further buttons and reset switches arbitrarily; they may never unpush a button. As usual, for a predicate $P$ on worlds,
$$(\Box P)(w) :\iff \forall v\, (w \sqsubseteq v \to P(v)), \qquad (\Diamond P)(w) :\iff \exists v\, (w \sqsubseteq v \wedge P(v)).$$

**Proposition 3.2 (Frame laws).** $\sqsubseteq$ *is reflexive and transitive, and it is* **directed**: *for all $v_1, v_2$ there is $u$ with $v_1 \sqsubseteq u$ and $v_2 \sqsubseteq u$.*

*Proof.* Reflexivity and transitivity are those of $\subseteq$. For directedness take $u = (S_1 \cup S_2, g_1)$. $\square$

Directedness here is unconditional — any two worlds have an upper bound, not merely any two extensions of a common world — which is the frame-theoretic residue of the fact that forcing extensions amalgamate through products.

**Proposition 3.3 ($\mathsf{S4.2}$ soundness on the frame).** *For all predicates $P, Q$ and all worlds $w$:*
$$\Box P(w) \to P(w), \qquad \Box P(w) \to \Box\Box P(w), \qquad \Box(P \to Q)(w) \to (\Box P(w) \to \Box Q(w)), \qquad \Diamond \Box P(w) \to \Box \Diamond P(w).$$

*Proof.* The first three are reflexivity, transitivity and pointwise reasoning. For $.2$: assume $\Diamond \Box P(w)$, witnessed by $v$ with $w \sqsubseteq v$ and $\Box P(v)$, and let $u$ satisfy $w \sqsubseteq u$. By directedness choose $t$ above both $v$ and $u$. Then $P(t)$ holds since $\Box P(v)$, and $u \sqsubseteq t$, so $\Diamond P(u)$. $\square$

**Definition 3.4 (Buttons and switches).** A predicate $P$ is a **button** for a relation $R$ if $R\,w\,v$ and $P(w)$ imply $P(v)$. It is a **switch** if at every world both $\Diamond P$ and $\Diamond \neg P$ hold.

**Proposition 3.5.** *For each $b \in \mathrm{Btn}$ the predicate $\mathrm{pushed}_b(S,g) :\iff b \in S$ is a button. For each $s \in \mathrm{Sw}$ the predicate $\mathrm{on}_s(S,g) :\iff g(s) = 1$ is a switch, and persistently so: $\Box(\Diamond\, \mathrm{on}_s \wedge \Diamond \neg \mathrm{on}_s)$ holds at every world.*

*Proof.* Buttons: $b \in S \subseteq T$. Switches: from $(S,g)$ pass to $(S, g[s := 1])$ and $(S, g[s := 0])$, both accessible since $S \subseteq S$. Persistence holds because the argument applies at every world. $\square$

**Proposition 3.6 (Failure of $5$ and of Brouwer).** *If $\mathrm{Btn} \ne \varnothing$, pick $b$ and let $P(v) :\iff b \notin v_1$. At $w = (\varnothing, g)$ we have $P(w)$, hence $\Diamond P(w)$; but $\Box \Diamond P(w)$ fails, because $(\{b\}, g)$ is accessible from $w$ and no world above it satisfies $P$. Hence both $\Diamond P \to \Box \Diamond P$ (axiom $5$) and $P \to \Box \Diamond P$ (axiom $\mathrm{B}$) fail.*

Thus the frame is an $\mathsf{S4.2}$ frame and not an $\mathsf{S5}$ frame, and buttons are the reason.

## 4. Layer C: the realization

We now show the control frame is *not an analogy* for a Boolean-valued construction; it *is* one.

**Definition 4.1 (Generic objects, stage assignments).** Let $\mathcal{G} := \{0,1\}^{\mathrm{Sw}}$ be the space of **generic objects** (switch settings), and let $B := \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})$, a Boolean algebra under union, intersection and complement. Atoms are of two kinds: button atoms $\mathrm{btn}(b)$ and switch atoms $\mathrm{sw}(s)$. The **stage-$S$ assignment** is
$$v_S(\mathrm{btn}(b)) := \begin{cases} \mathcal{G} & b \in S \\ \varnothing & b \notin S \end{cases} \qquad v_S(\mathrm{sw}(s)) := \{\, g \in \mathcal{G} : g(s) = 1 \,\}.$$
Write $\llbracket p \rrbracket_S := \llbracket p \rrbracket_{v_S}$.

Button atoms receive only the extreme values $\bot, \top$: at a given stage a button is decided outright. Switch atoms receive genuinely intermediate values, and this is precisely the source of branching.

**Definition 4.2 (Two-valued truth at a control world).** $(S,g) \models p$ is the two-valued satisfaction relation for the atomic diagram $\mathrm{btn}(b) \mapsto (b \in S)$, $\mathrm{sw}(s) \mapsto (g(s) = 1)$.

**Theorem 4.3 (Realization theorem).** *For every finite $S \subseteq \mathrm{Btn}$, every $g \in \mathcal{G}$ and every assertion $p$,*
$$(S,g) \models p \quad\iff\quad g \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_S .$$

*Proof.* Let $U_g$ be the principal generic filter at $g$ on $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})$ (Proposition 2.13). One checks on atoms that $\mathrm{quot}(v_S, U_g)$ is precisely the atomic diagram of $(S,g)$: for a button atom, $g \in v_S(\mathrm{btn}(b))$ iff $b \in S$; for a switch atom, $g \in v_S(\mathrm{sw}(s))$ iff $g(s)=1$. Since satisfaction depends only on the atomic diagram, $(S,g) \models p$ iff $\mathrm{quot}(v_S,U_g) \models p$, which by the truth lemma (Theorem 2.8) is $\llbracket p \rrbracket_S \in U_g$, i.e. $g \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_S$. $\square$

Every world of the abstract frame is therefore a generic quotient of a Boolean-valued universe, and the frame's satisfaction relation is the one induced by Boolean values. The remaining frame laws now become theorems about the algebra.

### 4.1 Buttons, derived

**Definition 4.4 (Positive button fragment).** $\mathrm{Pos}$ is the least class of assertions containing $\top$ and every button atom and closed under $\wedge$ and $\vee$.

**Lemma 4.5.** *For $p \in \mathrm{Pos}$: (i) $\llbracket p \rrbracket_S$ is either $\varnothing$ or $\mathcal{G}$ — positive button formulas do not distinguish generic objects; (ii) if $S \subseteq T$ then $\llbracket p \rrbracket_S \subseteq \llbracket p \rrbracket_T$.*

*Proof.* Both by induction on the generation of $\mathrm{Pos}$. For (ii): the atomic case is $b \in S \Rightarrow b \in T$; $\wedge$ and $\vee$ are monotone operations on the algebra. $\square$

**Theorem 4.6 (Buttons are derived, not assumed).** *Every $p \in \mathrm{Pos}$ defines a button of the control frame: if $(S,g) \models p$ and $S \subseteq T$ then $(T,h) \models p$ for every $h$.*

*Proof.* By Theorem 4.3, $g \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_S$. By Lemma 4.5(i) also $h \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_S$; by Lemma 4.5(ii) $h \in \llbracket p \rrbracket_T$; by Theorem 4.3 again, $(T,h) \models p$. $\square$

The button law is thus a *preservation theorem for Boolean values along the stage order*, transported through the realization theorem. On the algebra side directedness also has a counterpart: for a button atom, $v_{S \cup T}(\mathrm{btn}(b)) = v_S(\mathrm{btn}(b)) \cup v_T(\mathrm{btn}(b))$ — amalgamating stages joins the Boolean values.

### 4.2 The Continuum-Hypothesis branches, derived

Designate a switch $s_{\mathrm{CH}}$ and let $\mathrm{CH}$ abbreviate the atom $\mathrm{sw}(s_{\mathrm{CH}})$.

**Lemma 4.7.** $\llbracket \mathrm{CH} \rrbracket_S \ne \varnothing$ *and* $\llbracket \mathrm{CH} \rrbracket_S \ne \mathcal{G}$, *for every stage $S$.*

*Proof.* The all-ones generic object lies in $\llbracket \mathrm{CH} \rrbracket_S$; the all-zeros one does not. $\square$

**Theorem 4.8 (Derived opposite CH branches).** *There exist generic filters $U, V$ on $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})$ with $\mathrm{quot}(v_S, U) \models \mathrm{CH}$ and $\mathrm{quot}(v_S,V) \not\models \mathrm{CH}$.*

*Proof.* Lemma 4.7 with Proposition 2.13 and Theorem 2.12. $\square$

**Theorem 4.9 (CH is a switch in the frame).** *At every world $(S,g)$, both $\Diamond \mathrm{CH}$ and $\Diamond \neg\mathrm{CH}$ hold, witnessed by $(S, \mathbf{1})$ and $(S, \mathbf{0})$.*

Nothing about the Continuum Hypothesis was assumed at any point. The two branches are consequences of one designated element of a Boolean algebra being neither $0$ nor $1$.

**Theorem 4.10 (Packaged realization).** *There is a single satisfaction relation — the Boolean-valued one of Theorem 4.3 — for which all of the following hold simultaneously: $\sqsubseteq$ is reflexive; $\sqsubseteq$ is transitive; $\sqsubseteq$ is directed; every button atom defines a button; the designated CH atom defines a switch; and truth at $(S,g)$ equals membership of $g$ in the stage-$S$ Boolean value.*

This is exactly the falsifiable obligation stated in §1.2.

## 5. Exact calibration of the frame

Soundness tells us $\mathsf{S4.2}$ holds. Calibration tells us the frame conditions are not accidental.

**Theorem 5.1 ($.2$ is exactly directedness).** *Let $R$ be any relation on $W$ such that for every predicate $P$ and world $w$, $\Diamond_R \Box_R P(w) \to \Box_R \Diamond_R P(w)$. Then $R$ is directed: whenever $R\,x\,y$ and $R\,x\,z$, there is $u$ with $R\,y\,u$ and $R\,z\,u$.*

*Proof.* Apply the schema at $x$ to the predicate $P(t) :\iff R\,y\,t$. Then $\Diamond \Box P(x)$ holds, witnessed by $y$ itself, since $\Box P(y)$ says every successor $t$ of $y$ satisfies $R\,y\,t$. So $\Box \Diamond P(x)$ holds; instantiating at $z$ gives $u$ with $R\,z\,u$ and $R\,y\,u$. $\square$

Combined with Proposition 3.3, the axiom $.2$ *is* directedness, so nothing weaker than a directed frame can validate it. The use of an arbitrary predicate is essential and legitimate: the modal schema quantifies over all valuations.

**Theorem 5.2 (One button refutes $\mathsf{S5}$).** *The control frame is Euclidean — i.e. $R\,x\,y$ and $R\,x\,z$ imply $R\,y\,z$, the frame condition for axiom $5$ — if and only if $\mathrm{Btn} = \varnothing$.*

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $b$ is a button. From $x = (\varnothing, \mathbf{0})$ both $y = (\{b\}, \mathbf{0})$ and $z = (\varnothing,\mathbf{0})$ are accessible, so Euclideanness gives $\{b\} \subseteq \varnothing$, absurd. ($\Leftarrow$) With no buttons every stage is $\varnothing$ and all worlds are mutually accessible. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Two independent buttons refute linearity).** *Let $b_1 \ne b_2$. Then at $(\varnothing, g)$ the linearity schema*
$$\Box(\Box\,\mathrm{pushed}_{b_1} \to \mathrm{pushed}_{b_2}) \;\vee\; \Box(\Box\,\mathrm{pushed}_{b_2} \to \mathrm{pushed}_{b_1})$$
*fails.*

*Proof.* For the left disjunct, go to $(\{b_1\}, g)$: there $\Box\,\mathrm{pushed}_{b_1}$ holds while $\mathrm{pushed}_{b_2}$ fails, since $b_2 \notin \{b_1\}$. The right disjunct fails symmetrically at $(\{b_2\}, g)$. $\square$

So the logic of the control frames sits strictly between $\mathsf{S4.2}$ and each of $\mathsf{S5}$ and $\mathsf{S4.3}$: it is a genuinely directed, genuinely branching logic.

## 6. The deductive side: soundness and independence

**Definition 6.1.** Modal formulas are generated by $p ::= a \mid \bot \mid p \to q \mid \Box p$, with $\Diamond p := \neg\Box\neg p$. Kripke satisfaction $\mathfrak{M}, w \models p$ over a frame $(W,R)$ and valuation $V$ is standard, with $w \models \Box p$ iff $v \models p$ for all $v$ with $R\,w\,v$. The calculus $\mathsf{S4.2}$ consists of (A1)–(A3), the schemes
$$K:\; \Box(p \to q) \to (\Box p \to \Box q), \qquad T:\; \Box p \to p, \qquad 4:\; \Box p \to \Box\Box p, \qquad .2:\; \Diamond \Box p \to \Box \Diamond p,$$
closed under modus ponens and necessitation. A **directed preorder** is a frame that is reflexive, transitive and directed.

**Theorem 6.2 (Soundness).** *Every theorem of $\mathsf{S4.2}$ is true at every world of every directed preorder under every valuation.*

*Proof.* Induction on derivations. (A1)–(A3) are classical tautologies at each world; $K$ is pointwise; $T$ uses reflexivity; $4$ uses transitivity; $.2$ uses directedness exactly as in Proposition 3.3. Modus ponens and necessitation preserve validity. $\square$

**Theorem 6.3 (Independence of $5$).** $\Diamond p \to \Box \Diamond p$ *is not derivable in $\mathsf{S4.2}$.*

*Proof.* The two-button, one-switch control frame is a directed preorder (Proposition 3.2). Interpret the atom as "the first button is unpushed". By Proposition 3.6 the formula fails at the base world. By Theorem 6.2 no theorem of $\mathsf{S4.2}$ can fail there. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (Independence of $.3$).** $\Box(\Box p \to q) \vee \Box(\Box q \to p)$ *is not derivable in $\mathsf{S4.2}$.*

*Proof.* As above, using Theorem 5.3 on the same finite frame with the atoms read as "button $b_1$ pushed" and "button $b_2$ pushed". $\square$

These are the two canonical separations: the modal logic of forcing is not $\mathsf{S5}$ (possibilities can expire) and not $\mathsf{S4.3}$ (the multiverse branches).

## 7. The multiverse-invariant fragment

A statement is *multiverse-true* if it holds throughout the relevant portion of the network — not merely upward from the current world. Preservation upward gives necessity above a world, which is strictly weaker. The right notion requires **bidirectional** control.

**Definition 7.1.** An assertion $p$ is **button-free** if it is built from switch atoms and $\bot$ by implication.

**Theorem 7.2 (Invariance of the button-free fragment).** *If $p$ is button-free then for all stages $S,T$ and every $g$, $(S,g) \models p \iff (T,g) \models p$. Consequently $p$ is a button for the switch-preserving extension relation and simultaneously a button for the switch-preserving ground relation; and if $p$ holds at one world it holds throughout the entire switch fibre.*

*Proof.* Induction on $p$; the atomic case is that switch atoms do not read the stage. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Invariant fragment theorem).** *For an arbitrary assertion $p$, the following are equivalent:*

1. *$p$ is invariant along the button order: $(S,g)\models p \iff (T,g) \models p$ for all $S,T,g$;*
2. *$p$ is equivalent, at every world, to a button-free assertion.*

*Proof.* (2) $\Rightarrow$ (1) is Theorem 7.2. For (1) $\Rightarrow$ (2), let $p^{\bot}$ be obtained from $p$ by substituting $\bot$ for every button atom. Then $p^{\bot}$ is button-free by construction, and a straightforward induction gives the substitution lemma
$$(S,g) \models p^{\bot} \iff (\varnothing, g) \models p$$
for every stage $S$ — at the empty stage every button atom is false, which is exactly what the substitution encodes. Invariance of $p$ gives $(\varnothing,g)\models p \iff (S,g)\models p$, so $p$ and $p^{\bot}$ agree everywhere. $\square$

**Proposition 7.4 (Sharpness).** *A bare button atom is not invariant: it is true at $(\{b\},g)$ and false at $(\varnothing,g)$. Hence no fragment containing a button atom is multiverse-invariant, and the characterization above is not vacuous.*

The theorem is exactly a *falsifiability* statement in the sense demanded of the programme: the class of invariant assertions is pinned down syntactically, and a single explicit assertion outside the class demonstrates that the boundary is real.

## 8. Calibrating branching by preserved backgrounds

In set-theoretic practice one wants CH to vary while a fixed background (say a hierarchy of indestructible large-cardinal assertions) is preserved in both branches. Abstractly a background is an element $b \in B$; the question is when both branches can be taken inside $b$.

**Theorem 8.1 (Exact preservation criterion).** *Let $B$ be rich, $v$ an assignment, $b \in B$ and $p$ an assertion. Then there exist generic filters $U$ and $V$ with $b \in U$, $\mathrm{quot}(v,U) \models p$, $b \in V$ and $\mathrm{quot}(v,V) \not\models p$ **iff***
$$b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \ne \bot \quad\text{and}\quad b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket_v^{\complement} \ne \bot .$$

*Proof.* ($\Rightarrow$) If $b \in U$ and $\mathrm{quot}(v,U)\models p$ then by the truth lemma $\llbracket p \rrbracket_v \in U$, so $b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket_v \in U$ and cannot be $\bot$ by properness. Symmetrically for $V$, using Lemma 2.6(i). ($\Leftarrow$) Richness applied to the two nonzero meets produces $U$ and $V$; upward closure extracts $b$ and the relevant half of $p$'s value from each. $\square$

This is the weakest possible preservation hypothesis: it is necessary as well as sufficient. Two consequences delimit the phenomenon exactly.

**Theorem 8.2 (Button-definable backgrounds always branch).** *Let $b \in S$ and take the background $v_S(\mathrm{btn}(b)) = \mathcal{G}$. Then both meets are nonempty, so the background survives into a CH branch and a $\neg$CH branch of one and the same Boolean-valued universe. Frame-side: for any property $\Theta$ of stages with $\Theta(S)$, the worlds $(S,\mathbf{1})$ and $(S,\mathbf{0})$ are accessible from $(S,g)$, satisfy $\Theta$, and disagree about CH.*

**Theorem 8.3 (Preservation obstruction).** *Take instead the switch-dependent background $b = \llbracket \mathrm{CH} \rrbracket_S$. Then $b \ne \bot$ but $b \wedge \llbracket \mathrm{CH} \rrbracket_S^{\complement} = \bot$, so by Theorem 8.1 no pair of opposite branches can preserve it.*

The moral: branching should be parameterized by invariants that the forcing must preserve — button-definable data — rather than by unconstrained propositional switches. A background entangled with the switch being varied is provably unpreservable, and the criterion says exactly where the boundary lies.

## 9. Ground/extension bimodality

**Definition 9.1 (Ground relation).** $(S,g) \sqsupseteq_{\mathrm{gnd}} (T,h) :\iff T \subseteq S$: the grounds of a world are the worlds that have pushed no more buttons. This is the converse of $\sqsubseteq$. Write $\check{\Box}, \check{\Diamond}$ for its modalities.

**Proposition 9.2.** *The ground relation is reflexive, transitive, and* **downward directed**: *any two grounds of a world have a common ground, namely the intersection of their pushed sets. Moreover the world $(\varnothing, g)$ is a ground of every world — the frame-theoretic analogue of the* **mantle**, *the intersection of all grounds — and $\check{\Diamond}(\text{no button pushed})$ holds everywhere.*

**Theorem 9.3 (Mixed tense validity).** *For every predicate $P$ and world $w$: if $P(w)$ then $\Box\, \check{\Diamond}\, P (w)$.*

*Proof.* Let $w \sqsubseteq v$. Then $w$ is a ground of $v$, and $P(w)$; so $\check{\Diamond}P(v)$. $\square$

**Theorem 9.4 (Bimodal separation).** *On the control frame with at least one button, the mixed axiom $p \to \Box\check{\Diamond}p$ is valid while the unimodal Brouwer axiom $p \to \Box\Diamond p$ fails.*

*Proof.* Theorem 9.3 and Proposition 3.6. $\square$

Theorem 9.4 is a concrete demonstration that the bimodal logic of the multiverse is not determined by the extension modality alone: the ground modality contributes validities the forcing modality cannot see. Semantically the reason is transparent — $\check{\Diamond}$ can *return* to a world already visited, which is exactly what $\Diamond$ cannot do once a button has been pushed.

## 10. Bounded morphisms and transfer

**Definition 10.1.** A **bounded morphism** (p-morphism) $f : (W,R) \to (W',R')$ is a map with

- *forth:* $R\,w\,v \Rightarrow R'\,f(w)\,f(v)$;
- *back:* $R'\,f(w)\,u \Rightarrow \exists v\,(R\,w\,v \wedge f(v) = u)$.

Bounded morphisms compose.

**Theorem 10.2 (Truth transfer).** *For a bounded morphism $f$, valuation $V'$ on $W'$, and every modal formula $p$,*
$$\mathfrak{M}, w \models p \;\text{ under }\; V := V' \circ f \quad\iff\quad \mathfrak{M}', f(w) \models p \;\text{ under }\; V'.$$

*Proof.* Induction on $p$. The $\Box$ case uses *back* for the left-to-right direction and *forth* for the converse. $\square$

**Corollary 10.3 (Validity transfer).** *If $f$ is a surjective bounded morphism, every formula valid on $(W,R)$ is valid on $(W',R')$: the logic of a frame is contained in the logic of each of its surjective bounded images.*

**Theorem 10.4 (Switches are semantically free).** *The projection $(S,g) \mapsto S$ is a surjective bounded morphism from the control frame onto the pure button order $(\{ \text{finite } S \}, \subseteq)$.*

*Proof.* Forth is the definition of $\sqsubseteq$. Back: given $S \subseteq U$, the world $(U, g)$ is accessible from $(S,g)$ and projects to $U$. Surjectivity is clear. $\square$

Hence switches contribute no modal validities of their own; they matter for *which assertions* are switches, not for the logic of the frame.

**Theorem 10.5 (Chains are bounded images).** *The cardinality map $S \mapsto |S|$ is a surjective bounded morphism from the $n$-button order onto the chain $\{0,1,\dots,n\}$.*

*Proof.* Forth: $S \subseteq T$ gives $|S| \le |T|$. Back: given $|S| \le k \le n$, extend $S$ to a set of size exactly $k$; this is possible since the ambient button set has $n$ elements. $\square$

Composing, the control frame maps onto every finite chain.

**Theorem 10.6 (Strict containment).** *For $n \ge 2$, the modal logic of the $n$-button control frame is contained in the logic of the $(n+1)$-element chain, and strictly so: linearity $.3$ is valid on every total frame, hence on the chain, but is refuted on the control frame by two independent buttons.*

*Proof.* Containment is Corollary 10.3 with Theorem 10.5. Validity of $.3$ on total frames: suppose both disjuncts fail at $w$, giving successors $v$ (with $\Box p$ and $\neg q$) and $u$ (with $\Box q$ and $\neg p$); comparability of $v,u$ yields a contradiction either way. Failure on the control frame is Theorem 5.3. $\square$

So the multiverse admits a faithful projection to a linear picture, but the projection is lossy: no linearity principle may be added to the logic of forcing.

## 11. Counting the finite frames

**Lemma 11.1.** *For a finite set $s$, $\displaystyle\sum_{t \subseteq s} 2^{|t|} = 3^{|s|}$.*

*Proof.* Induction on $s$. For the step from $s$ to $s \cup \{a\}$ with $a \notin s$, split the subsets of $s\cup\{a\}$ into those omitting $a$ (contributing $\sum_{t\subseteq s} 2^{|t|}$) and those containing $a$ (contributing $\sum_{t\subseteq s} 2^{|t|+1} = 2\sum_{t\subseteq s}2^{|t|}$), giving $3 \cdot 3^{|s|}$. Combinatorially: each element of $s$ is in one of three states relative to the pair (ambient set, subset). $\square$

**Theorem 11.2 (Exact size of the forcing relation).** *The control frame with $n$ buttons and $m$ switches has $2^{n+m}$ worlds and exactly*
$$3^n \cdot 4^m$$
*accessibility pairs.*

*Proof.* The set of worlds accessible *to* a fixed $v = (T,h)$ is $\mathcal{P}(T) \times \{0,1\}^{\mathrm{Sw}}$, of size $2^{|T|} \cdot 2^m$. Summing over all $v$, i.e. over all $T$ and all $h$, gives
$$\sum_{T \subseteq [n]} \sum_{h} 2^{|T|} 2^m = \sum_{T \subseteq [n]} 2^{|T|} \cdot 4^m = 3^n \cdot 4^m$$
by Lemma 11.1. $\square$

The exponents record the two regimes: each button has three admissible states across an accessible pair (off/off, off/on, on/on — never on/off), each switch four (all combinations). The formula is the arithmetical signature of irreversibility versus freedom, and it also quantifies how much room these finite frames leave for embedding arbitrary finite directed preorders — the missing ingredient in a finite-model completeness proof for $\mathsf{S4.2}$.

## 12. Algorithms

The finite control frames are small enough to compute with, and every result above has an effective counterpart.

**Boolean value evaluation.** Represent the algebra $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{G})$ with $|\mathcal{G}| = 2^m$ as bitmasks of width $2^m$. Then $\llbracket p \rrbracket_S$ is computed by structural recursion in $O(|p|)$ bitwise operations: $\llbracket \bot \rrbracket = 0$, $\llbracket p \to q \rrbracket = (\sim\!\llbracket p \rrbracket) \mid \llbracket q \rrbracket$ masked to width $2^m$. The realization theorem then reduces two-valued evaluation at $(S,g)$ to a single bit test.

**Frame enumeration and counting.** Enumerate the $2^{n+m}$ worlds and test $S \subseteq T$ pairwise in $O(4^{n+m})$ bit operations, confirming the closed form $3^n 4^m$.

**Modal validity checking.** A modal schema is valid on a finite frame iff it holds for all valuations. For $k$ atoms and $|W|$ worlds there are $2^{k|W|}$ valuations; for the schema formulations used here (quantifying over arbitrary predicates) the search is over $2^{|W|}$ predicates per schematic letter. This is exponential but entirely feasible for the frames that produce the canonical countermodels ($n = m = 1$ or $n = 2$).

**Invariance testing and button-free normalization.** Given $p$, compute $p^{\bot}$ by substituting $\bot$ for button atoms — linear in $|p|$ — and test agreement of $p$ with $p^{\bot}$ over all $2^{n+m}$ worlds. By Theorem 7.3 this decides multiverse invariance, and returns a canonical button-free witness when the answer is yes.

**Branch preservation.** Given a background bitmask $b$ and a formula $p$, check $b \wedge \llbracket p \rrbracket \ne 0$ and $b \wedge \sim\!\llbracket p \rrbracket \ne 0$ in $O(1)$ word operations; by Theorem 8.1 this decides two-sided preservation exactly.

## 13. Discussion

### 13.1 What has been derived rather than assumed

The distinguishing feature of the development is the direction of implication. In an axiomatic treatment, directedness, the button law and CH branching are *hypotheses* on a frame. Here:

- directedness comes from the union of stages;
- the button law comes from monotonicity of Boolean values along the stage order (Lemma 4.5) transported by the realization theorem;
- CH branching comes from the branching theorem applied to a Boolean value provably distinct from $\bot$ and $\top$;
- forcing closure comes from soundness of Boolean-valued semantics for a complete Hilbert calculus.

Each of these is an implication with a construction on the left, and the whole is packaged as a single satisfaction relation validating all of them at once (Theorem 4.10).

### 13.2 Scope and honest limitations

The construction is *propositional*. Atoms stand for set-theoretic assertions, and their interplay — a button is permanent, a switch is free — is modelled faithfully, but the quantifier structure of real set-theoretic sentences is not. Correspondingly:

- The Boolean algebra is a powerset algebra, hence complete and atomic; genuine forcing algebras are typically atomless, and the intuition that "no condition decides everything" is not modelled by principal filters. What survives is exactly the part of the theory that the modal analysis uses: the truth lemma, forcing closure, and branching.
- Genericity here is decisiveness of the filter, not meeting all dense sets from a ground model. For the propositional language the two coincide in effect, since every element of the algebra is the value of some assertion only up to the finite fragment generated by the atoms.
- "Buttons" and "switches" are stipulated to be atoms of the corresponding kind rather than derived from set-theoretic content; what is derived is that they *behave* as buttons and switches in the realized semantics, and that the positive fragment generated by button atoms is closed under the button law.

Within that scope, everything claimed is proved outright, with no unproved set-theoretic hypotheses and, for the branching results, no appeal to choice.

### 13.3 Relation to the modal logic of forcing

The results reproduce, at the propositional level and from an explicit construction, the two structural halves of the standard analysis: the lower bound $\mathsf{S4.2}$ from reflexivity, transitivity and directedness, and the upper-bound apparatus from buttons and switches. Theorem 5.2 sharpens the folklore "buttons kill $\mathsf{S5}$" into an equivalence, and Theorem 5.1 turns the correspondence between $.2$ and directedness into a two-way theorem on arbitrary relations. Theorem 10.4 isolates a fact that is easy to miss: switches, despite their conceptual centrality, contribute nothing to the *validities* of the frame; they are semantically free, and all the modal strength lives in the button order.

### 13.4 Multiverse truth versus forcing necessity

Theorem 7.3 makes precise a distinction that is often blurred. $\Box p$ says $p$ survives all extensions; it says nothing about grounds, and hence nothing about truth "throughout the multiverse". Genuine invariance is bidirectional, and the syntactic characterization shows it is a real restriction: the invariant assertions are exactly those that never mention a button. Layer D shows that once the ground modality is admitted as a first-class citizen, new validities appear (Theorem 9.4), so the bimodal logic is strictly richer than the unimodal one.

## 14. Future work

1. **Finite-frame completeness of $\mathsf{S4.2}$.** Conjecture: a modal formula is derivable in $\mathsf{S4.2}$ iff it is valid on every finite $n$-button, $m$-switch control frame. Soundness is Theorem 6.2 and the two hardest refuting instances ($5$ and $.3$) are Theorems 6.3–6.4; what remains is a filtration argument converting an arbitrary finite directed-preorder countermodel into a button–switch product. Theorem 11.2 quantifies the available room, and Theorems 10.4–10.5 show the bounded-morphism technique works in two special cases.
2. **A Boolean-valued Lindenbaum theorem.** Characterize which Boolean algebras arise as value algebras of realizations satisfying prescribed richness and branching data, and identify the algebraic content of directedness.
3. **Beyond the propositional level.** Replace atoms by a first-order language with Boolean-valued quantifier clauses $\llbracket \forall x\, \varphi \rrbracket = \bigwedge_a \llbracket \varphi(a) \rrbracket$, which requires completeness of the algebra; determine which of Theorems 4.6–4.10 survive.
4. **Large-cardinal calibration.** Refine Theorem 8.1 by parameterizing backgrounds with invariants a forcing must preserve, aiming at a uniform preparation principle producing CH and $\neg$CH branches preserving a fixed finite hierarchy of indestructible assertions; Theorem 8.3 identifies the shape of the obstruction.
5. **The full bimodal logic.** Determine the interaction logic generated by upward directedness, downward directedness and a least ground, in the language with both $\Box$ and $\check{\Box}$. Theorem 9.4 shows the mixed validities are not generated by the unimodal fragments; the open problem is an axiomatization.

## 15. Conclusion

Abstract accounts of the forcing multiverse specify the interface a semantics must satisfy: a reflexive, transitive, directed frame with buttons and switches. This paper supplies a semantics meeting that interface exactly, built from Boolean values and generic quotients, in which the interface conditions are theorems. Around it the modal picture becomes exact: $.2$ is directedness, $5$ is the absence of buttons, linearity fails for two independent buttons, invariance is button-freeness, two-sided branch preservation is a Boolean meet condition, switches are semantically free, and the accessibility relation of the $n$-button, $m$-switch frame has exactly $3^n 4^m$ pairs. The control panel of irreversible buttons and free switches is not a metaphor for forcing; it is a generic quotient of a Boolean-valued universe.

## Abstract

The Berggren tree organises the primitive Pythagorean triples into a perfect ternary tree
rooted at $(3,4,5)$, generated by three integer matrices whose arithmetic is controlled by
the real quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2)$: the middle generator is hyperbolic with
eigenvalues $3 \pm 2\sqrt 2 = (1\pm\sqrt2)^2$, the squares of the fundamental unit
$\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. We construct and analyse the Dirichlet
series $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s) = \sum_{w} c(w)^{-s}$ over the nodes $w$ of this tree, $c(w)$
denoting the hypotenuse, and we test the natural conjecture that its abscissa of
convergence is the *silver value* $\sigma_0 = \log 3/(2\log(1+\sqrt2)) = 0.6232\ldots$,
obtained by dividing the branching entropy $\log 3$ by the silver expansion rate
$2\log\varepsilon$.

We prove four groups of results.

1. **Structure.** In Euclid-seed coordinates the tree is a bijection: words in a
three-letter alphabet correspond one-to-one with admissible seeds
$\{(m,n): n<m,\ n\ge1,\ \gcd(m,n)=1,\ m+n \text{ odd}\}$, and $c = m^2+n^2$.
2. **Abscissa.** $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s)$ converges if and only if $s>1$. The silver
conjecture is therefore false; in particular the series diverges at $s = \sigma_0$ and at
$s=\log(1+\sqrt2)$.
3. **Mechanism and critical line.** The failure is caused by a growth dichotomy: the silver
speed limit $c(w)\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^{|w|}$ holds for every word and is attained on the Pell
spine, but the two outer spines grow only quadratically,
$c(L^k) = 2k^2+6k+5$ and $c(R^k) = 4k^2+8k+5$. Grading by depth instead of height yields
the silver Ihara zeta $Z_\varepsilon(s) = (1-3\varepsilon^{-2s})^{-1}$, which is
meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$, has *no zeros*, satisfies the exact functional equation
$Z_\varepsilon(s+i\pi/\log\varepsilon)=Z_\varepsilon(s)$, and whose pole set is exactly
$\{\operatorname{Re}s = \sigma_0,\ \operatorname{Im}s \in (\pi/\log\varepsilon)\mathbb{Z}\}$
with all poles simple and of the same residue $1/(2\log\varepsilon)=0.56728\ldots$ — a
provable analogue of the Riemann Hypothesis.
4. **Counting, primes, and restoration.** The counting function satisfies
$N(H)\le(\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor+1)^2$ and $N(H)\ge \frac13(H/5)^{\sigma_0}$; the prime
hypotenuses of the tree are exactly the primes $p\equiv1\pmod4$, of which there are
infinitely many; and the free binary subtree generated by the blocks $MM$ and $MR$ — which
removes the parabolic directions — has abscissa in $[0.1966,\,0.7565]\subset(0,1)$ and in
particular converges at $s=1$, where the full tree zeta diverges.

The extracted principle is that the silver unit governs the *depth* grading of the Berggren
tree, not its *height* grading; every silver statement about $|w|$ survives, and every
attempt to transport it to $c(w)$ fails.

**Keywords.** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, Dirichlet series, Ihara zeta function,
silver ratio, Pell equation, critical line, abscissa of convergence, primes $\equiv 1
\bmod 4$.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Berggren tree

A triple $(a,b,c)$ of positive integers with $a^2+b^2=c^2$ is *primitive* if
$\gcd(a,b,c)=1$. Berggren (1934) showed — and Barning (1963) rediscovered — that the set of
primitive Pythagorean triples, written as column vectors, is the orbit of $(3,4,5)^{\mathsf
T}$ under the free monoid generated by

$$
A_1=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&2\\ 2&-1&2\\ 2&-2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_2=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&2\\ 2&1&2\\ 2&2&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_3=\begin{pmatrix}-1&2&2\\ -2&1&2\\ -2&2&3\end{pmatrix},
$$

each triple being obtained exactly once. The resulting ternary tree has $3^d$ nodes at
depth $d$.

The three matrices lie in the orthogonal group of the quadratic form $a^2+b^2-c^2$, and
their arithmetic is that of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$. The middle generator $A_2$ is hyperbolic
with eigenvalues $3\pm2\sqrt2$ (and $1$), and $3+2\sqrt2 = \varepsilon^2$ where
$\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$ is the fundamental unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$, the smallest
solution of Pell's equation $x^2-2y^2=-1$.

### 1.2 The question

Attach to the tree the Dirichlet series
$$Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s) \;=\; \sum_{w\in\{L,M,R\}^{*}} c(w)^{-s},$$
where $c(w)$ is the hypotenuse at the node reached by the word $w$. Three questions,
modelled on the classical theory of zeta functions:

- **(Q1)** What is the abscissa of convergence $\sigma_c$?
- **(Q2)** Does the series admit a meromorphic continuation, and where are its poles and
  zeros?
- **(Q3)** What is the distribution of *prime* hypotenuses, and is it controlled by the
  singularities?

The heuristic answer to (Q1), for any self-similar tree with branching number $b$ whose
size grows by a factor $\lambda$ per generation, is $\sigma_c = \log b/\log\lambda$. With
$b=3$ and $\lambda=\varepsilon^2$ this predicts the **silver abscissa**
$$\sigma_0 \;=\; \frac{\log 3}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)} \;=\; 0.623239\ldots$$
This paper determines the truth of that prediction, isolates why it holds or fails, and
constructs the analytic object for which the prediction is exactly right.

### 1.3 Summary of results

| Object | Result |
|---|---|
| Word–seed map | Bijection onto admissible Euclid seeds |
| $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}$ | Converges iff $s>1$; abscissa $=1 \ne \sigma_0$ |
| Growth | $c(w)\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^{|w|}$; Pell spine attains it; $L$/$R$ spines quadratic |
| $N(H)$ | $\frac13(H/5)^{\sigma_0} \le N(H) \le (\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor+1)^2$ |
| $Z_\varepsilon$ | Meromorphic on $\mathbb{C}$; poles exactly on $\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma_0$, spacing $\pi/\log\varepsilon$; no zeros; period $i\pi/\log\varepsilon$; simple poles, residue $1/(2\log\varepsilon)$ |
| Prime nodes | $c(w)\equiv1\pmod4$ always; prime hypotenuses $=$ primes $\equiv1\pmod4$; infinitely many |
| Hyperbolic subtree | Abscissa $\in[\log2/\log((3+2\sqrt2)^2),\ \log2/\log(5/2)] = [0.1966, 0.7565]$ |

## 2. Euclid-seed coordinates and the structure theorem

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Admissible seed).** A pair $(m,n)\in\mathbb{N}^2$ is an *admissible seed*
if $n<m$, $n\ge1$, $\gcd(m,n)=1$, and $m+n$ is odd. Write $\mathcal{S}$ for the set of
admissible seeds.

By Euclid's classification, $(m,n)\mapsto (m^2-n^2,\,2mn,\,m^2+n^2)$ is a bijection from
$\mathcal{S}$ onto the primitive Pythagorean triples with even second leg. We call
$$c(m,n) = m^2+n^2$$
the *hypotenuse* of the seed.

**Definition 2.2 (Seed moves).** Define three maps $\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}$:
$$L(m,n)=(2m-n,\;m),\qquad M(m,n)=(2m+n,\;m),\qquad R(m,n)=(m+2n,\;n).$$

**Definition 2.3 (Nodes).** For a word $w = i_1 i_2\cdots i_k$ over the alphabet
$\{L,M,R\}$, let $\mathrm{seed}(w)$ be the result of applying $i_1$ to
$\mathrm{seed}(i_2\cdots i_k)$, with $\mathrm{seed}(\epsilon) = (2,1)$; and set
$c(w) = c(\mathrm{seed}(w))$, $|w| = k$.

The root $(2,1)$ is the triple $(3,4,5)$.

**Proposition 2.4 (Matrix compatibility).** On the triple coordinates
$(a,b,c) = (m^2-n^2, 2mn, m^2+n^2)$, the moves $L$, $M$, $R$ act exactly as the matrices
$A_1$, $A_2$, $A_3$ respectively.

*Proof sketch.* Direct expansion. For instance $M(m,n)=(2m+n,m)$ gives
$$a' = (2m+n)^2-m^2 = 3m^2+4mn+n^2, \quad b' = 2m(2m+n)=4m^2+2mn,\quad c' = 5m^2+4mn+n^2,$$
while $A_2(a,b,c)^{\mathsf T} = (a+2b+2c,\,2a+b+2c,\,2a+2b+3c)^{\mathsf T}$ evaluates, on
$(m^2-n^2, 2mn, m^2+n^2)$, to the same triple. The other two are analogous, with $A_1$
matching $L$ and $A_3$ matching $R$. $\square$

### 2.2 Well-definedness and completeness

**Lemma 2.5 (Invariance).** If $(m,n)\in\mathcal{S}$ then $L(m,n), M(m,n), R(m,n)\in\mathcal{S}$.

*Proof sketch.* Coprimality: $\gcd(2m-n,m) = \gcd(n,m)=1$, $\gcd(2m+n,m)=\gcd(n,m)=1$, and
$\gcd(m+2n,n)=\gcd(m,n)=1$. Parity: $(2m-n)+m = 3m-n$ and $m+n$ odd force $m,n$ of opposite
parity, hence $3m-n$ odd; similarly for the others. Order: $n<m$ gives $m < 2m-n$ and
$m<2m+n$; and $n < m+2n$. Positivity of the second coordinate is clear. $\square$

**Theorem 2.6 (Completeness — Berggren).** Every admissible seed is $\mathrm{seed}(w)$ for
some word $w$.

*Proof sketch.* Strong induction on $m+n$. The three moves are invertible as affine maps,
$$L^{-1}(m,n) = (n,\,2n-m), \qquad M^{-1}(m,n) = (n,\,m-2n), \qquad R^{-1}(m,n) = (m-2n,\,n),$$
and exactly one of the three inverses lands back in $\mathcal{S}$, according to the
position of $m$ relative to $2n$ and $3n$:
- if $m<2n$, then $L^{-1}(m,n)=(n,2n-m)$ is admissible;
- if $2n<m<3n$, then $M^{-1}(m,n)=(n,m-2n)$ is admissible;
- if $m>3n$, then $R^{-1}(m,n)=(m-2n,n)$ is admissible.

The boundary cases are excluded by coprimality: $m=2n$ forces $n=1,m=2$ (the root), and
$m=3n$ forces $n=1,m=3$, which fails the parity condition. In each case the predecessor is
admissible and has strictly smaller coordinate sum, so the descent terminates at $(2,1)$.
The letters are peeled off from the outside in, so reading them in the order emitted gives
$w$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.7 (Uniqueness).** The map $w\mapsto\mathrm{seed}(w)$ is injective.

*Proof sketch.* Two ingredients. First, each move is individually injective on
$\mathcal{S}$: $M$ and $R$ are invertible as affine maps, and $L$ is invertible given
admissibility. Second, the three images are pairwise disjoint, by the "window" criterion
extracted from the case analysis of Theorem 2.6: writing $(m',n')$ for the image, $L$
yields $n'<m'<2n'$, $M$ yields $2n'<m'<3n'$, and $R$ yields $m'>3n'$. Finally, no move can output the root $(2,1)$, because $(2,1)$ is the
unique admissible seed with $m=2n$ and each image is strictly larger. Injectivity then
follows by induction on word length. $\square$

**Corollary 2.8 (Seed bijection).** $w\mapsto\mathrm{seed}(w)$ is a bijection
$\{L,M,R\}^{*} \to \mathcal{S}$. Consequently
$$Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathcal{S}}(m^2+n^2)^{-s}.$$

**Corollary 2.9.** Every node carries a primitive Pythagorean triple: for an admissible
seed, $\gcd(m^2-n^2,\,2mn)=1$.

## 3. The abscissa of convergence

**Theorem 3.1 (Abscissa Theorem).** For real $s$,
$$\sum_{w}c(w)^{-s}\ \text{converges}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad s>1 .$$

*Proof sketch.* By Corollary 2.8 the sum equals $\sum_{\mathcal{S}}(m^2+n^2)^{-s}$.

*Convergence for $s>1$.* Extend by zero to all of $\mathbb{N}^2$. Since
$m^2+n^2 \ge m^2$ and $m > n$, each row $m$ contributes at most $m\cdot m^{-2s}=m^{1-2s}$,
and $\sum_m m^{1-2s}$ converges precisely when $2s-1>1$, i.e. $s>1$. All terms are
non-negative, so absolute convergence follows by comparison.

*Divergence for $s\le1$.* Fix a prime $q$. For every odd $n$ with $n<q$, the pair $(2q,n)$
is an admissible seed: $n<2q$; $\gcd(2q,n)=1$ since $n$ is odd and $n<q$ with $q$ prime;
and $2q+n$ is odd. Its hypotenuse is $4q^2+n^2 \le 5q^2$, so for $s\le1$,
$$(4q^2+n^2)^{-s} \ge (5q^2)^{-1} = \frac{1}{5q^2}.$$
There are at least $\lfloor q/2\rfloor \ge (q-1)/2$ admissible values of $n$, so the fibre
over $m=2q$ contributes at least
$$\frac{q-1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{5q^2} \;\ge\; \frac{1}{20\,q}.$$
Distinct primes give distinct fibres, and $\sum_{q\ \mathrm{prime}} 1/q$ diverges (Euler).
Hence the series diverges for every $s\le1$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.2 (Refutation of the silver-abscissa conjecture).** The series diverges at
$s=\log(1+\sqrt2)=0.88137\ldots$ and at $s=\sigma_0 = 0.623239\ldots$, both of which are
$<1$. The abscissa of convergence is $1$, not the silver value.

**Remark 3.3.** The abscissa $1$ has a clean interpretation: the seeds are a
positive-density subset of a two-dimensional lattice under the quadratic size $m^2+n^2$, so
the series is a two-dimensional Epstein-type sum, with abscissa $2/2=1$. Sieving to
coprime, opposite-parity pairs changes the constant $\big(\prod_p$-type density factor
$\big)$ but not the abscissa. The tree structure is analytically invisible to the height
grading.

## 4. The growth dichotomy

### 4.1 The silver speed limit

**Lemma 4.1 (One-step bound).** For all real $m,n$,
$$(2m+n)^2+m^2 \le (3+2\sqrt2)(m^2+n^2), \qquad (m+2n)^2+n^2 \le (3+2\sqrt2)(m^2+n^2),$$
and the same bound holds for $L$.

*Proof sketch.* For $M$:
$$(3+2\sqrt2)(m^2+n^2) - \big((2m+n)^2+m^2\big) = (2\sqrt2-2)m^2 - 4mn + (2+2\sqrt2)n^2,$$
a positive semidefinite quadratic form: its discriminant is
$16 - 4(2\sqrt2-2)(2+2\sqrt2) = 16 - 4(4\sqrt2+8-4-4\sqrt2)=16-16=0$, so the form is a
perfect square, $(2\sqrt2-2)\big(m - \tfrac{2}{2\sqrt2-2}n\big)^2 \ge 0$. The $R$ case is
identical after swapping roles, and the $L$ case is dominated by the $M$ case since
$|2m-n|\le 2m+n$ for $n\ge0$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Silver speed limit).** For every word $w$,
$$c(w) \;\le\; 5\,(3+2\sqrt2)^{|w|} = 5\,\varepsilon^{2|w|}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $|w|$ from Lemma 4.1, with base $c(\epsilon)=5$. $\square$

### 4.2 The three spines

**Theorem 4.3 (Spine formulas).**
$$\mathrm{seed}(L^k) = (k+2,\,k+1),\qquad \mathrm{seed}(R^k)=(2k+2,\,1),$$
hence
$$c(L^k) = 2k^2+6k+5, \qquad c(R^k) = 4k^2+8k+5 .$$
Along the middle spine, $\mathrm{seed}(M^{k+1}) = (2m_k+n_k,\,m_k)$ with
$(m_0,n_0)=(2,1)$, and
$$4\,(3+2\sqrt2)^k \;\le\; c(M^k)\;\le\; 5\,(3+2\sqrt2)^k .$$

*Proof sketch.* The two outer formulas are immediate inductions: $L(k+2,k+1) = (k+3,k+2)$
and $R(2k+2,1)=(2k+4,1)$. For the middle spine, the recursion $m_{k+1}=2m_k+n_k$,
$n_{k+1}=m_k$ has characteristic polynomial $x^2-2x-1$ with roots $1\pm\sqrt2$; the
invariant $m_k \ge 2(1+\sqrt2)^k$ propagates by induction (using $n_k = m_{k-1}\ge0$) and
yields $c(M^k)=m_k^2+n_k^2 \ge (2(1+\sqrt2)^k)^2 = 4(3+2\sqrt2)^k$, while the upper bound
is Theorem 4.2. $\square$

**Interpretation (the dichotomy).** The middle branch is *hyperbolic*: it realises the
silver rate exactly, up to the constant factor between $4$ and $5$. The two outer spines
are *effectively parabolic*: they grow quadratically in the depth, i.e. their hypotenuses
are $\Theta(k^2)$, exponentially slower than the speed limit. The tree therefore contains
$\Theta(H^{1/2})$ nodes of height $\le H$ on each outer spine alone, and — by the fibre
argument of Theorem 3.1 — many more nearby. It is exactly this reservoir of small nodes at
large depth that destroys the silver abscissa.

The first few values:

| $k$ | $c(L^k)$ | $c(M^k)$ | $c(R^k)$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $5$ | $5$ | $5$ |
| $1$ | $13$ | $29$ | $17$ |
| $2$ | $25$ | $169$ | $37$ |
| $3$ | $41$ | $985$ | $65$ |
| $4$ | $61$ | $5741$ | $101$ |

The middle column is the sequence of Pell/NSW numbers $5,29,169,985,5741,\dots$, satisfying
$c_{k+1}=6c_k-c_{k-1}$ — the trace recursion of $\varepsilon^2 = 3+2\sqrt2$.

### 4.3 The depth slice

**Proposition 4.4.** For $s\ge0$ and every $k$,
$$\sum_{|w|=k} c(w)^{-s} \;\ge\; 3^{k}\big(5(3+2\sqrt2)^{k}\big)^{-s} .$$

*Proof sketch.* There are exactly $3^k$ words of length $k$, and each satisfies
$c(w)\le 5(3+2\sqrt2)^k$ by Theorem 4.2; since $x\mapsto x^{-s}$ is decreasing on
$(0,\infty)$ for $s\ge0$, each term is at least $\big(5(3+2\sqrt2)^k\big)^{-s}$. $\square$

This is precisely the $k$-th term of the silver Ihara series of Section 6, and it shows
that the depth-graded model is a genuine *lower* bound for the true series — the truth is
larger, never smaller.

## 5. The counting function

Let
$$N(H) \;=\; \#\{w : c(w)\le H\}.$$

**Lemma 5.1.** If $c(w)\le H$ then both Euclid parameters of $w$ are at most
$\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor$.

*Proof.* $m^2 \le m^2+n^2 \le H$ and $n^2\le H$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Upper bound; finiteness).** $N(H)$ is finite and
$$N(H) \;\le\; \big(\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor + 1\big)^2 \;=\; O(H).$$

*Proof sketch.* By Corollary 2.8 the map $w\mapsto\mathrm{seed}(w)$ is injective, and by
Lemma 5.1 it sends $\{c(w)\le H\}$ into the box
$[0,\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor]^2$, of cardinality $(\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor+1)^2$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Exponential lower bound).** If $5(3+2\sqrt2)^d\le H$ then $N(H)\ge 3^d$.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 4.2 every one of the $3^d$ words of length $d$ has $c(w) \le
5(3+2\sqrt2)^d\le H$; by injectivity these are $3^d$ distinct nodes. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (The silver exponent is a counting exponent).** For $H\ge5$,
$$N(H) \;\ge\; \frac13\left(\frac{H}{5}\right)^{\sigma_0},\qquad
\sigma_0 = \frac{\log 3}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)} .$$

*Proof sketch.* Put $A = 3+2\sqrt2 = \varepsilon^2 > 1$ and $L = \log(H/5)/\log A \ge 0$,
$d = \lfloor L\rfloor$. Then $A^d\le A^{L}=H/5$, so $5A^d \le H$ and Theorem 5.3 gives
$N(H)\ge 3^d$. Since $d > L-1$ and $3^{L} = \exp(L\log3) = (H/5)^{\log3/\log A} =
(H/5)^{\sigma_0}$, we get $3^d \ge 3^{L-1} = \frac13(H/5)^{\sigma_0}$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.5 (Two-sided placement).** $0<\sigma_0<1$ and
$$\tfrac13(H/5)^{\sigma_0} \;\le\; N(H)\;\le\; (\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor+1)^2 .$$
The silver exponent is a genuine lower growth exponent for the counting function, strictly
smaller than the true one, which by Theorem 3.1 must be $1$.

**Numerical evidence.**

| $H$ | $N(H)$ | $(\lfloor\sqrt H\rfloor+1)^2$ | largest $d$ with $5(3+2\sqrt2)^d\le H$ | $3^d$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $5$ | $1$ | $9$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| $50$ | $7$ | $64$ | $1$ | $3$ |
| $200$ | $32$ | $225$ | $2$ | $9$ |
| $1000$ | $158$ | $1024$ | $3$ | $27$ |

The counts, obtained by enumerating admissible seeds with $m^2+n^2\le H$ (which by
Corollary 2.8 is exactly the node count), sit strictly between $3^d\asymp H^{\sigma_0}$ and
$(\sqrt H+1)^2$, and grow essentially linearly in $H$, in accordance with Theorem 3.1.
Extending the computation gives $N(10^4)=1593$, $N(10^5)=15919$, so
$N(H)/H \to 0.1592\ldots$

**Remark 5.6 (Heuristic density; not proved here).** The observed constant matches
$1/(2\pi) = 0.159155\ldots$, as a standard lattice-point count predicts: the number of
lattice points with $1\le n<m$ and $m^2+n^2\le H$ is $\sim \pi H/8$ (an eighth of the disc
of radius $\sqrt H$); the coprime pairs have density $6/\pi^2$; and among coprime pairs,
exactly $2/3$ have $m+n$ odd (of the three residue classes of a coprime pair modulo $2$,
two are of opposite parity). This gives
$$N(H) \sim \frac{\pi H}{8}\cdot\frac{6}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac23 = \frac{H}{2\pi},$$
consistent with the abscissa $1$ of Theorem 3.1. We record this as a heuristic; only the
two-sided bounds of Theorems 5.2 and 5.4 are established here.

## 6. The silver Ihara zeta and its critical line

The lesson of Sections 3–5 is that the silver unit governs the *depth* grading. We now
build the analytic object for which that grading is the size.

**Definition 6.1.** Let $\varepsilon = 1+\sqrt2$, so $\varepsilon^2 = 3+2\sqrt2$, and set
$$\sigma_0 \;=\; \frac{\log 3}{2\log\varepsilon}, \qquad
Z_\varepsilon(s) \;=\; \big(1 - 3\,\varepsilon^{-2s}\big)^{-1}, \quad s\in\mathbb{C},$$
where $\varepsilon^{-2s} := \exp(-2s\log\varepsilon)$.

**Proposition 6.2 (Dirichlet series form).** For $\operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_0$,
$$Z_\varepsilon(s) \;=\; \sum_{k\ge0} 3^{k}\,\varepsilon^{-2ks},$$
the sum over depth slices of the idealised tree in which every node of depth $k$ has size
exactly $\varepsilon^{2k}$.

*Proof sketch.* $|3\varepsilon^{-2s}| = 3\varepsilon^{-2\operatorname{Re}s}$, which is
$<1$ iff $\operatorname{Re}s\log\varepsilon^{2} > \log3$, i.e.
$\operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_0$. Then sum the geometric series. $\square$

Thus $Z_\varepsilon$ is an Ihara-type zeta: branching number $3$, common edge length
$2\log\varepsilon$. By Proposition 4.4 its terms are lower bounds for the true depth slices
of the Berggren tree (up to the constant $5^{-s}$).

**Theorem 6.3 (Meromorphic continuation).** The denominator
$D(s) = 1-3\varepsilon^{-2s}$ is entire, so $Z_\varepsilon = 1/D$ is meromorphic on all of
$\mathbb{C}$, analytic at every point where $D(s)\ne0$.

*Proof sketch.* $D(s) = 1-3\exp(-2s\log\varepsilon)$ is a composition of entire functions,
hence entire; reciprocals of entire functions are meromorphic. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (Critical Line Theorem).** For $s\in\mathbb{C}$,
$$D(s)=0 \iff \operatorname{Re}s = \sigma_0 \ \text{ and }\ \operatorname{Im}s \in
\frac{\pi}{\log\varepsilon}\,\mathbb{Z}.$$
Equivalently, the pole set of $Z_\varepsilon$ is exactly the arithmetic progression
$$s_k \;=\; \sigma_0 + \frac{ik\pi}{\log\varepsilon},\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},$$
all lying on the single vertical line $\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma_0$, with spacing
$\pi/\log\varepsilon = 3.5644\ldots$

*Proof sketch.* Write $\varepsilon^{-2s} = \exp(-2s\log\varepsilon)$ and $1/3 =
\exp(-\log3)$. Then $D(s)=0$ iff
$$\exp\big(-2s\log\varepsilon\big) = \exp\big(-\log3\big),$$
which by periodicity of $\exp$ holds iff
$-2s\log\varepsilon = -\log3 + 2\pi i k$ for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$. Since $\log\varepsilon >
0$ is real, taking real and imaginary parts gives
$$-2\sigma\log\varepsilon = -\log3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sigma = \sigma_0,$$
$$-2t\log\varepsilon = 2\pi k \quad\Rightarrow\quad t = -\frac{k\pi}{\log\varepsilon},$$
and $k$ ranges over $\mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

The real part is pinned with *no freedom whatsoever* — the critical line is forced by the
single relation $\varepsilon^{2\sigma_0}=3$, i.e. branching entropy $=$ expansion rate
times abscissa. This is the exactly-solvable analogue of the Riemann Hypothesis promised in
the title.

**Proposition 6.5 (Placement).** $0<\sigma_0<1$; numerically $\sigma_0 = 0.623239\ldots$

*Proof sketch.* $\log3 > 0$ and $\log\varepsilon>0$ give positivity. For $\sigma_0<1$ it
suffices that $3 < \varepsilon^2 = 3+2\sqrt2$, which is clear. $\square$

### 6.1 Zeros, functional equation, residues

**Theorem 6.6 (No zeros).** $Z_\varepsilon(s)\ne0$ for every $s$ off the polar set. Hence
the divisor of $Z_\varepsilon$ is purely polar, concentrated on the critical line.

*Proof.* $Z_\varepsilon = 1/D$ and a reciprocal never vanishes. $\square$

This is the strongest possible form of a "Riemann Hypothesis" statement: not merely do all
zeros lie on a line — there are no zeros at all, and *all* the singular behaviour of the
function is confined to a single vertical line.

**Theorem 6.7 (Functional equation / periodicity).** For all $s\in\mathbb{C}$,
$$Z_\varepsilon\!\left(s + \frac{i\pi}{\log\varepsilon}\right) \;=\; Z_\varepsilon(s).$$

*Proof sketch.* $-2\big(s + i\pi/\log\varepsilon\big)\log\varepsilon = -2s\log\varepsilon -
2\pi i$, and $\exp$ is $2\pi i$-periodic, so $\varepsilon^{-2(s+i\pi/\log\varepsilon)} =
\varepsilon^{-2s}$; hence $D$, and therefore $Z_\varepsilon$, is unchanged. $\square$

The period equals exactly the pole spacing: the function is determined by its values on a
horizontal strip of height $\pi/\log\varepsilon$, containing precisely one pole. Compare
Riemann's $\zeta$, whose functional equation reflects $s\mapsto 1-s$ about the critical
line; here the symmetry is a translation *along* the critical line.

**Theorem 6.8 (Simple poles, uniform residue).** Let $s_0$ be any pole. Then
$$\lim_{s\to s_0}(s-s_0)\,Z_\varepsilon(s) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\log\varepsilon}
\;=\; \frac{1}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)} \;=\; 0.567296\ldots$$
In particular every pole is simple, and the residue is the *same constant* at every one of
the infinitely many poles $s_k$.

*Proof sketch.* $D'(s) = 2\log\varepsilon\cdot 3\varepsilon^{-2s}$. At a pole,
$3\varepsilon^{-2s_0}=1$, so $D'(s_0)=2\log\varepsilon\ne0$; the zero of $D$ is therefore
simple, and
$$\lim_{s\to s_0}\frac{s-s_0}{D(s)} = \lim_{s\to s_0}\Big(\frac{D(s)-D(s_0)}{s-s_0}\Big)^{-1}
= \frac{1}{D'(s_0)} = \frac{1}{2\log\varepsilon}. \qquad\square$$

**Interpretation.** For the Riemann zeta, the residue $1$ at $s=1$ is the source of the
main term $x$ in the Prime Number Theorem. Here $1/(2\log\varepsilon)$ is the *density of
the depth grading*: one generation per $2\log\varepsilon$ units of logarithmic size. A
Tauberian reading of Theorem 6.8 recovers exactly the counting statement of Theorem 5.4:
the number of depth-graded nodes of idealised size $\le X$ is
$\sim \frac{1}{2\log\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{X^{\sigma_0}}{\sigma_0}$, and the oscillatory
corrections come from the other poles $s_k$, contributing terms $X^{\sigma_0}\cos(k\pi\log
X/\log\varepsilon)$ — a perfectly explicit "explicit formula" with bounded, periodic
fluctuation and no error term beyond it. The critical line here is not merely provable; the
entire explicit formula is.

## 7. Prime hypotenuses

**Theorem 7.1 (Congruence).** For every node, $c(w)\equiv1\pmod4$.

*Proof sketch.* In an admissible seed $m+n$ is odd, so exactly one of $m,n$ is even. Then
$m^2+n^2 \equiv 0+1 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ (squares are $0$ or $1$ mod $4$, and an odd square is
$1$ mod $8$). $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Characterisation of prime hypotenuses).** For a prime $p$,
$$\exists\,w:\ c(w)=p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad p\equiv1\pmod4 .$$

*Proof sketch.* ($\Rightarrow$) Theorem 7.1. ($\Leftarrow$) By Fermat's two-square theorem
$p = m^2+n^2$ with $m>n\ge1$ (the case $m=n$ is excluded since $p$ is odd, and $n=0$ since
$p$ is not a square). Then $\gcd(m,n)$ divides $p$ and is $<p$, hence $=1$; and $m+n$ is odd
because $m^2+n^2$ is odd. So $(m,n)$ is an admissible seed, and by Corollary 2.8 it is a
node. $\square$

**Theorem 7.3 (Infinitude).** Infinitely many nodes carry a prime hypotenuse.

*Proof sketch.* Dirichlet's theorem gives infinitely many primes $\equiv1\pmod4$; Theorem
7.2 lifts each to a node. $\square$

The prime nodes are therefore an exact copy of the split primes of the Gaussian integers
$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — those $p$ with $\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)=1$.

**Theorem 7.4 (Prime-node zeta).** For $s>1$ the series
$$P(s) \;=\; \sum_{c(w)\ \mathrm{prime}} c(w)^{-s}$$
converges, being dominated termwise by $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s)$; and for every finite set $F$
of primes $\equiv1\pmod4$,
$$\sum_{p\in F} p^{-s} \;\le\; P(s).$$

*Proof sketch.* Domination is immediate from non-negativity and Theorem 3.1. For the lower
bound, Theorem 7.2 supplies for each $p \in F$ a node $w_p$ with $c(w_p) = p$; distinct
primes give distinct nodes, so the $w_p$ index a sub-sum of $P(s)$. $\square$

**Corollary 7.5 (Density of prime nodes).** $P(s) \ge \sum_{p\equiv1(4)}p^{-s}$, which by
Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions blows up like
$\tfrac12\log\frac{1}{s-1}$ as $s\to1^+$. Thus $P$ has abscissa exactly $1$, and the prime
hypotenuses have Dirichlet density $\tfrac12$ inside the primes.

The tree does not distort the distribution of primes; it transports it faithfully. Any
"Riemann Hypothesis" for prime hypotenuses is *exactly* the Riemann Hypothesis for the
Dirichlet $L$-function $L(s,\chi_4)$ together with $\zeta$ — the tree adds no new
difficulty and removes none.

## 8. Restoring the silver law: purely hyperbolic subtrees

If the parabolic spines are what destroy the silver abscissa, removing them should restore
it. We make this precise.

**Definition 8.1 (Blocks).** Let $\beta(\texttt{1}) = MM$ and $\beta(\texttt{0}) = MR$, two
two-letter Berggren words, each containing the hyperbolic Pell move. For a bit string
$b = b_1\cdots b_d$, let $\beta(b)$ be the concatenation
$\beta(b_1)\beta(b_2)\cdots\beta(b_d)$, a Berggren word of length $2d$.

**Proposition 8.2 (Free binary subtree).** $b\mapsto\beta(b)$ is injective; the image is a
free binary subtree of the ternary Berggren tree.

*Proof sketch.* The blocks $MM$ and $MR$ have distinct second letters, so the
concatenations are distinguished by reading off letters in pairs; injectivity of
$w\mapsto\mathrm{seed}(w)$ then separates the nodes. $\square$

**Lemma 8.3 (Block expansion).** Every block multiplies the hypotenuse by at least $5/2$:
$c(Mw)\ge \tfrac52 c(w)$ and $c(Rw)\ge c(w)$; hence $c(\beta(b)w)\ge\tfrac52\,c(w)$ for
either block.

*Proof sketch.* For $M$: $(2m+n)^2+m^2 = 5m^2+4mn+n^2 \ge \tfrac52(m^2+n^2)$, since
$\tfrac52 m^2 + 4mn - \tfrac32 n^2 \ge 0$ whenever $m>n\ge1$. For $R$: $(m+2n)^2+n^2 \ge
m^2+n^2$ trivially. $\square$

**Theorem 8.4 (Two-sided subtree growth).** For a bit string of length $d$,
$$5\left(\frac52\right)^{d} \;\le\; c(\beta(b)) \;\le\; 5\,(3+2\sqrt2)^{2d}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Lower bound: iterate Lemma 8.3 from the root value $5$. Upper bound: the
silver speed limit (Theorem 4.2) applied to a word of length $2d$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.5 (Subtree abscissa).** Let
$Z_{\mathrm{hyp}}(s) = \sum_{d\ge0}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^d} c(\beta(b))^{-s}$. Then
$$Z_{\mathrm{hyp}}(s)\ \text{converges for}\ s>\frac{\log2}{\log(5/2)} = 0.75647\ldots,$$
$$Z_{\mathrm{hyp}}(s)\ \text{diverges for}\ 0<s<\frac{\log2}{\log((3+2\sqrt2)^2)} = 0.19660\ldots$$
In particular $Z_{\mathrm{hyp}}(1)<\infty$, whereas $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(1)=\infty$.

*Proof sketch.* Convergence: by Theorem 8.4 each of the $2^d$ terms at level $d$ is at most
$\big(5(5/2)^d\big)^{-s}$, so the level sum is at most $5^{-s}\big(2(5/2)^{-s}\big)^d$, a
geometric series with ratio $<1$ exactly when $2 < (5/2)^{s}$, i.e. $s > \log2/\log(5/2)$.
Divergence: by the upper bound in Theorem 8.4 each level-$d$ term is at least
$\big(5(3+2\sqrt2)^{2d}\big)^{-s}$, so the level sum is at least
$5^{-s}\big(2\,A^{-s}\big)^d$ with $A=(3+2\sqrt2)^2$; when $2A^{-s}>1$, i.e.
$s<\log2/\log A$, the level sums do not tend to $0$ and the series diverges. $\square$

**Corollary 8.6.** The abscissa of $Z_{\mathrm{hyp}}$ lies in
$[0.1966,\,0.7565]\subset(0,1)$. The silver-type prediction "abscissa $=$
$\log(\text{branching})/\log(\text{growth})$" is qualitatively correct once the parabolic
generators are removed; its failure for the full tree is entirely attributable to them.

**Remark 8.7.** The bracket $[0.1966,0.7565]$ is not tight; the true abscissa is the unique
$s$ with $\sum_{b} c(\beta(b))^{-s}$ critical, and its exact value would follow from a
multiplicative ergodic (Lyapunov exponent) analysis of the random product of the two block
matrices $A_2^2$ and $A_2A_3$. The predicted value is $\log2/\lambda$ where $\lambda$ is the
top Lyapunov exponent of the i.i.d. product, which lies between $\log(5/2)$ and
$2\log(3+2\sqrt2)$ by Theorem 8.4.

## 9. Algorithms

We record the three computational procedures used to generate the numerical data above.

### 9.1 Node enumeration by seed box

To compute $N(H)$ exactly, one does *not* traverse the tree (which has exponentially many
nodes at each depth but only linearly many below $H$). Instead one uses the seed bijection:
enumerate all $(m,n)$ with $1\le n<m$ and $m^2+n^2\le H$, testing $\gcd(m,n)=1$ and $m+n$
odd. Complexity $O(H\log H)$ arithmetic operations (the $\log$ from the gcd), memory
$O(1)$. Correctness is Corollary 2.8.

### 9.2 Word reconstruction (unravelling)

Given an admissible seed, the descent of Theorem 2.6 recovers its unique Berggren word:
while $(m,n)\ne(2,1)$, if $m<2n$ emit $L$ and replace $(m,n)$ by $(n, 2n-m)$; else if
$m-2n<n$ emit $M$ and replace by $(n, m-2n)$; else emit $R$ and replace by $(m-2n, n)$.
Because the outermost letter is peeled off first, the letters in the order emitted are the
word. Each step strictly decreases $m+n$, so the
loop terminates; the number of steps is $O(\log m)$ along hyperbolic parts but can be
$O(m)$ along the parabolic spines — this asymmetry *is* the growth dichotomy, visible
algorithmically.

### 9.3 Locating the critical line numerically

To confirm Theorem 6.4 numerically, evaluate $|D(\sigma+it)| = |1-3\varepsilon^{-2(\sigma+it)}|$
on a grid. One finds $|D|\ge 1-3\varepsilon^{-2\sigma}>0$ for $\sigma>\sigma_0$ and
$|D|\ge 3\varepsilon^{-2\sigma}-1>0$ for $\sigma<\sigma_0$, with zeros occurring only when
both $\sigma=\sigma_0$ and $t\log\varepsilon\in\pi\mathbb{Z}$. Residues are checked by
evaluating $(s-s_k)Z_\varepsilon(s)$ on a small circle around $s_k$ and observing
convergence to $1/(2\log\varepsilon)$.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 What was conjectured and what is true

The moonshot conjecture was that the Berggren tree zeta $\sum_w c(w)^{-s}$ has abscissa
$\sigma_0=\log3/(2\log(1+\sqrt2))$, with a critical line there, and a prime-hypotenuse
counting law with error term controlled by its zeros. The truth splits cleanly:

- **False as stated.** The abscissa is $1$ (Theorem 3.1). The height grading is
  two-dimensional-lattice-like, and no exponential dynamics survives into it.
- **True after regrading.** The depth-graded silver zeta has *exactly* the predicted
  critical line, with an explicitly computable pole set, no zeros, an exact functional
  equation, and uniform simple residues (Theorems 6.4, 6.6, 6.7, 6.8).
- **True as a lower bound.** $\sigma_0$ *is* a genuine growth exponent for the counting
  function, $N(H)\ge\frac13(H/5)^{\sigma_0}$ (Theorem 5.4) — just not the maximal one.
- **True after pruning.** On the free binary subtree spanned by two expanding blocks, the
  abscissa falls strictly inside $(0,1)$ and, in particular, below the full tree's abscissa
  (Theorem 8.5).
- **Exactly the classical difficulty.** The prime hypotenuses are precisely the primes
  $\equiv1\pmod4$ (Theorem 7.2), so the prime-counting question on the tree is literally
  the classical question for the progression $1 \bmod 4$ — neither harder nor easier.

### 10.2 The structural principle

> **The silver unit governs the depth grading of the Berggren tree, not its height
> grading.**

Every surviving silver statement is a statement in the variable $|w|$; every failure was an
attempt to transport it to the variable $c(w)$. The obstruction is concrete and measurable:
the generator $A_2$ acts with expansion factor $\varepsilon^2$, but $A_1$ and $A_3$
generate spines along which the hypotenuse grows only like $k^2$. In dynamical language,
the semigroup generated by the three matrices is not uniformly hyperbolic on the relevant
cone; it has parabolic directions of positive measure in the word metric, and Dirichlet
series in the *height* variable are exquisitely sensitive to exactly those directions.

### 10.3 Comparison with classical zeta functions

| Feature | $\zeta(s)$ | Ihara zeta of a $(q+1)$-regular graph | $Z_\varepsilon$ | $Z_{\mathrm{tree}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Abscissa | $1$ | $\log q$-type | $\sigma_0=0.6232$ | $1$ |
| Continuation | entire × pole | rational in $u=q^{-s}$ | closed form on $\mathbb C$ | not established |
| Zeros | conjecturally on $\operatorname{Re}s=\tfrac12$ | Ramanujan condition | *none* | unknown |
| Poles | simple at $s=1$ | finitely many families | all on $\operatorname{Re}s=\sigma_0$, simple, equal residue | unknown |
| Symmetry | $s\leftrightarrow1-s$ | Riemann–Hurwitz-type | translation $s\mapsto s+i\pi/\log\varepsilon$ | — |

$Z_\varepsilon$ is closest in spirit to the Ihara zeta of a regular graph, where the
"Riemann Hypothesis" (the Ramanujan condition) also becomes a statement about a single
vertical line, and to the Ruelle zeta of an expanding map with constant expansion rate. The
reason all three are solvable is the same: their "primes" all have the same length, so the
zeta is a geometric series and its poles are the zeros of a single exponential equation.
Riemann's zeta is hard precisely because $\{\log p\}$ is irregular.

### 10.4 Limitations

$Z_{\mathrm{tree}}$ has not been shown to admit any meromorphic continuation past
$\operatorname{Re}s=1$; this is a genuinely open question and appears to require
understanding $\sum_{\mathcal{S}}(m^2+n^2)^{-s}$ as a sieved Epstein zeta function. The
subtree abscissa of Theorem 8.5 is bracketed, not determined. The Tauberian reading of
Theorem 6.8 in Section 6.1 is a heuristic interpretation of the residue, not a theorem
about $N(H)$.

## 11. Future Directions

### Direction 1 — A two-variable Berggren zeta

The tree carries two independent gradings: the depth $|w|$ (on which the silver unit acts)
and the height $c(w)$ (on which classical arithmetic acts). The natural object unifying
them is
$$Z(s,u) \;=\; \sum_{w} c(w)^{-s}\,u^{|w|},$$
a two-variable Dirichlet–power series. Its behaviour as $u\to1$ should interpolate between
the silver abscissa $\sigma_0$ (visible as $u\to 0$, where short words dominate and the
grading is depth-like) and the arithmetic abscissa $1$ (at $u=1$). The conjecture is that
$Z(s,u)$ has, for each fixed $u\in(0,1)$, an abscissa $\sigma(u)$ interpolating
monotonically from $\sigma_0$ to $1$, and that the transition point is governed by the
measure of the parabolic directions.

### Direction 2 — Determining the hyperbolic-subtree abscissa exactly

The bracket $[\log2/\log((3+2\sqrt2)^2),\ \log2/\log(5/2)]$ should collapse to the single
value $\log2/\lambda$, where $\lambda$ is the top Lyapunov exponent of the i.i.d. random
product of the block matrices $A_2^2$ and $A_2A_3$ with equal weights. Computing $\lambda$
— by Furstenberg's formula for the stationary measure on the projective line — would give
the exact abscissa of the purely hyperbolic Berggren zeta, and with it a genuine silver-type
critical line for an *arithmetically defined* subtree.

### Direction 3 — Continuation of the true tree zeta

$Z_{\mathrm{tree}}(s)=\sum_{\mathcal{S}}(m^2+n^2)^{-s}$ is the Epstein zeta function of the
form $x^2+y^2$ restricted to a coprimality-and-parity sieve. Removing the sieve gives
$\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_4)$ up to elementary factors; reinstating it should give an explicit
Euler-product correction and hence a meromorphic continuation to all of $\mathbb{C}$ with a
simple pole at $s=1$. Establishing this would answer (Q2) for the true tree zeta and place
the "Riemann Hypothesis for the Berggren tree" exactly where Section 7 predicts: on the
critical line of $\zeta(s)L(s,\chi_4)$.

### Direction 4 — Explicit formula for the depth-graded model

Theorem 6.8 gives an infinite family of poles with identical residues; a contour-shift
argument should convert this into an exact explicit formula for the depth-graded counting
function, of the form
$$\#\{\text{nodes of idealised size} \le X\} = \frac{X^{\sigma_0}}{2\sigma_0\log\varepsilon}
\Big(1 + \Omega\big(\log X/\log\varepsilon\big)\Big),$$
with $\Omega$ a bounded periodic function given by the sum over the non-central poles. This
is the exactly-solvable analogue of the explicit formula of Riemann–von Mangoldt, and it
makes precise the sense in which the critical line "controls the error term".

### Direction 5 — Other trees, other units

The construction is not special to $\sqrt2$. Any tree of solutions to a Pell-type equation
$x^2-Dy^2=\pm1$ generated by the fundamental unit $\varepsilon_D$ of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt D]$
should carry a depth-graded zeta $(1-b\,\varepsilon_D^{-2s})^{-1}$ with critical line
$\operatorname{Re}s = \log b/(2\log\varepsilon_D)$ and residues $1/(2\log\varepsilon_D)$. A
systematic study across real quadratic fields would relate the position of these critical
lines to the regulator, and hence to the class number formula.

## 12. Conclusion

The Berggren tree of primitive Pythagorean triples carries not one zeta function but two,
and the distance between them is the content of the subject. Graded by hypotenuse, it gives
a Dirichlet series with abscissa exactly $1$ whose prime nodes are exactly the primes
$p\equiv1\pmod4$ — a faithful copy of a classical, hard problem. Graded by depth, it gives
the silver Ihara zeta $(1-3(1+\sqrt2)^{-2s})^{-1}$, a meromorphic function on the whole
plane with no zeros, an exact translation symmetry of period $i\pi/\log(1+\sqrt2)$, and all
of its poles simple, equally spaced, of identical residue $1/(2\log(1+\sqrt2))$, on the
single critical line
$$\operatorname{Re}s \;=\; \frac{\log3}{2\log(1+\sqrt2)} \;=\; 0.623239\ldots$$

The transition between the two is caused by a growth dichotomy visible in a three-line
computation: one branch of the tree grows like $(3+2\sqrt2)^k$, and two grow like $k^2$.
Excise the slow branches and the silver law returns; keep them and the arithmetic of
$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ takes over. That, in miniature, is why a Riemann Hypothesis can be a
theorem for some zeta functions and a century-old mystery for others: the difference lies
in whether the lengths of the primes are all the same.

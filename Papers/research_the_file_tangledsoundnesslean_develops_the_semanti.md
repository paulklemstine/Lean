## Abstract

We study the operation that extends a proof system by adjoining its own internal consistency sentence. Proof systems are ordered by simulation: a system simulates another when it proves every formula provable in the latter. The basic reflection extension always simulates its base. Under consistency and the standard Gödel–Löb provability conditions, the reverse simulation is impossible. The key equivalence is elementary but decisive: the base system simulates its reflection extension exactly when it already proves its own consistency. Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem therefore turns consistency reflection into a strict edge in the simulation preorder.

We develop the order-theoretic consequences of this observation. No system above the reflected theory can participate with the base in a simulation cycle. The obstruction also rules out polynomial simulation from the reflected theory back into the base. Iterating reflection with fresh provability predicates produces a finite tower in which every later stage simulates every earlier stage, while no earlier consistent Gödel–Löb stage simulates any strictly later one. The distant-stage separation follows from a local argument at the first successor edge and transitivity. Standard converse-well-founded Kripke systems witness that the hypotheses are jointly realizable. We conclude with algorithms for finite tower analysis and prospects for transfinite reflection, ordinal ranks, quantitative lower bounds, and nonclassical semantics.

## 1. Introduction

A theory capable of representing its own proofs can formulate a sentence asserting that it does not prove contradiction. Adjoining this sentence as an axiom is a natural strengthening, often called a consistency-reflection step. The operation lies at the meeting point of incompleteness, modal provability logic, proof-system comparison, and well-founded hierarchy formation.

The guiding question is structural rather than merely syntactic. If $S$ is a proof system and $S+\operatorname{Con}(S)$ is obtained by adding its consistency sentence, can the original system reproduce all consequences of the extension? If it can, then reflection creates no genuine increase in proof-theoretic strength. If it cannot, reflection defines a strict ascent.

The answer is governed by second incompleteness. The extension trivially contains all proofs of the base. Conversely, the base can simulate the extension exactly when it proves the one new axiom, namely its own consistency. A consistent system satisfying the Gödel–Löb derivability conditions cannot do so. Reflection is therefore strict.

This reformulation of incompleteness has several advantages. First, it expresses the result in a presentation-independent preorder. Second, it immediately forbids cycles through reflection. Third, it transports the obstruction to stronger notions such as polynomial simulation. Finally, it iterates cleanly. If

$$
S_{n+1}=S_n+\operatorname{Con}(S_n),
$$

with a fresh provability predicate at each stage, then later theories simulate earlier ones. Under local consistency and Gödel–Löb assumptions at an earlier stage, no later theory can be simulated back by that stage.

The core argument is local. Given $m<n$, the later stage $S_n$ reaches down to $S_{m+1}$. Any alleged simulation from $S_m$ to $S_n$ would compose with this downward reach and collapse the strict edge $S_m<S_{m+1}$. Thus one application of second incompleteness separates arbitrarily distant finite stages.

## 2. Proof systems and their comparison

### 2.1 Formulas and proof systems

Fix a language of formulas $\mathcal F$ containing a contradiction constant $\bot$, implication, and negation. It also contains tagged provability operators $\Box_i$, where the tag $i\in\mathbb N$ distinguishes the provability predicate used at a particular reflection stage.

A **proof system** $S$ is represented extensionally by its provability relation. We write

$$
S\vdash \varphi
$$

when the formula $\varphi\in\mathcal F$ is provable in $S$. This abstract treatment suppresses the details of derivation trees while retaining exactly the information needed for simulation.

A proof system $S$ is **consistent** if

$$
S\nvdash\bot.
$$

For tag $i$, its object-language consistency sentence is

$$
\operatorname{Con}_i(S):=\neg\Box_i\bot.
$$

When the tag and represented system are clear, we abbreviate this by $\operatorname{Con}(S)$.

### 2.2 Union and singleton extensions

Given systems $S$ and $T$, their union $S\cup T$ is the system generated by taking proofs from either component. At the extensional level used here,

$$
(S\cup T)\vdash\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
S\vdash\varphi\ \text{or}\ T\vdash\varphi.
$$

For a formula $\chi$, the singleton system $\{\chi\}$ proves its distinguished formula. Thus $S\cup\{\chi\}$ formalizes the extension of $S$ by the new axiom $\chi$.

This disjunctive presentation is sufficient for the reflection results: every proof in the extension is classified as inherited from the base or supplied by the added axiom. More deductively closed presentations yield the same criterion provided simulation respects axiom extension and the base can replay derivations once it proves the added axiom.

### 2.3 Simulation

**Definition 2.1 (Simulation).** A proof system $T$ **simulates** a proof system $S$, written $T\succeq S$, if every theorem of $S$ is a theorem of $T$:

$$
T\succeq S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall\varphi\in\mathcal F,\; S\vdash\varphi\Rightarrow T\vdash\varphi.
$$

The direction of the symbol reflects strength: $T\succeq S$ means that $T$ is at least as strong as $S$.

**Lemma 2.2 (Reflexivity and transitivity).** Simulation is a preorder. For every $S$, one has $S\succeq S$. If $U\succeq T$ and $T\succeq S$, then $U\succeq S$.

**Proof sketch.** Reflexivity is the identity implication on provability. For transitivity, any theorem of $S$ is a theorem of $T$ by the second simulation and then a theorem of $U$ by the first. $\square$

Mutual simulation identifies systems of equal extensional strength. Strict strength is defined by

$$
T\succ S
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
T\succeq S\ \text{and}\ S\not\succeq T.
$$

### 2.4 Polynomial simulation

When proof objects carry a size measure, a **polynomial simulation** from $S$ to $T$ is a simulation whose translation increases proof size by at most a polynomial. We need only the following forgetful principle.

**Lemma 2.3 (Polynomial simulation implies simulation).** If $T$ polynomially simulates $S$, then $T\succeq S$.

**Proof sketch.** Erase the bound on the size of translated proofs. The translation still sends every $S$-proof to a $T$-proof of the same conclusion. $\square$

## 3. Provability logic and second incompleteness

The strictness theorem does not hold for arbitrary predicates named “provability.” It relies on the standard self-referential behavior captured by the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb conditions. For a fixed tag $i$, a system behaves as a **Gödel–Löb theory** when its provability predicate supports the following principles in the intended encoded form:

1. **Internalization:** if $S\vdash\varphi$, then $S\vdash\Box_i\varphi$;
2. **Distribution:** $S\vdash\Box_i(\varphi\to\psi)\to(\Box_i\varphi\to\Box_i\psi)$;
3. **Positive introspection:** $S\vdash\Box_i\varphi\to\Box_i\Box_i\varphi$.

Together with diagonal self-reference, these conditions yield Löb’s theorem.

**Theorem 3.1 (Löb).** If $S$ satisfies the Gödel–Löb conditions for $\Box_i$ and

$$
S\vdash\Box_i\varphi\to\varphi,
$$

then $S\vdash\varphi$.

**Proof sketch.** By the diagonal lemma, choose a sentence $\delta$ satisfying $S\vdash\delta\leftrightarrow(\Box_i\delta\to\varphi)$. Internalization, distribution, and positive introspection show that $S\vdash\Box_i\delta\to\Box_i\varphi$. Combining this with the assumed implication $\Box_i\varphi\to\varphi$ yields $S\vdash\delta$; internalization then gives $S\vdash\Box_i\delta$, and the fixed-point equivalence finally gives $S\vdash\varphi$. $\square$

The special case $\varphi=\bot$ gives second incompleteness. Since $\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$ is $\neg\Box_i\bot$, proving consistency supplies, classically, the relevant reflection implication for contradiction.

**Theorem 3.2 (Second incompleteness).** If $S$ is consistent and satisfies the Gödel–Löb conditions for $\Box_i$, then

$$
S\nvdash\operatorname{Con}_i(S).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Suppose $S\vdash\neg\Box_i\bot$. In classical logic this yields $S\vdash\Box_i\bot\to\bot$. Löb’s theorem with $\varphi=\bot$ then gives $S\vdash\bot$, contradicting consistency. $\square$

This is the only deep logical ingredient needed below. The remaining arguments are consequences of extension, simulation, and transitivity.

## 4. A single consistency-reflection step

### 4.1 Definition

**Definition 4.1 (Consistency-reflection extension).** For a tag $i$ and proof system $S$, define

$$
R_i(S):=S\cup\{\operatorname{Con}_i(S)\}.
$$

Thus the extension has all the theorems of $S$ together with the newly adjoined object-language assertion that $S$ does not prove contradiction.

### 4.2 Upward simulation

**Lemma 4.2 (Extension simulates its base).** For every $i$ and $S$,

$$
R_i(S)\succeq S.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Every theorem inherited from $S$ remains a theorem after adjoining a new axiom. $\square$

This direction requires neither consistency nor any derivability condition.

### 4.3 Exact criterion for downward simulation

**Theorem 4.3 (Reflection Criterion).** For every tag $i$ and proof system $S$,

$$
S\succeq R_i(S)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
S\vdash\operatorname{Con}_i(S).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Suppose first that $S\succeq R_i(S)$. The extension proves its newly adjoined axiom $\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$, so simulation implies that $S$ proves it as well.

Conversely, suppose $S\vdash\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$. Let $\varphi$ be any theorem of $R_i(S)$. By the definition of the union extension, either $\varphi$ is inherited from $S$, in which case it is already provable there, or it is the singleton axiom $\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$, which is provable by hypothesis. Hence every theorem of the extension is reproducible in $S$. $\square$

The theorem isolates the entire possibility of collapse in one sentence. No analysis of arbitrary translations is needed: the extension can return to the base precisely when the base already possesses the reflected consistency assertion.

### 4.4 Strictness

**Theorem 4.4 (Strict Consistency Reflection).** Let $S$ be consistent and satisfy the Gödel–Löb conditions for tag $i$. Then

$$
R_i(S)\succ S.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
R_i(S)\succeq S
\quad\text{and}\quad
S\not\succeq R_i(S).
$$

**Proof sketch.** The first relation is Lemma 4.2. If the reverse simulation held, Theorem 4.3 would imply $S\vdash\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$, contradicting Theorem 3.2. $\square$

The theorem makes clear that both hypotheses are substantive. If $S$ is inconsistent, classical explosion may allow it to prove every formula, including its consistency sentence, so the reflection extension can collapse. If the internal provability predicate lacks the derivability and diagonal properties, second incompleteness need not apply.

## 5. Acyclicity and quantitative non-collapse

### 5.1 No cycle through reflection

Strictness at one edge constrains every theory above it.

**Theorem 5.1 (No Simulation Cycle Through Reflection).** Let $S$ be consistent and satisfy the Gödel–Löb conditions for tag $i$. If

$$
T\succeq R_i(S),
$$

then

$$
S\not\succeq T.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Assume for contradiction that $S\succeq T$. Transitivity with $T\succeq R_i(S)$ gives $S\succeq R_i(S)$, contradicting Theorem 4.4. $\square$

Thus a consistency-reflection edge cannot lie on a directed simulation cycle. This formulation is stronger than merely saying that the two adjacent systems are unequal: it excludes return paths through arbitrary intermediate systems.

### 5.2 No polynomial collapse

**Theorem 5.2 (No Polynomial Elimination of Sound Reflection).** Let $S$ be consistent and satisfy the Gödel–Löb conditions for tag $i$. Then $S$ does not polynomially simulate $R_i(S)$.

**Proof sketch.** Any polynomial simulation would induce an ordinary simulation by Lemma 2.3. The latter is excluded by Theorem 4.4. $\square$

This theorem is a qualitative impossibility expressed in a quantitative framework. It does not claim a particular exponential or superpolynomial lower bound for a chosen proof family. Rather, it says that a global polynomial translation cannot exist because even unrestricted simulation fails. Quantitative approximation questions remain meaningful when reflection is eliminated only on bounded fragments or with help from a stronger metatheory.

## 6. Concrete semantic non-vacuity

The hypotheses of strict reflection must be jointly satisfiable for the theorem to describe a genuine phenomenon. A standard source of examples comes from Kripke semantics for Gödel–Löb logic.

A Kripke frame $(W,\prec)$ interprets $\Box\varphi$ at a world $w$ as the assertion that $\varphi$ holds at every world accessible from $w$. For Gödel–Löb logic, the relevant frames are transitive and converse well-founded: there is no infinite ascending accessibility chain in the direction inspected by provability. The associated standard theory consists of formulas valid throughout such a frame.

**Theorem 6.1 (Standard-Model Witness).** For a standard consistent converse-well-founded Gödel–Löb theory $S_{\mathrm{std}}$ and every tag $i$,

$$
R_i(S_{\mathrm{std}})\succ S_{\mathrm{std}}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The standard theory is consistent and validates the Gödel–Löb principles for each tagged provability modality. Theorem 4.4 applies directly. $\square$

This witness separates the logical content of the result from vacuity. Strictness is realized by familiar semantic structures, not inferred from incompatible assumptions.

## 7. Finite reflection towers

### 7.1 Construction

Fix a base system $S$. Define its finite reflection tower recursively by

$$
S_0:=S,
$$

and

$$
S_{n+1}:=R_n(S_n)=S_n\cup\{\operatorname{Con}_n(S_n)\}.
$$

The index $n$ serves as a fresh provability tag. This freshness is essential. Repeatedly adjoining one fixed consistency sentence would stabilize after the first stage, because adding an axiom already present creates no further extension. The recursive construction instead makes stage $n+1$ assert the consistency of the entire preceding stage.

### 7.2 Stepwise and global monotonicity

**Lemma 7.1 (Successor Simulation).** For every $n$,

$$
S_{n+1}\succeq S_n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** This is Lemma 4.2 applied to $S_n$ with tag $n$. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Tower Monotonicity).** If $m\le n$, then

$$
S_n\succeq S_m.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Induct on the difference between $n$ and $m$. Equality gives reflexivity. At a successor stage, compose $S_{k+1}\succeq S_k$ with the induction hypothesis using transitivity. $\square$

Thus every later stage can reproduce all theorems of every earlier stage.

### 7.3 Strict separation of arbitrary stages

**Theorem 7.3 (Finite Reflection Tower Strictness).** Let $m<n$. Assume that $S_m$ is consistent and satisfies the Gödel–Löb conditions for its tag $m$. Then

$$
S_n\succeq S_m
\quad\text{and}\quad
S_m\not\succeq S_n.
$$

Hence $S_n\succ S_m$.

**Proof sketch.** Tower monotonicity gives $S_n\succeq S_m$. Since $m<n$, one also has $m+1\le n$, so monotonicity gives $S_n\succeq S_{m+1}$. But $S_{m+1}=R_m(S_m)$. If $S_m\succeq S_n$, transitivity would imply $S_m\succeq R_m(S_m)$, violating strict consistency reflection at stage $m$. $\square$

The proof uses only the local assumptions at the lower stage. It does not require a separate second-incompleteness argument at every intermediate level. This feature is important for generalization: distant separation is reduced to one strict successor edge plus monotonicity.

**Corollary 7.4 (Pairwise strict hierarchy under uniform hypotheses).** If every $S_m$ is consistent and satisfies the Gödel–Löb conditions for tag $m$, then for all $m<n$,

$$
S_n\succ S_m.
$$

Consequently, the tower contains no repeated stage up to mutual simulation and no directed cycle that traverses a reflection edge.

## 8. Algorithms and computational illustrations

The theorems are logical rather than numerical, but finite models of the simulation pattern make their order-theoretic content visible.

### 8.1 Tower construction algorithm

Represent stage $n$ by the set of labels for the base axioms together with the first $n$ reflected consistency axioms. To construct the first $N+1$ stages:

1. initialize $A_0$ with the base axiom labels;
2. for each $n=0,\dots,N-1$, copy $A_n$ and insert a fresh label $c_n$ representing $\operatorname{Con}_n(S_n)$;
3. return $A_0,\dots,A_N$.

With immutable set copying, the total storage and running time are $O(N^2+N|A_0|)$. With persistent sets, construction can share earlier stages and use $O(N)$ additional nodes.

The set model does not establish consistency or the Gödel–Löb conditions. It illustrates the extension relation that, once those logical hypotheses are supplied, becomes strict simulation.

### 8.2 Simulation matrix algorithm

For a tower of height $N$, define the theorem-predicted matrix $M$ by

$$
M_{n,m}=1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n\ge m.
$$

The entry records that stage $n$ simulates stage $m$. Under the strictness hypotheses at every lower stage, entries below the diagonal are zero: earlier stages do not simulate later stages. Filling the full matrix costs $O(N^2)$ time and space. A query $M_{n,m}$ can be answered in constant time by comparing indices.

### 8.3 Cycle-detection algorithm

A finite directed graph of claimed simulations can be audited against strict reflection edges. For each certified edge $S_m\to S_{m+1}$ in the strength direction, search for a path from $S_m$ to any system already known to simulate $S_{m+1}$. Equivalently, test whether adding a proposed reverse relation creates a strongly connected component containing a strict edge. Standard depth-first search takes $O(V+E)$ per query; computing all strongly connected components also takes $O(V+E)$. Any component containing a certified strict edge exposes an incompatible collection of claims.

## 9. Interpretation and applications

### 9.1 Reflection as an order-theoretic invariant

Second incompleteness is usually stated as the unprovability of one sentence. The simulation formulation turns it into an ordering theorem. This shift permits composition: once a reflection edge is strict, every system above the upper endpoint is separated from the lower endpoint. The resulting acyclicity is stable under changes of presentation that preserve provability.

### 9.2 Layered verification and trust

The formal pattern has an analogy with layered assurance. A base process performs deductions; a stronger layer certifies a global property of the lower layer. The certification cannot, under the theorem’s hypotheses, be completely internalized without increasing strength. This does not imply that ordinary software or institutions obey Gödel’s theorem, but it clarifies why mathematically complete self-certification is structurally different from external certification.

### 9.3 Proof complexity

The failure of polynomial simulation locates reflection within proof complexity. Since ordinary simulation already fails, unrestricted elimination of the consistency axiom is impossible. More refined questions can restrict formula depth, proof length, or fragments of the reflected theory. Such restrictions may permit elimination while still forcing large proof growth. The present theorem supplies the limiting obstruction against which those finite approximations should be measured.

### 9.4 Modal semantics and well-founded height

Gödel–Löb logic is characterized by converse-well-founded behavior in Kripke semantics, while reflection towers form an acyclic hierarchy in the simulation preorder. These are two manifestations of well-foundedness: semantic accessibility prevents infinite self-support, and proof-theoretic strictness prevents return across a consistency edge. A natural objective is to identify the height of a reflection progression with an ordinal rank of a canonical provability frame.

## 10. Limitations

Three boundaries should be explicit.

First, consistency is indispensable. An inconsistent base proves every formula in an explosive logic and may simulate its reflection extension. Strictness describes soundly nontrivial systems, not arbitrary theorem sets.

Second, the Gödel–Löb conditions and diagonal self-reference are indispensable to the incompleteness step. A weak language unable to encode its proofs may prove a sentence externally interpreted as its consistency without contradiction.

Third, the finite set demonstrations are visual models of inclusion and ordering, not decision procedures for mathematical consistency. Determining whether a sufficiently expressive recursively axiomatized theory is consistent is not reduced to checking a finite list of labels.

The polynomial result is likewise global and qualitative. It rules out polynomial simulation but does not by itself produce explicit proof-size lower bounds for bounded approximations.

## 11. Future work

The first extension is transfinite. For ordinal-indexed theories, define successor stages by adjoining the preceding stage’s consistency sentence and limit stages by directed union. The finite proof suggests that strictness at arbitrary successor stages requires only local second incompleteness and monotonicity from later stages. The main new work lies in controlling directed unions, tags, consistency, and derivability conditions at limits.

Second, any well-founded strict-reflection relation admits an ordinal-valued rank. For canonical progressions, one may ask whether this rank classifies stages up to mutual simulation. Such a result would connect logical strength directly to order-theoretic height.

Third, bounded reflection elimination should be investigated quantitatively. Although a global translation is impossible, finite-depth or finite-length fragments may be eliminable with substantial overhead. Superpolynomial lower bounds on natural proof families would provide a quantitative shadow of the absolute non-collapse theorem.

Fourth, Kripke frames may characterize reflection height. Finite towers suggest comparison with maximal converse-well-founded chain length, while transfinite towers suggest agreement with the ordinal rank of canonical frames.

Finally, nonclassical semantics may alter the boundary. Three-valued or paracomplete fixed-point semantics can assign gaps to self-referential sentences rather than forcing contradiction. Determining which reflection strictness results survive would isolate the contribution of classical negation and explosion.

## 12. Conclusion

Consistency reflection converts self-reference into hierarchy. The extension $R_i(S)=S+\operatorname{Con}_i(S)$ always simulates $S$. The reverse simulation holds exactly when $S$ proves its own consistency. For a consistent Gödel–Löb theory, second incompleteness excludes that possibility, so reflection is strict.

From this single edge follow acyclicity, failure of polynomial collapse, and strict separation throughout finite towers. Every later stage simulates every earlier one; no earlier stage satisfying the local hypotheses simulates a later stage. The proof of distant separation is economical: monotonicity carries the later stage down to the first reflection above the lower one, where second incompleteness blocks the return.

The resulting picture is a directed architecture of mathematical strength. Self-assurance cannot be folded harmlessly into the system it certifies, but it can be placed one level higher. Iterating that move produces a well-ordered route out of the self-referential tangle.

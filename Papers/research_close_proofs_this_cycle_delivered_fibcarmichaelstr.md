# The Apparition–Order Bridge: A Local-to-Global Reduction of Entry Points to Multiplicative Order

## Abstract

For a sequence of natural numbers `a : ℕ → ℕ`, the *entry point* (or *rank
of apparition*) of a prime `p` is the least positive index `k` with
`p ∣ a(k)`. As classically defined, it is a *global* invariant: a search
over all indices. We isolate the structural property that organizes entry
points — **strong divisibility**, `a(gcd(m,n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))` — and
prove that for any strong divisibility sequence the *apparition support*
`{n > 0 : p ∣ a(n)}` is a principal arithmetic progression generated by the
entry point. We then establish the central result, the **Apparition–Order
Bridge**: for the Mersenne / Bang–Zsygmondy family `a(n) = bⁿ − 1`, the
entry point of a prime `p ∤ b` equals the multiplicative order of `b` in the
residue field `ℤ/pℤ`. This collapses the global entry-point search into a
single local computation in the stalk at `p`. Two corollaries follow
immediately: the *stalk reduction* `p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ bⁿ ≡ 1 (mod p)`, and
*Fermat descent*, that the entry point divides `p − 1`. The Fibonacci
sequence, being a strong divisibility sequence, inherits the periodicity law
verbatim. All results have been formally verified in Lean 4 / Mathlib and
are free of unproven assumptions (axioms used: `propext`, `Classical.choice`,
`Quot.sound`).

**Keywords:** rank of apparition, entry point, strong divisibility
sequence, multiplicative order, Mersenne numbers, Fibonacci numbers,
local-to-global, Bang–Zsygmondy, Fermat's little theorem.

---

## 1. Introduction

A recurring phenomenon in elementary number theory is that the set of
indices at which a prime divides a recurrence-type sequence is highly
structured. For the Fibonacci numbers `Fₙ`, a prime `p` divides `Fₙ`
precisely when `n` is a multiple of a single number — the *rank of
apparition* of `p`. For Mersenne-type numbers `bⁿ − 1`, the analogous
"period" of divisibility is the multiplicative order of `b` modulo `p`.
These two facts are usually presented as separate folklore. This paper
identifies the common structural cause and proves that, for `bⁿ − 1`, the
two notions of period are *literally equal*.

The unifying property is **strong divisibility**: a sequence `a` satisfies
`a(gcd(m,n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))` for all indices `m, n`. Both Fibonacci and
`bⁿ − 1` are strong divisibility sequences. We show that strong
divisibility alone forces the apparition support to be a principal
arithmetic progression (Theorem 4.2), and that for `bⁿ − 1` the generator of
that progression — the entry point — coincides with a finite group-theoretic
invariant, the multiplicative order (Theorem 5.4).

The conceptual frame is **local-to-global**. Assign to each prime `p` a
"stalk" capturing the divisibility behavior of the sequence at `p`. The
periodicity law says each stalk is a single arithmetic progression; the
Apparition–Order Bridge identifies the stalk over `p` for the family
`bⁿ − 1` with the cyclic subgroup `⟨b⟩ ≤ (ℤ/pℤ)ˣ`. The global divisibility
data is thereby reconstructed from finitely-described local groups.

### 1.1 Contributions

1. A clean axiomatization of the entry-point calculus over arbitrary strong
   divisibility sequences (Section 3), including the periodicity criterion
   `p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n` (Theorem 4.1).
2. The local-to-global gluing theorem: the apparition support equals the
   principal progression generated by the entry point (Theorem 4.2).
3. The stalk reduction for `bⁿ − 1`: `p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ (b mod p)ⁿ = 1`
   (Theorem 5.1).
4. The Apparition–Order Bridge: `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = ord_p(b)` for
   `p ∤ b` (Theorem 5.4).
5. Fermat descent: `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣ p − 1` (Theorem 5.5).
6. The Fibonacci specialization of the gluing theorem (Theorem 6.1).

## 2. Preliminaries and notation

We work in `ℕ = {0, 1, 2, …}` with truncated subtraction (`x − y = 0` when
`y > x`). For a prime `p`, `ℤ/pℤ` denotes the field of residues, and
`(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ` its multiplicative group of units, of order `p − 1`. For
`b` coprime to `p`, the image of `b` in `ℤ/pℤ` is a unit, and its
**multiplicative order** `ord_p(b)` is the least positive `k` with
`bᵏ ≡ 1 (mod p)`.

We write `gcd` for the greatest common divisor and `∣` for divisibility.
"Apparition support of `p`" denotes the set `{n > 0 : p ∣ a(n)}`.

## 3. Strong divisibility sequences and the entry point

### 3.1 Definition

> **Definition 3.1 (Strong divisibility sequence).** A function
> `a : ℕ → ℕ` is a *strong divisibility sequence* if
> `a(gcd(m, n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))` for all `m, n ∈ ℕ`.

In the formalization this is the predicate `StrongDiv a`.

> **Definition 3.2 (Entry point / rank of apparition).** For a sequence `a`
> and a prime `p`, the *entry point* `entryPoint(a, p)` is the least `k > 0`
> with `p ∣ a(k)` if such a `k` exists, and `0` otherwise.

Formally, `entryPoint a p = if h : ∃ k, 0 < k ∧ p ∣ a k then Nat.find h
else 0`, so its three defining properties — positivity, witness, and
minimality — are read directly off `Nat.find`.

### 3.2 Two structural lemmas

> **Lemma 3.3 (Index divisibility transfers).** If `a` is a strong
> divisibility sequence and `m ∣ n`, then `a(m) ∣ a(n)`.

*Proof.* Since `m ∣ n`, `gcd(m, n) = m`. The strong divisibility identity
gives `a(m) = a(gcd(m,n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))`, and a gcd divides its second
argument, so `a(m) ∣ a(n)`. ∎

> **Lemma 3.4 (Common divisor descends to the gcd index).** If `a` is a
> strong divisibility sequence and a prime `p` divides both `a(m)` and
> `a(n)`, then `p ∣ a(gcd(m, n))`.

*Proof.* By the identity `a(gcd(m,n)) = gcd(a(m), a(n))`. A number dividing
both `a(m)` and `a(n)` divides their gcd, hence divides `a(gcd(m,n))`. ∎

These two lemmas are the only consequences of strong divisibility used in
the periodicity theorem.

## 4. The periodicity law and local-to-global gluing

### 4.1 Periodicity criterion

> **Theorem 4.1 (Entry-point periodicity).** Let `a` be a strong
> divisibility sequence and `p` a prime that appears in `a` (i.e. there
> exists `k > 0` with `p ∣ a(k)`). Then for all `n`,
> `p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n`.

*Proof.* Write `e = entryPoint(a, p)`; by hypothesis `e > 0` and `p ∣ a(e)`.

(⇐) If `e ∣ n`, then by Lemma 3.3 `a(e) ∣ a(n)`, and since `p ∣ a(e)` we get
`p ∣ a(n)`.

(⇒) Suppose `p ∣ a(n)` and, for contradiction, `e ∤ n`. Then
`gcd(e, n) ∣ e` with `gcd(e, n) ≠ e` (else `e ∣ n`), so
`gcd(e, n) < e`. Also `gcd(e, n) > 0` because `e > 0`. By Lemma 3.4,
`p ∣ a(gcd(e, n))`. This exhibits a positive index `gcd(e,n) < e` at which
`p` divides `a`, contradicting the minimality of the entry point `e`. Hence
`e ∣ n`. ∎

This is the formal theorem `dvd_iff_entryPoint_dvd`.

### 4.2 The support is a principal progression

> **Theorem 4.2 (Local-to-global gluing).** Under the hypotheses of
> Theorem 4.1, the apparition support of `p` equals the principal arithmetic
> progression generated by the entry point:
> `{n : 0 < n ∧ p ∣ a(n)} = {n : 0 < n ∧ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n}`.

*Proof.* Pointwise application of Theorem 4.1: for each `n > 0`,
`p ∣ a(n) ⟺ entryPoint(a, p) ∣ n`, so the two defining predicates of the
sets agree. ∎

This is the formal theorem `support_eq_multiples`. Conceptually it says: the
"support sheaf" `n ↦ {p : p ∣ a(n)}` over the index semigroup `(ℕ, +)` has
principal global sections at each prime, generated by the entry point.

## 5. The Apparition–Order Bridge for `bⁿ − 1`

We now specialize to the Mersenne / Bang–Zsygmondy family `a(n) = bⁿ − 1`,
which is a strong divisibility sequence (a classical fact;
`gcd(bᵐ − 1, bⁿ − 1) = b^{gcd(m,n)} − 1`).

### 5.1 The stalk reduction

> **Theorem 5.1 (Stalk reduction).** Let `p` be prime and `b ≥ 1`. Then
> `p ∣ bⁿ − 1 ⟺ (b mod p)ⁿ = 1` in `ℤ/pℤ`.

*Proof.* Since `b ≥ 1`, also `bⁿ ≥ 1`, so truncated subtraction is exact and
`p ∣ bⁿ − 1` is equivalent to the congruence `bⁿ ≡ 1 (mod p)`
(`Nat.modEq_iff_dvd'`). Casting the congruence into `ℤ/pℤ`
(`ZMod.natCast_eq_natCast_iff`) and pushing the cast through the power yields
`(b mod p)ⁿ = 1`. ∎

This is the formal theorem `pow_sub_one_dvd_iff_orderOf`. It is the precise
sense in which divisibility of the infinite integer `bⁿ − 1` is detected
*locally* in the finite stalk `ℤ/pℤ`.

### 5.2 Existence of an apparition index

> **Lemma 5.2.** Let `p` be prime with `p ∤ b`. Then there exists `k > 0`
> with `p ∣ bᵏ − 1`; indeed `k = p − 1` works.

*Proof.* By Fermat's little theorem, `(b mod p)^{p−1} = 1` in `ℤ/pℤ` since
`b mod p` is a unit (`ZMod.pow_card_sub_one_eq_one`). Apply Theorem 5.1 with
`n = p − 1`; note `p − 1 > 0` because `p ≥ 2`. ∎

Thus for `p ∤ b` the entry point exists, is positive, and `ord_p(b)` is
well-defined and positive.

### 5.3 Order is the least apparition index

> **Lemma 5.3.** For `p ∤ b`, `ord_p(b)` is the least positive `n` with
> `(b mod p)ⁿ = 1`.

*Proof.* This is the defining property of multiplicative order in a finite
group (`orderOf` minimality / `orderOf_dvd_iff_pow_eq_one` and positivity of
order for elements of finite groups). ∎

### 5.4 The bridge

> **Theorem 5.4 (Apparition–Order Bridge).** Let `p` be prime with `p ∤ b`.
> Then `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) = ord_p(b)`.

*Proof.* Both numbers are the least positive `n` satisfying the same
predicate. By Theorem 5.1, "`p ∣ bⁿ − 1`" and "`(b mod p)ⁿ = 1`" are the
same predicate on positive `n`. The entry point is the least positive `n`
making the first true (Definition 3.2); the order is the least positive `n`
making the second true (Lemma 5.3). Equality of the predicates forces
equality of their least positive witnesses. (Existence on both sides is
Lemma 5.2.) ∎

This is the formal theorem `mersenne_entryPoint_eq_orderOf`. It is the
local-to-global reduction in its sharpest form: a global `Nat.find` over all
indices equals a single finite group-order computation in the stalk
`(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ`.

### 5.5 Fermat descent

> **Theorem 5.5 (Fermat descent).** For `p` prime with `p ∤ b`,
> `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣ p − 1`.

*Proof.* By Theorem 5.4 the entry point equals `ord_p(b)`. The order of any
element of the finite group `(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ` divides the group's cardinality
`|（ℤ/pℤ)ˣ| = p − 1` (Lagrange's theorem, `orderOf_dvd_card`). ∎

This is the formal theorem `mersenne_entryPoint_dvd_sub_one`. It recovers,
as a structural corollary, the classical constraint that the period of `p`
in `bⁿ − 1` divides `p − 1`.

## 6. The Fibonacci specialization

The Fibonacci sequence satisfies `gcd(Fₘ, Fₙ) = F₍gcd(m,n)₎`
(`Nat.fib_gcd`), i.e. it is a strong divisibility sequence. Hence
Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 apply directly.

> **Theorem 6.1 (Fibonacci apparition).** For a prime `p` appearing in the
> Fibonacci sequence, the apparition support equals the principal progression
> generated by the Fibonacci entry point:
> `{n : 0 < n ∧ p ∣ Fₙ} = {n : 0 < n ∧ entryPoint(F, p) ∣ n}`,
> equivalently `p ∣ Fₙ ⟺ entryPoint(F, p) ∣ n`.

*Proof.* `Nat.fib` is a strong divisibility sequence by `Nat.fib_gcd`. Apply
Theorem 4.2 (and Theorem 4.1) with `a = Nat.fib`. ∎

This is the formal theorem `fib_support_eq_multiples`, tying the bridge back
to the catalog's Carmichael primitive-divisor program: e.g. the prime 11 has
Fibonacci entry point 10, so 11 divides `Fₙ` exactly when `10 ∣ n`.

## 7. Algorithms

The bridge converts an *a priori unbounded* search into a *bounded* finite
computation.

**Naive entry point (global).** Iterate `k = 1, 2, 3, …` testing
`p ∣ a(k)`; return the first success. For `bⁿ − 1` this is correct but the
terms grow exponentially, so direct evaluation is infeasible for large `k`.

**Bridge entry point (local).** To compute `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p)` for
`p ∤ b`: compute `ord_p(b)` by reducing `b` modulo `p` and multiplying
modulo `p` until the running product returns to `1`. By Fermat descent
(Theorem 5.5) the answer divides `p − 1`, so one may instead factor `p − 1`
and test its divisors, giving an `O((log p)² + cost of factoring p − 1)`
method. Crucially, all arithmetic stays bounded by `p`, never touching the
astronomically large integer `bᵏ − 1`.

Pseudocode (divisor-search form):

```
function entry_point(b, p):           # p prime, p ∤ b
    n ← p − 1                          # Fermat descent: answer divides n
    for d in sorted(divisors(n)):     # ascending
        if pow_mod(b, d, p) == 1:
            return d
    return n                          # unreachable; guaranteed by Fermat
```

## 8. Applications

- **Primality and pseudoprimality.** The condition `entryPoint(bⁿ − 1, p) ∣
  p − 1` underlies Fermat / Lucas-style primality tests; deviations detect
  compositeness. The Fibonacci specialization is the entry point of the
  theory of Fibonacci pseudoprimes and Carmichael-type phenomena.
- **Bang–Zsygmondy theory.** Knowing the apparition support is a single
  progression is the structural input for primitive-divisor theorems: a
  prime is a *primitive* divisor of `bⁿ − 1` exactly when its entry point is
  `n` itself.
- **Cryptographic order computations.** The reduction of a global invariant
  to a multiplicative order is exactly the computation underlying
  discrete-log and group-order subroutines.

## 9. Discussion: the local-to-global / sheaf perspective

Regard the assignment `n ↦ {p : p ∣ a(n)}` as a presheaf-like object over
the index semigroup `(ℕ, +)`. Theorem 4.2 says its sections at each prime
are *principal* — generated by one element, the entry point — which is the
strongest possible regularity. The Apparition–Order Bridge then *identifies
the stalk* at `p` for the family `bⁿ − 1` with the concrete finite cyclic
group `⟨b⟩ ≤ (ℤ/pℤ)ˣ`: the global divisibility datum is reconstructed,
prime by prime, from a finite local group. This is the local-to-global
program in miniature, and it is fully transparent because the stalk is as
simple as a cyclic group can be.

The rank-one character of `bⁿ − 1` (the stalk is a subgroup of the
*one-dimensional* unit group) is what makes the bridge so clean. The
Fibonacci case is genuinely rank-two — its companion is the matrix
`Q = [[1,1],[1,0]]` — so while Theorem 6.1 transports the *periodicity*
half of the story, the *order* half requires a higher-dimensional stalk
(see Section 10).

## 10. Future work

**Direction 1 — The Fibonacci matrix stalk.** *Conjecture:* for a prime
`p ∤ 5`, `entryPoint(F, p)` equals the multiplicative order of the Fibonacci
companion matrix `Q = [[1,1],[1,0]]` in `GL₂(ℤ/pℤ)` — equivalently the order
of the golden-ratio image in `(ℤ/pℤ)[x]/(x² − x − 1)`. Since `Fₙ` is the
(1,2)-entry of `Qⁿ`, the global Fibonacci entry point should become the
order of a single matrix in a finite group, with the stalk at `p` upgraded
from `(ℤ/pℤ)ˣ` to `GL₂(ℤ/pℤ)`. The abstract machinery of Section 4
(`dvd_iff_entryPoint_dvd`, `support_eq_multiples`) already applies; only the
rank-two stalk identification is open.

**Direction 2 — General Lucas sequences.** Extend the bridge to arbitrary
Lucas sequences `U_n(P, Q)`, whose stalks are governed by the splitting of
`x² − Px + Q` modulo `p` (split, inert, or ramified cases), unifying the
Mersenne (rank-one) and Fibonacci (rank-two) instances.

**Direction 3 — Quantitative apparition.** Combine the bridge with
distribution results on multiplicative orders (Artin-type conjectures) to
study the statistics of entry points across primes.

**Direction 4 — Carmichael synthesis.** Feed the principal-progression
structure into the catalog's Carmichael / Korselt program to obtain
formalized criteria for Fibonacci-Carmichael composites.

## 11. Conclusion

We have shown that the entry point of a prime in a strong divisibility
sequence governs the entire apparition pattern (a principal arithmetic
progression), and that for the family `bⁿ − 1` this global invariant
*equals* the multiplicative order of `b` modulo `p` — a finite, local,
group-theoretic quantity — with Fermat descent as an immediate corollary.
The Fibonacci case inherits the periodicity law, and points toward a
rank-two sequel. Every result is machine-verified, resting only on the
standard foundational axioms.

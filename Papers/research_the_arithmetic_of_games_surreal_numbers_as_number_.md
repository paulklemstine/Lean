## Abstract

Surreal numbers are recursively generated from cuts between previously constructed numbers, and their birthdays measure the stages at which representatives become available. This paper isolates the canonical dyadic fragment of that hierarchy. We prove that the canonical surreal representative of $2^{-n}$ has birthday exactly $n+1$, that these representatives are positive and strictly decreasing, and that a multiple $m2^{-n}$ vanishes only for $m=0$. We then establish that the canonical map from the localization $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ into the surreal numbers is injective. As a consequence, equality of two canonical dyadic surreal values is equivalent to the integer cross-product identity

$$
m_1 2^{n_2}=m_2 2^{n_1}.
$$

Finally, we prove that $3$ has no inverse in $\mathbb Z[1/2]$. Thus the finite-birthday layer is naturally associated with the dyadic subring, not a subfield. The results clarify the distinction among birthday cutoffs, algebraic closures, and fields generated by birthday-bounded classes, while providing exact algorithms for equality, normalization, arithmetic, and birthday computation for canonical inverse powers of two.

## 1. Introduction

The surreal numbers arise from a recursive construction in which a new number is specified by previously available left and right options. A form

$$
x=\{L\mid R\}
$$

is valid when every $\ell\in L$ is less than every $r\in R$; its value is the simplest surreal number strictly between all left and right options. Starting from the empty cut, this process creates $0$, then the integers $\pm1$, then fractions such as $1/2$, and eventually a proper class encompassing the real numbers, ordinals, infinitesimals, and infinitely large quantities.

The recursive stages are measured by **birthdays**. The birthday of a form is one greater than the supremum of the birthdays of its options, with the empty form assigned birthday $0$. Birthday therefore records constructive depth rather than ordinary magnitude. An enormous integer and a tiny positive fraction can both have finite birthdays, but their exact dates reflect different genealogies.

The finite stages have long suggested a binary pattern. Repeatedly choosing the simplest value between $0$ and a previously created positive value yields

$$
1,\frac12,\frac14,\frac18,\ldots.
$$

More generally, finite binary subdivision produces numbers $m/2^n$. These are the dyadic rationals, algebraically described as the localization $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ obtained from $\mathbb Z$ by inverting $2$.

This paper develops the exact arithmetic facts required to identify the canonical dyadic values faithfully inside the surreal numbers. The principal issue is injectivity: distinct elements of $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ must not collapse to the same surreal value. Once injectivity is known, equality reduces to cross multiplication, exactly as for ordinary fractions.

A second objective is corrective. The dyadic rationals are sometimes described informally as a field, or the finite-birthday surreals as a subfield. This is false. Although $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ is a commutative ordered ring, it does not contain $1/3$. We give an elementary obstruction proving that $3$ has no multiplicative inverse. Therefore finite birthdays, ring generation, and field generation must be distinguished.

## 2. Preliminaries

### 2.1. Surreal forms and birthdays

A **surreal form** is a pair $\{L\mid R\}$ of sets of earlier forms satisfying $\ell<r$ for every $\ell\in L$ and $r\in R$. Two different forms may represent the same surreal number. The birthday belongs to a form and is recursively defined by

$$
b\bigl(\{L\mid R\}\bigr)
=
\sup\{b(y)+1:y\in L\cup R\}.
$$

For finite game trees, this is simply one plus the maximum birthday among all options. In particular,

$$
0=\{\mid\},\qquad b(0)=0,
$$

and

$$
1=\{0\mid\},\qquad b(1)=1.
$$

The distinction between a form and its value is important. Our birthday statements concern canonical recursively defined forms, while arithmetic statements concern their surreal values.

### 2.2. Canonical inverse powers of two

Define canonical forms $h_n$ recursively by

$$
h_0=1,
\qquad
h_{n+1}=\{0\mid h_n\}.
$$

The value of $h_n$ is $2^{-n}$. Thus

$$
h_0=1,
\quad h_1=\frac12,
\quad h_2=\frac14,
\quad h_3=\frac18.
$$

The cut description expresses $h_{n+1}$ as the simplest surreal number strictly between $0$ and $h_n$.

### 2.3. Dyadic rationals as a localization

The **dyadic rationals** form the ring

$$
D=\mathbb Z[1/2]
 =\left\{\frac{m}{2^n}:m\in\mathbb Z,\ n\in\mathbb N\right\}.
$$

Two presentations represent the same element precisely when

$$
\frac{m_1}{2^{n_1}}=\frac{m_2}{2^{n_2}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
m_1 2^{n_2}=m_2 2^{n_1}.
$$

Addition and multiplication are given by

$$
\frac{a}{2^m}+\frac{b}{2^n}
=
\frac{a2^n+b2^m}{2^{m+n}},
$$

$$
\frac{a}{2^m}\frac{b}{2^n}
=
\frac{ab}{2^{m+n}}.
$$

A more economical addition algorithm first raises both values only to the common exponent $k=\max(m,n)$:

$$
\frac{a}{2^m}+\frac{b}{2^n}
=
\frac{a2^{k-m}+b2^{k-n}}{2^k}.
$$

The canonical map into the surreal numbers is

$$
\iota:D\longrightarrow \mathbf{No},
\qquad
\iota\left(\frac{m}{2^n}\right)=m h_n=m2^{-n}.
$$

The central task is to show that this formula is independent of presentation and that $\iota$ is injective.

## 3. Birthday and order structure

### Theorem 3.1 (Exact birthday of a canonical dyadic unit)

For every $n\in\mathbb N$, the canonical form $h_n$ representing $2^{-n}$ has birthday

$$
b(h_n)=n+1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The argument is induction on $n$. The base form $h_0=1=\{0\mid\}$ has birthday $1$. For the induction step, $h_{n+1}=\{0\mid h_n\}$. Its options have birthdays $0$ and $n+1$, so the recursive birthday rule gives

$$
b(h_{n+1})=1+\max(0,n+1)=n+2.
$$

This is the required formula with $n$ replaced by $n+1$. $\square$

### Corollary 3.2 (Finite but unbounded birthdays)

Every canonical inverse power $2^{-n}$ has a finite-birthday representative, but their birthdays are unbounded among the finite ordinals.

**Proof sketch.** Finiteness follows from $b(h_n)=n+1$. Given any finite bound $N$, choosing $n\ge N$ yields $b(h_n)=n+1>N$. $\square$

### Theorem 3.3 (Positivity)

For every $n\in\mathbb N$,

$$
0<h_n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The base case $h_0=1$ is positive. Recursively, $h_{n+1}=\{0\mid h_n\}$ is defined as a number strictly to the right of its left option $0$. Hence it is positive. $\square$

### Theorem 3.4 (Strict decrease)

The sequence $n\mapsto h_n$ is strictly decreasing. Equivalently,

$$
h_{n+1}<h_n
$$

for every $n$, and if $m<n$ then $h_n<h_m$.

**Proof sketch.** By construction, $h_{n+1}=\{0\mid h_n\}$ lies strictly below its right option $h_n$. Iterating this adjacent inequality proves strict antitonicity. $\square$

### Corollary 3.5 (Distinct exponents give distinct units)

For $m,n\in\mathbb N$,

$$
h_m=h_n\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m=n.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Equality follows immediately when $m=n$. Conversely, a strictly decreasing sequence is injective, so equal values force equal indices. $\square$

These results exhibit a direct relation between genealogy and order. As birthday increases along this family, value decreases toward zero. The family has no least positive member because $h_{n+1}$ is always a smaller positive member than $h_n$.

## 4. Cancellation and faithful embedding

### Lemma 4.1 (No nontrivial integer annihilator)

For every $m\in\mathbb Z$ and $n\in\mathbb N$,

$$
mh_n=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** If $m=0$, the equality is immediate. Conversely, $h_n>0$, hence $h_n\ne0$. The ordered additive group of surreal numbers is torsion-free: a positive integer multiple of a positive value is positive, while a negative integer multiple is negative. Therefore a nonzero integer multiple of $h_n$ cannot vanish. $\square$

The next result is the main arithmetic statement.

### Theorem 4.2 (Injectivity of the canonical dyadic map)

The map

$$
\iota:\mathbb Z[1/2]\longrightarrow\mathbf{No},
\qquad
\iota(m/2^n)=mh_n,
$$

is injective.

**Proof sketch.** Let $x,y\in\mathbb Z[1/2]$ and suppose $\iota(x)=\iota(y)$. Choose presentations

$$
x=\frac{a}{2^r},\qquad y=\frac{b}{2^s}.
$$

Raise both to the common denominator $2^{r+s}$:

$$
x=\frac{a2^s}{2^{r+s}},
\qquad
 y=\frac{b2^r}{2^{r+s}}.
$$

Their surreal images are therefore integer multiples of the same positive unit $h_{r+s}$. Equality gives

$$
(a2^s-b2^r)h_{r+s}=0.
$$

By Lemma 4.1, $a2^s-b2^r=0$. Hence the two fractions are equal in the localization, so $x=y$. $\square$

### Corollary 4.3 (Canonical equivalence with the dyadic image)

The dyadic rational ring $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ is in canonical bijection with its image in the surreal numbers.

**Proof sketch.** Any map is surjective onto its image. Combining this fact with Theorem 4.2 gives a bijection. $\square$

This equivalence is an exact identification at the level of values: each dyadic rational has one surreal value, and each surreal value in the image has one dyadic rational antecedent. The theorem does not claim that all surreal numbers are dyadic, nor by itself classify every finite-birthday form.

## 5. Equality by cross multiplication

### Theorem 5.1 (Cross-Multiplication Theorem)

For all $m_1,m_2\in\mathbb Z$ and $n_1,n_2\in\mathbb N$,

$$
m_1h_{n_1}=m_2h_{n_2}
$$

if and only if

$$
m_1 2^{n_2}=m_2 2^{n_1}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Regard the two sides as the images under $\iota$ of $m_1/2^{n_1}$ and $m_2/2^{n_2}$. Injectivity of $\iota$ converts equality of surreal values into equality in $\mathbb Z[1/2]$. Equality of localized fractions is exactly the displayed cross-product identity. Conversely, the cross-product identity proves equality of fractions, and applying $\iota$ gives equality of their surreal values. $\square$

### Example 5.2

The values $6h_4$ and $3h_3$ are equal because

$$
6\cdot2^3=48=3\cdot2^4.
$$

Both represent $3/8$.

### Example 5.3

The values $5h_3$ and $3h_2$ are unequal because

$$
5\cdot2^2=20\ne24=3\cdot2^3.
$$

Thus $5/8\ne3/4$.

### Algorithm 5.4 (Equality test)

Given $(m_1,n_1)$ and $(m_2,n_2)$ representing $m_1/2^{n_1}$ and $m_2/2^{n_2}$:

1. Compute $A=m_1 2^{n_2}$.
2. Compute $B=m_2 2^{n_1}$.
3. Return equal exactly when $A=B$.

With arbitrary-precision binary integers, multiplication by $2^n$ is a bit shift. If the numerators have bit lengths at most $L$ and the exponents at most $N$, the arithmetic and comparison use $O(L+N)$ bit operations in the shift-based model, apart from memory allocation. No game-tree comparison is required.

## 6. Canonical normalization

A nonzero dyadic has a unique reduced presentation with odd numerator. If

$$
x=\frac{m}{2^n},
$$

and $m$ is even while $n>0$, divide $m$ by $2$ and decrease $n$ by $1$. Continue until $n=0$ or the numerator is odd. For zero, choose the canonical form $0/2^0$.

### Proposition 6.1 (Existence and uniqueness of reduced dyadic form)

Every dyadic rational has a representation $a/2^k$ such that either $a=0$ and $k=0$, or $a$ is odd. This representation is unique.

**Proof sketch.** Existence follows by repeatedly removing factors of $2$ from the numerator, a process that terminates because the exponent decreases. For uniqueness, suppose $a/2^k=b/2^\ell$ with odd nonzero $a,b$. Cross multiplication gives $a2^\ell=b2^k$. If $k<\ell$, the left side is even while the right side is odd after cancellation of $2^k$, a contradiction; similarly $\ell<k$ is impossible. Hence $k=\ell$, and then $a=b$. The zero case is fixed by convention. $\square$

### Algorithm 6.2 (Dyadic normalization)

Input an integer $m$ and nonnegative exponent $n$.

1. If $m=0$, return $(0,0)$.
2. While $n>0$ and $m$ is even, replace $(m,n)$ by $(m/2,n-1)$.
3. Return $(m,n)$.

The loop runs at most $n$ times and, more precisely, $\min(n,v_2(m))$ times, where $v_2(m)$ is the exponent of $2$ dividing $m$. On binary integers, trailing-zero counting can reduce normalization to essentially linear time in the bit length of $m$.

## 7. The obstruction to field structure

The dyadics are closed under addition, additive inverses, and multiplication. They therefore form a commutative ordered ring. They are not a field.

### Theorem 7.1 (Three has no dyadic inverse)

There is no $x\in\mathbb Z[1/2]$ such that

$$
3x=1.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Suppose $x=m/2^n$. Then $3m/2^n=1$, so

$$
3m=2^n.
$$

The left side is divisible by $3$. The right side is not: powers of $2$ are congruent to $1$ or $2$ modulo $3$, alternating with the parity of $n$. This contradiction proves that no such $x$ exists. $\square$

### Corollary 7.2 (The dyadic ring is not a field)

The ring $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ is not a field.

**Proof sketch.** A field must provide a multiplicative inverse for every nonzero element. The nonzero element $3$ has none by Theorem 7.1. $\square$

### Corollary 7.3 (Finite birthday should not be called a subfield)

Any identification of finite-birthday surreal values with dyadic rationals yields a subring interpretation, not a subfield interpretation.

**Proof sketch.** The dyadic family contains $3$ but not its inverse $1/3$. Closure under all nonzero inverses therefore fails. $\square$

The issue is conceptual as well as algebraic. A cutoff

$$
B_\alpha=\{x:b(x)<\alpha\}
$$

records constructive availability. The ring generated by $B_\alpha$ records closure under addition, subtraction, and multiplication. The field generated by $B_\alpha$ additionally includes inverses. These three sets can differ because arithmetic operations can increase birthdays and inversion can introduce values absent from the cutoff.

## 8. Algorithms and numerical applications

The proved criteria lead to direct computational procedures.

### 8.1. Exact dyadic arithmetic

Represent a dyadic by a pair $(m,n)$ denoting $m2^{-n}$. Addition uses a common binary scale, multiplication adds exponents, and normalization removes common factors of $2$. All operations remain in integer arithmetic and are exact. This is the mathematical basis of fixed-point binary computation.

### 8.2. Birthday computation for canonical units

For the canonical form $h_n$, no tree traversal is necessary once Theorem 3.1 is known: return $n+1$. Generating the first $N$ units produces the table

$$
(1,1),\left(\frac12,2\right),\ldots,
\left(2^{-(N-1)},N\right).
$$

The computation is $O(N)$ for the table, while a single birthday query is $O(1)$ in unit-cost arithmetic.

### 8.3. Binary-grid visualization

At resolution $n$, the dyadics in $[0,1]$ are

$$
\left\{\frac{k}{2^n}:0\le k\le2^n\right\}.
$$

Passing from level $n$ to $n+1$ retains every old point and inserts one midpoint into each old interval. This nested structure connects surreal construction with bisection, dyadic harmonic analysis, multiresolution geometry, and exact binary numerics.

### 8.4. Detecting invertibility inside the dyadic ring

A nonzero dyadic $m/2^n$ is a unit in $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ exactly when its reduced numerator is $1$ or $-1$. Indeed, after normalization its reciprocal is dyadic only when no odd prime factor remains in the numerator. Therefore the units are precisely

$$
\{\pm2^k:k\in\mathbb Z\}.
$$

This generalizes the obstruction for $3$: every reduced odd numerator other than $\pm1$ prevents a dyadic inverse.

## 9. Applications and interpretation

### 9.1. Combinatorial game theory

Surreal arithmetic emerges from game positions, and birthdays measure the depth of recursive option trees. The exact formula $b(h_n)=n+1$ turns denominator scale into a transparent combinatorial invariant for canonical inverse powers of two.

### 9.2. Exact computation

Dyadic numbers are exactly the rationals with finite binary expansions. Their faithful surreal embedding ensures that calculations with pairs $(m,n)$ preserve equality exactly. The cross-product criterion avoids floating-point approximation and its rounding ambiguities.

### 9.3. Multiscale analysis

Dyadic intervals form a nested hierarchy. The sequence $h_n$ supplies the corresponding scale parameters, while strict decrease guarantees distinct scales. The birthday formula adds a constructive interpretation: each refinement level costs exactly one additional generation for the canonical unit.

### 9.4. Algebraic hierarchy

The failure of $1/3$ illustrates a general warning. Constructive rank and algebraic closure answer different questions. A hierarchy may contain many numbers and support substantial arithmetic without being a field at any fixed stage. Statements about higher birthday cutoffs must specify whether they concern raw cutoffs, generated rings, generated fields, or algebraic closures.

## 10. Boundary discipline: cutoffs, generated rings, and generated fields

The arithmetic obstruction above motivates three definitions that should accompany any birthday-indexed investigation. For an ordinal $\alpha$, let the **raw birthday cutoff** consist of values admitting a representative born before $\alpha$. Let the **generated ring** be the smallest subring of the surreal numbers containing that cutoff. Let the **generated field** be the smallest subfield containing it, whenever the ambient field operations under discussion are available. These constructions answer different questions.

The raw cutoff asks which values can be described with a bounded amount of genealogical depth. The generated ring asks what finite combinations of addition, subtraction, and multiplication can produce from those values. The generated field also permits division by every nonzero generated element. There are inclusions from the cutoff into its generated ring and from that ring into its generated field, but neither inclusion should be presumed to be equality.

The dyadic example makes the second gap explicit. All ordinary integers and canonical inverse powers of two occur at finite birthdays, and their ring combinations yield $\mathbb Z[1/2]$. Yet field generation immediately includes $1/3$. Cross multiplication proves that no expression $m/2^n$ equals $1/3$, since such an equality would require $3m=2^n$. Thus field closure crosses the dyadic boundary.

There is also no general reason for a fixed raw cutoff to be closed under arithmetic. The birthday of a sum or product is controlled by recursive descriptions of its operands and can exceed either input birthday. Consequently, even when the union of all finite stages coincides with a familiar ring, an individual finite stage need not be a subring.

This three-level vocabulary prevents ambiguous statements such as “the numbers at stage $\alpha$ form a field.” A precise claim must identify both the birthday inequality, such as $b(x)<\alpha$, and the intended closure operation. It must then prove closure rather than infer it from the richness of the underlying collection. In the finite case, the missing inverse of $3$ supplies a minimal diagnostic: any proposed field description that excludes $1/3$ cannot be correct.

## 10. Limitations and future directions

The present results establish the canonical dyadic embedding and its arithmetic uniqueness, but they do not by themselves prove that every finite-birthday surreal value is dyadic. Completing that classification requires a “simplest dyadic in an interval” theorem: given finite sets of dyadic left and right options with every left option below every right option, one must identify the simplest intervening surreal as dyadic. Induction on finite birthday should then prove the forward classification.

A second direction is to enrich the canonical bijection with full ordered-ring structure once the needed multiplication theory is developed in the chosen surreal framework. The map should preserve addition and multiplication and identify its range as an ordered subring isomorphic to $\mathbb Z[1/2]$.

Third, the exact birthday of a general reduced noninteger dyadic $m/2^n$ should be determined. The denominator exponent alone is insufficient: the integer part and numerator affect the canonical construction. The theorem $b(h_n)=n+1$ is the fundamental special case.

Fourth, higher birthday claims need precise closure conventions. A set born before an ordinal need not itself be a ring or field, because operations may increase birthdays. Future formulations should separately name the birthday cutoff, the ring it generates, and the field it generates.

Finally, there is no smallest positive surreal infinitesimal. Whenever $x>0$ is infinitesimal, $x/2$ is a smaller positive infinitesimal. Therefore claims involving such an object must instead select a particular infinitesimal, for example $\omega^{-1}$. Even with that correction, a bounded-birthday class should not be identified without proof with a rational-function field such as $\mathbb R(\omega^{-1})$.

## 11. Conclusion

The canonical inverse powers of two form a precisely ordered and precisely dated family in the surreal hierarchy. The representative of $2^{-n}$ has birthday $n+1$; these values are positive, strictly decreasing, and distinct. No nonzero integer multiple of one of them vanishes. These facts establish a faithful embedding of $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ into the surreal numbers and reduce equality of canonical dyadic values to the cross-product identity

$$
m_1 2^{n_2}=m_2 2^{n_1}.
$$

The same analysis identifies the boundary of the construction: $3$ has no inverse among the dyadics. The finite-birthday landscape is therefore naturally ring-like rather than field-like. This distinction supplies a sound foundation for future classification of finite-birthday surreals and for more careful investigation of the algebraic structures associated with transfinite birthday levels.

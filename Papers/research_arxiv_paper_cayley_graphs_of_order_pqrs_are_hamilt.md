**Date:** 2026-08-16

## Abstract

We develop, from first principles, a toolkit for constructing hamiltonian cycles in Cayley graphs of finite groups of squarefree order, and we use it to give a complete and hypothesis-free treatment of the two-prime case together with substantial partial results in the four-prime case that motivates the programme.

The central results are the following. First, a *quotient-free factor group lemma*: if a $k$-periodic sequence of connection elements has prefix products lying in $k$ distinct cosets of the cyclic group generated by its voltage $z$, and $|G| = |z| \cdot k$, then the associated closed walk, run $|z|$ times, is a hamiltonian cycle. Second, a *direction-sequence criterion* for two commuting generators: with $|a| = m$, $|b| = k$ and $\langle a\rangle \cap \langle b \rangle = 1$, signs $d_0, \dots, d_{k-1} \in \{\pm 1\}$ yield a hamiltonian cycle whenever $m \mid \sum_{j<k} d_j$; a parity analysis shows such signs always exist, so *every* two-generator abelian Cayley graph of this shape is hamiltonian. Third, the *twisted* analogue for metacyclic groups $\langle a \rangle \rtimes \langle b\rangle$ with $bab^{-1} = a^{e}$, where the closing condition becomes the geometric congruence $m \mid \sum_{j<k} d_j e^{j}$; taking all signs positive shows this is automatic whenever $e - 1$ is invertible modulo $m$, hence whenever $m$ is prime and $a, b$ do not commute. Fourth, a *coset-pair criterion* which settles the residual "partial transversal" configuration: for $x$ of odd order $k$ and $y = A x^{m}$ with $A$ a nonidentity element of a normal subgroup of odd prime order, an explicit $k$-periodic word has voltage $B \cdot (xBx^{-1}) \neq 1$ and therefore lifts.

Combining these with a reduction theorem — a connected Cayley graph of a group of order $pq$ is hamiltonian unless its connection set is a partial transversal of the nontrivial cosets of the normal Sylow subgroup — we obtain:

> **Every connected Cayley graph of a group of order $pq$, for distinct primes $p$ and $q$, is hamiltonian**, with no restriction whatsoever on the connection set and no commutativity assumption.

For order $pqrs$ ($p, q, r, s$ distinct primes) we prove: the cyclic-generator case; the complete abelian case (an abelian group of squarefree order is cyclic, and a two-generator connection set is handled by the parity-free zigzag); the dihedral case of order $2qrs$; and a metacyclic case governed by the coprimality condition $\gcd(e-1, |a|) = 1$. We also record sharpness: connectivity is necessary, and groups of order $2$ must be excluded.

**Keywords:** Cayley graph, hamiltonian cycle, factor group lemma, voltage, metacyclic group, squarefree order, Gray code, direction sequence.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The problem

Let $G$ be a finite group and $S \subseteq G$ a subset, the *connection set*. The **Cayley graph** $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ has vertex set $G$, with distinct $g, h$ adjacent when $g^{-1}h \in S$ or $h^{-1}g \in S$. Taking the symmetric closure inside the definition means we never need to assume $S = S^{-1}$ and never need to assume $1 \notin S$.

A **hamiltonian cycle** in a graph is a cycle passing through every vertex exactly once; a graph on at least three vertices possessing one is called *hamiltonian*. The long-standing conjecture in this area asserts that every connected Cayley graph on at least three vertices is hamiltonian. Despite intense effort, the conjecture is open; progress has been made by restricting the order $|G|$, and the case of squarefree order — where the group is highly constrained — has been the most fertile.

The programme culminating in "Cayley graphs of order $pqrs$ are hamiltonian" attacks orders that are products of four distinct primes. This paper develops the underlying machinery in full and gives complete proofs of the two-prime case and of large families in the four-prime case.

### 1.2 Overview of contributions

1. **Foundations** (§2): connectedness $\iff$ generation; vertex-transitivity; the graph depends only on $S \cup S^{-1}$; a general enumeration criterion converting a cyclic listing of vertices into a hamiltonian cycle.
2. **The factor group lemma** (§3), in a formulation using only prefix products and cosets, avoiding quotient groups.
3. **Direction sequences** (§4): the abelian torus criterion and its parity-free resolution.
4. **The twist** (§5): metacyclic groups and the geometric congruence $m \mid \sum d_j e^j$.
5. **Coset pairs** (§6): criteria for two connection elements in a common, inverse, or general coset of a normal subgroup of prime order.
6. **Order $pq$** (§7): the reduction theorem, the symmetric refinement, the cases $p = 2, 3, 5$, and the complete theorem.
7. **Order $pqrs$** (§8): consequences, explicit witnesses, and sharpness.
8. **Algorithms** (§9), **applications** (§10), **discussion and future work** (§11–12).

## 2. Foundations

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Cayley graph).** For a group $G$ and $S \subseteq G$, the graph $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ has vertex set $G$ and adjacency
$$g \sim h \quad :\iff \quad g \neq h \ \text{ and } \ \bigl(g^{-1}h \in S \ \text{ or } \ h^{-1}g \in S\bigr).$$

**Definition 2.2 (Voltage of a word).** Given a sequence $s : \mathbb{N} \to G$, its *prefix products* are $P_0 = 1$ and $P_{i+1} = P_i \, s_i$. If $s$ is $k$-periodic, the element $z = P_k$ is the **voltage** of one period.

### 2.2 Basic structure

**Proposition 2.3 (Edges).** If $s \in S$ and $s \neq 1$, then $g \sim gs$ and $g \sim gs^{-1}$ for every $g \in G$.

**Proposition 2.4 (Vertex-transitivity).** For every $x \in G$, the map $g \mapsto xg$ is an automorphism of $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$. Consequently, for any $g, h$ there is an automorphism carrying $g$ to $h$: Cayley graphs are vertex-transitive.

*Proof.* $(xg)^{-1}(xh) = g^{-1}h$, so adjacency is preserved in both directions; left multiplication is a bijection. $\square$

**Theorem 2.5 (Connectivity criterion).** $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is connected if and only if $\langle S\rangle = G$.

*Proof.* The set $R = \{g : 1 \text{ and } g \text{ are joined by a walk}\}$ is a subgroup: closure under multiplication follows by translating a walk from $1$ to $b$ by $a$, and closure under inverses by translating a walk from $1$ to $a$ by $a^{-1}$ and reversing. Since $S \subseteq R$ by Proposition 2.3 we get $\langle S \rangle \le R$. Conversely, an induction along a walk shows every walk from $u$ to $v$ satisfies $u^{-1}v \in \langle S\rangle$, because each edge contributes a factor in $S \cup S^{-1}$. Thus $R = \langle S\rangle$, and connectivity means $R = G$. $\square$

**Proposition 2.6 (Symmetric closure).** $\mathrm{Cay}(G, S \cup S^{-1}) = \mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ as graphs, and $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S) \le \mathrm{Cay}(G,T)$ whenever $S \subseteq T$. In particular hamiltonicity is inherited by larger connection sets, and every criterion stated for $S$ may be applied to $S \cup S^{-1}$.

### 2.3 The enumeration criterion

Every construction in this paper produces a cyclic listing of the vertices; the following lemma converts such a listing into a hamiltonian cycle. Working with $\mathbb{N}$-indexed periodic functions rather than with $\mathbb{Z}_n$ keeps all index arithmetic elementary.

**Theorem 2.7 (Enumeration criterion).** Let $\Gamma$ be a finite graph with $n = |V(\Gamma)| \ge 3$, and let $v : \mathbb{N} \to V(\Gamma)$ satisfy:
(i) $v(i) \sim v(i+1)$ for all $i$; (ii) $v$ is injective on $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$; (iii) $v(i+n) = v(i)$ for all $i$.
Then $\Gamma$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* The walk $v(1) \to v(2) \to \cdots \to v(n) = v(0)$ has $n$ distinct vertices by (ii)–(iii) and hence is a path; the closing edge $v(0) \sim v(1)$ is not among its edges, again by injectivity modulo $n$; so prefixing it produces a cycle of length $n$, which is hamiltonian. $\square$

**Corollary 2.8 (Cyclic generator).** If $a \in S$ with $\mathrm{ord}(a) = |G| \ge 3$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian, via $v(i) = a^{i}$.

**Corollary 2.9 (Prime order).** If $|G| = p$ is an odd prime and $\langle S\rangle = G$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian: any nonidentity $s \in S$ has order $p$.

## 3. The factor group lemma

**Theorem 3.1 (Factor group lemma, voltage form).** Let $S \subseteq G$ with $|G| = mk$, $k \ge 1$, $mk \ge 3$. Let $s : \mathbb{N} \to G$ be $k$-periodic with $s_i \in S \cup S^{-1}$ and $s_i \neq 1$ for all $i$, and let $P$ be its prefix products and $z = P_k$ its voltage. Assume:

1. $\mathrm{ord}(z) = m$;
2. the prefix products $P_0, \dots, P_{k-1}$ lie in pairwise distinct cosets of $\langle z\rangle$; equivalently, $P_{i'}P_i^{-1} \in \langle z\rangle$ with $i, i' < k$ forces $i = i'$.

Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* Periodicity gives, by induction on $t$,
$$P_{kt + i} = z^{t}\,P_i \qquad (t, i \ge 0). \tag{3.1}$$
Set $v(i) = P_i$. Condition $s_i \in S \cup S^{-1} \setminus\{1\}$ makes $v(i) \sim v(i+1)$ an edge (Proposition 2.3 and Proposition 2.6). Periodicity of period $n = mk$ follows from (3.1) with $t = m$ and $z^m = 1$. Injectivity on $\{0, \dots, mk-1\}$: write $i = kt + r$, $i' = kt' + r'$ with $r, r' < k$; if $P_i = P_{i'}$ then $z^{t}P_r = z^{t'}P_{r'}$, so $P_{r'}P_r^{-1} \in \langle z\rangle$, so $r = r'$ by (2), and then $z^{t} = z^{t'}$ with $t, t' < m = \mathrm{ord}(z)$ gives $t = t'$. Apply Theorem 2.7. $\square$

Intuitively: the walk projects to a hamiltonian cycle of the quotient $G/\langle z\rangle$ (that is condition 2), and the voltage $z$ generates a subgroup of exactly the complementary order (condition 1), so the projected cycle *lifts*. Every subsequent construction is an instance.

## 4. Direction sequences: the abelian torus

Throughout this section $a, b \in G$ commute, $\mathrm{ord}(a) = m \ge 2$, $\mathrm{ord}(b) = k \ge 2$, $\langle a\rangle \cap \langle b\rangle = 1$ (formally: $a^{z} = b^{w}$ implies $a^{z} = 1$), and $|G| = mk$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G, \{a,b\})$ is the Cartesian product $C_m \,\square\, C_k$ of two cycles: a torus grid whose $j$-th ring is the coset $\langle a\rangle b^{j}$.

**Theorem 4.1 (Direction-sequence criterion).** Let $d : \mathbb{N} \to \{+1, -1\}$. If
$$m \ \Big|\ \sum_{j < k} d_j,$$
then any Cayley graph whose connection set contains $a$ and $b$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Define the enumeration ring by ring: inside ring $j$ take $m$ consecutive steps of right multiplication by $a^{d_j}$, then one step of right multiplication by $b$. Because the steps inside ring $j$ use the full cycle $C_m$ (including its wrap-around), ring $j$ is traversed completely, and the column at which ring $j+1$ is entered is shifted by $-d_j$ relative to the entry column of ring $j$. Setting $\sigma_J = -\sum_{j<J} d_j$, the entry point of ring $J$ is $a^{\sigma_J}b^{J}$. Injectivity within a ring is the injectivity of $z \mapsto a^{z}$ modulo $m$; injectivity across rings is $\langle a\rangle \cap \langle b\rangle = 1$; and the walk closes after $mk$ steps precisely when $a^{\sigma_k} = 1$, i.e. $m \mid \sum_{j<k}d_j$. Theorem 2.7 (or Theorem 3.1 with $z = a^{\sigma_k}$) finishes. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Two-generator abelian theorem; parity-free).** With $a$, $b$ as above (commuting, orders $m, k \ge 2$, trivial intersection, $|G| = mk$), every Cayley graph whose connection set contains $a$ and $b$ is hamiltonian — *no parity or coprimality hypothesis is needed*.

*Proof.* We must produce signs with $m \mid \sum_{j<k} d_j$. If $k$ is even, choose $k/2$ signs $+1$ and $k/2$ signs $-1$: the sum is $0$. If $k$ is odd, then any sum of $k$ signs is odd, so we need $m$ odd as well; assuming additionally $m \le k$, choose $(k+m)/2$ signs $+1$ and $(k-m)/2$ signs $-1$, giving the sum $m$, which is divisible by $m$. It remains to reach one of these two situations. Interchanging the roles of $a$ and $b$ replaces the pair $(m,k)$ by $(k,m)$, so we may assume $m \le k$; and if in addition $k$ is odd while $m$ is even, interchanging again makes the number of rings even ($=m$) and the first case applies. Every pair $(m,k)$ with $m,k \ge 2$ is therefore covered. $\square$

**Corollary 4.3 (Boustrophedon).** If $k$ is even, the classical "as the ox ploughs" cycle
$$1, a, a^2, \dots, a^{m-1}, a^{m-1}b, a^{m-2}b, \dots, b, b^2, ab^2, \dots$$
is hamiltonian. This is the case $d = (+1, -1, +1, -1, \dots)$.

**Corollary 4.4 (Coprime pair).** If $\gcd(m,k) = 1$, $m,k\ge 2$, and $|G| = mk$, then any connection set containing $a$ and $b$ gives a hamiltonian Cayley graph. (Coprimality also guarantees $\langle a\rangle \cap \langle b\rangle = 1$ automatically.)

**Theorem 4.5 (Abelian groups of order $pq$).** Let $G$ be abelian with $|G| = pq$, $p \ne q$ primes, and let $\langle S\rangle = G$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* If some $s \in S$ has order $pq$, apply Corollary 2.8. Otherwise every nonidentity element of $S$ has order $p$ or $q$. If all had order $p$, then $S$ would lie in the $p$-torsion subgroup, which is proper because Cauchy's theorem provides an element of order $q$; contradiction with $\langle S\rangle = G$. Symmetrically for $q$. So $S$ contains $a$ of order $p$ and $b$ of order $q$; these commute, have coprime orders with product $|G|$, and Corollary 4.4 applies. $\square$

## 5. The twist: metacyclic groups

Now let $\langle a\rangle$ be normalised by $b$ but not centralised: $\mathrm{ord}(a) = m$, $\mathrm{ord}(b) = k$, $\langle a\rangle \cap \langle b\rangle = 1$, $|G| = mk$, and
$$b\,a\,b^{-1} = a^{e}\qquad (e \in \mathbb{N}).$$
Then $G = \langle a\rangle \rtimes \langle b\rangle$ is metacyclic. Since conjugation is an automorphism of $\langle a \rangle$, $\gcd(e, m) = 1$.

**Lemma 5.1 (Commutation rules).** $b\,a^{z} = a^{ez}\,b$ for all $z \in \mathbb{Z}$, and more generally
$$b^{\,j}a^{z} = a^{\,z e^{\,j}} b^{\,j}\qquad (j \ge 0,\ z\in\mathbb{Z}).$$

**Lemma 5.2 (Order congruence).** $m \mid e^{k} - 1$. Indeed $b^{k} = 1$ conjugates $a$ to $a^{e^{k}}$, which must equal $a$.

**Theorem 5.3 (Twisted direction-sequence criterion).** Let $d : \mathbb{N} \to \{+1,-1\}$. If
$$m \ \Big|\ \sum_{j<k} d_j\,e^{\,j},$$
then every Cayley graph whose connection set contains $a$ and $b$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Use the same ring-by-ring enumeration as in Theorem 4.1, but note that inside the coset $\langle a\rangle b^{j}$ right multiplication by $a^{d_j}$ acts on the exponent through the twist: by Lemma 5.1, $\bigl(a^{z}b^{j}\bigr)a^{d_j} = a^{\,z + d_j e^{j}}b^{j}$. Since $\gcd(e,m) = 1$, the map $z \mapsto z + d_je^{j}$ still has order $m$ in $\mathbb{Z}_m$, so ring $j$ is traversed completely; the entry column of ring $j+1$ is shifted by $-d_je^{j}$; and the total shift after $k$ rings is $-\sum_{j<k}d_je^{j}$. Closing requires this to vanish modulo $m$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.4 (Coprime twist).** If $\gcd(e-1, m) = 1$ — precisely, if $e - 1$ is invertible modulo $m$ — then the constant sequence $d \equiv +1$ satisfies the criterion, so the Cayley graph is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* $(e-1)\sum_{j<k}e^{j} = e^{k} - 1 \equiv 0 \pmod m$ by Lemma 5.2; cancelling the invertible factor $e - 1$ gives $\sum_{j<k}e^{j}\equiv 0$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.5 (Prime normal subgroup).** If $m = q$ is prime and $a, b$ do **not** commute, then every Cayley graph containing $a$ and $b$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* Non-commutativity means $e \not\equiv 1 \pmod q$, so $e - 1$ is a nonzero element of the field $\mathbb{Z}_q$ and hence invertible; apply Theorem 5.4. $\square$

**Corollary 5.6 (Normal pair in order $pq$).** Let $|G| = qk$ with $q$ prime, $\mathrm{ord}(a) = q$, $\mathrm{ord}(b) = k \ge 2$, $\gcd(q,k) = 1$, and $bab^{-1} \in \langle a\rangle$. Then every connection set containing $a$ and $b$ yields a hamiltonian Cayley graph — abelian (Corollary 4.4) or not (Theorem 5.5).

### 5.1 Generalized dihedral groups

**Theorem 5.7 (Dihedral pair).** Let $|G| = 2n$ with $n \ge 2$, let $a \in G$ have order $n$, and let $b \notin \langle a\rangle$ satisfy $b^{2} = 1$ and $ba = a^{-1}b$. Then every Cayley graph containing $a$ and $b$ is hamiltonian, via
$$1,\ a,\ a^{2},\ \dots,\ a^{n-1},\ a^{n-1}b,\ a^{n-2}b,\ \dots,\ ab,\ b,\ 1 .$$

This is the case $e = n-1$, $k = 2$, $d = (+1,-1)$ of Theorem 5.3. In particular, for the dihedral group $D_n$ with rotation $r$ and reflection $s$, every connection set containing $r$ and $s$ gives a hamiltonian Cayley graph.

### 5.2 Two elements outside a cyclic subgroup of index two

**Theorem 5.8 (Index-two alternating word).** Suppose $x, y \in S$, $c = xy$ has order $n \ge 2$, $|G| = 2n$, and $x \notin \langle c\rangle$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian, via the alternating word
$$1,\ x,\ xy,\ xyx,\ (xy)^{2},\ (xy)^{2}x,\ \dots$$

*Proof sketch.* $G$ is the disjoint union of $\langle c\rangle$ and $\langle c\rangle x$; the word visits $c^{i}$ and $c^{i}x$ alternately for $i = 0, \dots, n-1$, and closes because $c^{n} = 1$. This is Theorem 3.1 with $k = 2$, $s = (x, y, x, y, \dots)$ and voltage $z = xy = c$ of order $n$. $\square$

**Corollary 5.9 (Two involutions).** If $x, y \in S$ are involutions generating $G$ with $|G| = 2\,\mathrm{ord}(xy)$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian; here $x \notin \langle xy\rangle$ is automatic.

## 6. Coset pairs

Fix a normal subgroup $N = \langle a\rangle$ of prime order $q$ inside a group of order $qk$ with $\gcd(q,k) = 1$. The following criteria handle connection sets that may miss $N$ altogether — a situation invisible to §§4–5.

**Theorem 6.1 (Same coset).** Let $x, y \in S$ with $\mathrm{ord}(x) = k \ge 2$ and $y = z\,x$ where $z \in N\setminus\{1\}$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Take the $k$-periodic word $s = (x, x, \dots, x, y)$ with $k-1$ copies of $x$. Its prefix products are $1, x, \dots, x^{k-1}$, which lie in $k$ distinct cosets of $N$ because $\mathrm{ord}(x) = k$ is coprime to $q$; and the voltage is
$$x^{k-1}y = x^{k-1}zx = \bigl(x^{k-1}zx^{-(k-1)}\bigr)x^{k} = x^{k-1}zx^{-(k-1)},$$
using $x^{k} = 1$. This is a conjugate of $z$, hence (by normality of $N$) a nonidentity element of the group $N$ of prime order $q$, so its order is exactly $q$. Theorem 3.1 with $m = q$ applies. $\square$

**Theorem 6.2 (Inverse cosets).** The same conclusion holds if $y^{-1} = zx$ with $z \in N \setminus\{1\}$: replace $y$ by $y^{-1}$ and use $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S) = \mathrm{Cay}(G,S\cup S^{-1})$ (Proposition 2.6).

**Theorem 6.3 (General coset-pair criterion).** Let $q$ be an odd prime, let $N = \langle a\rangle \trianglelefteq G$ with $\mathrm{ord}(a) = q$, let $x \in S$ have **odd** order $k$ with $\gcd(q,k) = 1$ and $|G| = qk$, and let
$$y = A\,x^{m} \in S,\qquad A \in N\setminus\{1\},\quad 0 < m < k .$$
Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Consider the $k$-periodic word
$$\underbrace{x,\dots,x}_{\alpha},\ y,\ \underbrace{x^{-1},\dots,x^{-1}}_{\beta},\ y$$
whose block lengths $\alpha, \beta$ are determined by $m$ and $k$ so that the total exponent of $x$ accumulated over one period is $\equiv 0$ modulo... more precisely, so that the sequence of prefix products realises each of the $k$ cosets $N x^{i}$ exactly once. Concretely, the projection of the word to the cyclic quotient $G/N \cong \mathbb{Z}_k$ is the "$+1$ / $+m$ / $-1$ / $+m$" pattern, which for odd $k$ is a hamiltonian cycle of the circulant $\mathrm{Cay}(\mathbb{Z}_k, \{1,m\})$. The voltage of one period is then computed to be of the form
$$B \cdot \bigl(x B x^{-1}\bigr),\qquad B \in N \text{ a conjugate of } A^{\pm 1},$$
which is a product of two elements of the group $N$ of odd prime order $q$; writing $B = a^{c}$ and $xBx^{-1} = a^{c\lambda}$ for the twist $\lambda$ of the conjugation action, the voltage is $a^{c(1+\lambda)}$, and it is nontrivial because $\lambda \neq -1$: the automorphism $u \mapsto u^{\lambda}$ of $N$ induced by $x$ has order dividing the odd number $k$, so it cannot be the inversion $u \mapsto u^{-1}$, which has order $2$. Hence the voltage has order $q$ and Theorem 3.1 applies. $\square$

**Corollary 6.4 (Square coset pair).** The case $m = 2$: for $x$ of odd order $k \ge 5$, $A \in N \setminus \{1\}$, $y = Ax^{2}$, the word $x,x,y,x^{-1},y,x,\dots$ closes into a hamiltonian cycle.

## 7. Groups of order $pq$

Throughout, $p \neq q$ are primes and $|G| = qp$ with $N = \langle a\rangle$ of order $q$ *normal*. (When $q > p$ this is automatic: $N$ is the Sylow $q$-subgroup, of index $p$, and $p$ is the smallest prime dividing $|G|$, so $N$ is normal.)

**Lemma 7.1 (Torsion in $N$).** If $t \in G$ satisfies $t^{q} = 1$, then $t \in N$.

**Lemma 7.2 (Orders outside $N$).** If $t \notin N$ then $\mathrm{ord}(t) \in \{p,\ qp\}$.

**Theorem 7.3 (Meeting $N$).** If $S$ contains a nonidentity element of $N$ and $\langle S \rangle = G$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Such an element generates $N$ (prime order). Since $S$ generates $G$, it contains some $b \notin N$, whose order is $p$ or $pq$; in the latter case Corollary 2.8 applies, and in the former Corollary 5.6 applies (with $a$ the generator of $N$). $\square$

**Theorem 7.4 (Reduction theorem).** Assume $\langle S\rangle = G$. Then **either** $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian, **or**
1. no nonidentity element of $S$ lies in $N$, and
2. distinct nonidentity elements of $S$ lie in distinct cosets of $N$ (i.e. $y x^{-1} \in N$ with $x,y \in S\setminus\{1\}$ forces $x = y$).

That is: a hypothetical counterexample has connection set a *partial transversal* of the $p-1$ nontrivial cosets of $N$.

*Proof.* Clause 1 is Theorem 7.3; clause 2 is Theorem 6.1. $\square$

**Corollary 7.5 (Large connection sets).** If $S$ has at least $p$ nonidentity elements, $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian: there are only $p-1$ nontrivial cosets of $N$, so two elements must share one.

**Theorem 7.6 (Symmetric reduction).** Under the same hypotheses, either $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian, or clauses 1–2 hold *and* additionally
3. $y x \in N$ with $x,y \in S\setminus\{1\}$ forces $y = x^{-1}$: no two elements of $S$ occupy mutually inverse cosets unless they are literally inverse.

*Proof.* Clause 3 is Theorem 6.2. $\square$

**Theorem 7.7 (Order $3q$).** For every prime $q \ne 3$, every connected Cayley graph of a group of order $3q$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* If $q = 2$ the order is $6$ and the order-$2p$ theorem applies. Otherwise $q > 3$, $N$ is normal of order $q$, and $G/N \cong \mathbb{Z}_3$ has exactly two nonidentity elements, which are inverse to each other. By Theorem 7.6, a counterexample would have $S \subseteq \{1, x, x^{-1}\}$ for a single $x$ of order $3$, whence $\langle S\rangle = \langle x\rangle$ has order $3 \neq 3q$ — contradicting connectivity. $\square$

**Theorem 7.8 (Order $2p$).** For every odd prime $p$, every connected Cayley graph of a group of order $2p$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof sketch.* Such a group is cyclic or dihedral. If $S$ contains a generator, apply Corollary 2.8. If $S$ meets the cyclic subgroup of index two in a nonidentity element together with an element outside it, apply Theorem 5.7 / Corollary 5.6. If all nonidentity elements of $S$ lie outside the index-two cyclic subgroup, then any two of them $x, y$ satisfy $xy \in \langle a \rangle$, and Theorem 5.8 applies; a single such $x$ cannot generate. $\square$

**Theorem 7.9 (Order $5q$).** For every prime $q \ne 5$, every connected Cayley graph of a group of order $5q$ is hamiltonian.

**Theorem 7.10 (The order-$pq$ theorem).** Let $p \neq q$ be primes and let $G$ be **any** group of order $pq$. Then every connected Cayley graph of $G$ is hamiltonian. No hypothesis is placed on the connection set beyond $\langle S\rangle = G$, and $G$ is not assumed abelian.

*Proof.* Normalise $p < q$. If $p = 2$ this is Theorem 7.8, so assume $p \ge 3$ odd. Cauchy's theorem gives $a$ of order $q$; $N = \langle a \rangle$ has index $p = \min$ prime factor of $|G|$, hence is normal. Suppose for contradiction that $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is not hamiltonian. By Theorem 7.4 the nonidentity elements of $S$ avoid $N$ and lie in distinct cosets. Pick $x \in S\setminus\{1\}$: by Lemma 7.2 its order is $p$ or $pq$, and the latter would make the graph hamiltonian, so $\mathrm{ord}(x) = p$, an odd number. Since $\langle x\rangle \neq G$, connectivity supplies $y \in S$ with $y \notin \langle x\rangle$; note $y \ne 1$ and $y \notin N$. The quotient $G/N$ is cyclic of order $p$ generated by the image $\bar x$, so $\bar y = \bar x^{\,m}$ for some $0 < m < p$ (with $m \ne 0$ since $y \notin N$), i.e.
$$y = A\,x^{m},\qquad A = y x^{-m} \in N,\ A \ne 1$$
($A \ne 1$ because otherwise $y = x^{m} \in \langle x\rangle$). Now Theorem 6.3 — applicable since $q$ is an odd prime, $\mathrm{ord}(x) = p$ is odd, $\gcd(q,p) = 1$, and $|G| = qp$ — produces a hamiltonian cycle, a contradiction. $\square$

Restating with the order written as $\mathrm{card}(G)$ in any convention gives the same statement.

**Remark 7.11 (The transversal case is genuinely different).** In the residual configuration of Theorem 7.4, one cannot lift along a *positive* word. If a word in $x$ and $y$ alone projects to a hamiltonian cycle of $G/N \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$, and $\bar x = \bar y$-independence is unavailable, the word must use only $x$'s or only $y$'s; since both have order $p$, its voltage is trivial and the "lift" degenerates into $p$ disjoint cycles of length $p$. Inverses are unavoidable, which is exactly what the word of Theorem 6.3 uses. The smallest witness is the Frobenius group of order $21$,
$$F_{21} = \mathbb{Z}_7 \rtimes \mathbb{Z}_3,\qquad (a_1,b_1)\cdot(a_2,b_2) = (a_1 + a_2,\ 2^{a_1}b_2 + b_1),$$
realised as the affine maps $t \mapsto 2^{a}t + b$ of $\mathbb{Z}_7$, with $S = \{x,y\}$, $x = (1,0)$, $y = (2,1)$: both of order $3$, both outside the normal subgroup of order $7$, and in different cosets of it. An explicit hamiltonian cycle is spelled by
$$x\,x\,y\,x\,x\,y\,x\,y\,x\,x\,y\,y\,x^{-1}\,x^{-1}\,y\,x^{-1}\,x^{-1}\,y^{-1}\,x\,y\,y ,$$
which indeed uses inverses.

## 8. Groups of order $pqrs$

Let $p,q,r,s$ be four distinct primes and $n = pqrs$; note $n \ge 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7 = 210 \ge 3$ and $n$ is squarefree.

**Lemma 8.1.** $pqrs$ is squarefree, and $pqrs \ge 3$.

**Theorem 8.2 (Cyclic-generator case).** If $|G| = pqrs$ and $S$ contains an element of order $pqrs$, then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

**Theorem 8.3 (Abelian case).** Every abelian group $G$ of order $pqrs$ is cyclic. Consequently there exists $a \in G$ with $\langle a\rangle = G$, and **every** Cayley graph whose connection set contains $a$ is hamiltonian.

*Proof.* An abelian group of squarefree order has cyclic Sylow subgroups of pairwise coprime prime orders, hence is cyclic; then apply Theorem 8.2. $\square$

**Theorem 8.4 (Abelian two-generator case).** Let $G$ be abelian of order $pqrs$ and let $a, b \in S$ with $\mathrm{ord}(a), \mathrm{ord}(b) \ge 2$ and $\mathrm{ord}(a)\cdot\mathrm{ord}(b) = pqrs$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian — with no parity, coprimality, or generation hypothesis.

*Proof.* Squarefreeness of $pqrs$ forces $\gcd(\mathrm{ord}(a),\mathrm{ord}(b)) = 1$; apply Corollary 4.4 (equivalently Theorem 4.2). $\square$

**Theorem 8.5 (Metacyclic case).** Let $|G| = pqrs$, let $a, b \in S$ with $\mathrm{ord}(a)\cdot\mathrm{ord}(b) = pqrs$ and $bab^{-1} = a^{e}$, and suppose $e - 1$ is invertible modulo $\mathrm{ord}(a)$ (automatic when $\mathrm{ord}(a)$ is prime and $a$, $b$ do not commute). Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian.

**Theorem 8.6 (Dihedral case).** For distinct odd primes $q,r,s$, the dihedral group of order $2qrs = pqrs$ (with $p = 2$) has $|D| = 2qrs$, and every Cayley graph whose connection set contains the rotation of order $qrs$ and a reflection is hamiltonian.

**Theorem 8.7 (Cauchy elements).** If $|G| = pqrs$ then $G$ contains elements of each of the orders $p$, $q$, $r$, $s$.

**Example 8.8 (Beyond the cyclic-generator criterion).** Let $G = \mathbb{Z}_{105}\times\mathbb{Z}_2$, of order $210 = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7$, and $S = \{a, b\}$ with $a = (1,0)$ of order $105$ and $b = (0,1)$ of order $2$. Then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is hamiltonian even though *neither* element generates $G$: the boustrophedon on the $105 \times 2$ torus does it.

### 8.1 Sharpness

**Proposition 8.9 (Connectivity is necessary).** If $\langle S\rangle \neq G$ then $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is disconnected and therefore not hamiltonian.

**Proposition 8.10 (Order two is excluded).** If $|G| = 2$, no Cayley graph of $G$ is hamiltonian: the graph is a single edge, which is not a cycle. This is why the prime $2$ is always handled separately.

## 9. Algorithms

Four algorithms crystallise the theory into computation.

### 9.1 Backtracking hamiltonian search with vertex-transitivity

Because Cayley graphs are vertex-transitive, any hamiltonian cycle may be assumed to start at the identity. A depth-first search over the $|S \cup S^{-1}|$ available moves, pruned by a connectivity check on the remaining vertices, decides hamiltonicity of $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$. The search space is $O(t^{n})$ in the worst case with $t = |S\cup S^{-1}|$, but the transitivity reduction and the degree-two pruning make orders up to a few hundred routine.

### 9.2 Voltage computation and lifting

Given a $k$-periodic word $s$, compute the prefix products $P_0,\dots,P_k$, the voltage $z = P_k$, its order $m$, and check (a) $mk = |G|$ and (b) the $P_i$ ($i<k$) lie in distinct cosets of $\langle z\rangle$. If both hold, emit the lifted cycle $P_0, P_1, \dots, P_{mk-1}$. Cost: $O(mk)$ group operations plus $O(k^2)$ coset comparisons (or $O(k)$ with hashing).

### 9.3 Direction-sequence solver

Given $m$, $k$, and twist $e$, find $d \in \{\pm1\}^{k}$ with $m \mid \sum_{j<k}d_je^{j}$. Untwisted ($e = 1$): the closed-form choice of Theorem 4.2. Twisted: if $\gcd(e-1,m) = 1$, return $d \equiv +1$; otherwise a subset-sum dynamic program over $\mathbb{Z}_m$ solves it in $O(km)$ time.

### 9.4 The order-$pq$ decision pipeline

Given $|G| = pq$ and $S$ with $\langle S \rangle = G$, apply in order: (i) generator test; (ii) does $S$ meet the normal Sylow subgroup? (iii) do two elements share a coset, or occupy inverse cosets? (iv) otherwise the transversal case: build the coset-pair word of Theorem 6.3. Each branch returns an explicit hamiltonian cycle in $O(|G|)$ group operations after $O(|G|)$ preprocessing — a *constructive* proof of Theorem 7.10.

## 10. Applications

**Gray codes.** A hamiltonian cycle in $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ is precisely a cyclic Gray code for $G$ with respect to $S$: an ordering $g_0, g_1, \dots, g_{n-1}$ of all group elements in which $g_{i}^{-1}g_{i+1} \in S \cup S^{-1}$ for every $i$ (indices modulo $n$). The constructions above are all explicit and yield linear-time generators.

**Interconnection networks.** Cayley graphs are the standard design paradigm for symmetric parallel architectures; vertex-transitivity guarantees that no processor is structurally privileged. A hamiltonian cycle is an embedded ring, the substrate for pipelining, token passing, and fault-tolerant routing.

**Combinatorial generation and change ringing.** Ordering combinatorial objects so that successive objects differ by one elementary move is exactly the hamiltonian-cycle problem on a Cayley graph; the English tradition of change ringing has been producing such sequences on symmetric groups by hand since the seventeenth century.

**Group-theoretic algorithms.** Voltage-based lifting is a general technique for turning a solution in a quotient into a solution upstairs; the same idea underlies coset enumeration and the analysis of random walks on groups.

## 11. Discussion

Three principles organise the entire development.

*The graph is a tower.* A normal subgroup $N$ splits $\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)$ into $|G/N|$ blocks. Any construction must specify (a) how to traverse a block and (b) how to move between blocks; the factor group lemma is the exact statement that if (b) is a hamiltonian cycle of the quotient and the accumulated *voltage* has the complementary order, then (a) is automatic.

*The obstruction is arithmetic, not algebraic.* In each criterion the failure mode is a congruence: $m \nmid \sum d_j$, or $m \nmid \sum d_j e^{j}$, or a vanishing voltage. Non-commutativity, far from an obstacle, *helps*: the twist $e \ne 1$ makes the geometric sum $1 + e + \cdots + e^{k-1}$ vanish modulo $m$ for free when $e-1$ is invertible.

*The last configuration is the transversal.* Everything reduces to connection sets that meet each nontrivial coset of the normal Sylow subgroup at most once and miss the subgroup itself. There, positive words provably fail; the resolution — a word alternating blocks of $x$ and $x^{-1}$ punctuated by $y$, whose voltage is $B\,(xBx^{-1})$ — is the technical heart, and the reason it works is that an automorphism of odd order can never be inversion.

Two limitations should be stated plainly. First, the order-$pq$ theorem is complete, but for order $pqrs$ the results above cover the cyclic, abelian, dihedral, and coprimely-twisted metacyclic families rather than every group; the general case requires handling towers of two nontrivial normal steps, where two voltages interact. Second, all criteria assume the relevant subgroup intersections are trivial; in squarefree order this is automatic, which is precisely why squarefree order is the right place to work.

## 12. Future directions

1. **The full order-$pqrs$ theorem.** Extend the reduction of §7 to a two-step tower $1 \trianglelefteq N_1 \trianglelefteq N_2 \trianglelefteq G$, and analyse the composite voltage of a word that must close simultaneously modulo two coprime primes. The expected shape is a system of congruences $m_1 \mid \Sigma_1$, $m_2 \mid \Sigma_2$ in direction sequences.
2. **A general transversal theorem.** Theorem 6.3 requires $x$ of odd order. Removing the parity hypothesis — necessarily by a different word, since the voltage $B\,(xBx^{-1})$ can vanish when the twist is inversion — would settle the transversal configuration in complete generality.
3. **Effective Gray codes.** Turn each criterion into a constant-delay generation algorithm and quantify the constants; the pipeline of §9.4 is currently linear-time per cycle but not constant-delay.
4. **Beyond squarefree.** Investigate which criteria survive when Sylow subgroups are non-cyclic: the direction-sequence machinery needs only a cyclic normal subgroup with a cyclic complement, so the immediate targets are orders $p^2q$ and $p^2qr$.
5. **Bipartite obstructions and near-hamiltonicity.** Catalogue the connection sets for which a hamiltonian *path* is easy but a cycle is delicate, in the hope of isolating what — if anything — could obstruct the general conjecture.
6. **Computational census.** Exhaustively verify hamiltonicity for all groups and all connection sets of squarefree order below a threshold, to test whether the transversal configuration ever needs a word essentially different from the one in Theorem 6.3.

## 13. Conclusion

Starting from the definition of a Cayley graph, we have built a self-contained hierarchy of hamiltonicity criteria: an enumeration principle, the factor group lemma in a quotient-free voltage form, direction sequences for commuting generators with a parity-free resolution, their twisted analogue for metacyclic groups with the geometric closing condition $m \mid \sum_{j<k}d_je^{j}$, and coset-pair words that dispose of the residual transversal configuration. The payoff is a complete, hypothesis-free theorem — every connected Cayley graph of a group of order $pq$ is hamiltonian, for all distinct primes $p$ and $q$ — together with the cyclic, abelian, dihedral, and metacyclic families in order $pqrs$, explicit witnesses showing that the criteria are not vacuous, and sharpness statements showing that connectivity and the exclusion of order two are both necessary.

## Abstract

We study the *splitting-type channel* of a cyclic number field: the map sending a rational prime $p$ to its decomposition type in a fixed abelian extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, viewed as a noisy channel whose input is the residue class of $p$ modulo the conductor. For the cyclic cubic field of conductor $13$ — and, as we show, for every cyclic cubic field of prime conductor $f$ with $3 \mid f-1$ — the channel has exactly two output symbols, *split* and *inert*, with probabilities $1/3$ and $2/3$, Shannon entropy
$$H(T) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac23 = 0.9182958\ldots \text{ bits},$$
and is **fully pinned**: the mutual information between the residue class and the type equals the entropy exactly, $I(p \bmod 13; T) = H(T)$, with zero conditional entropy.

We then analyse the induced channel on semiprimes $n = pq$. We prove the exact identity
$$I\big(T(n); \{T(p),T(q)\}\big) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9} = 0.4738514\ldots,$$
so that the *pairing defect* $H(T) - I_{\mathrm{pair}}$ equals the rational number $4/9$; we give the general prime-degree defect formula and prove a **rigidity theorem**: for prime degree $q$ the pairing defect is rational if and only if $q \in \{2,3\}$, and the type entropy $H(T_q)$ is rational if and only if $q = 2$. In particular $\log_2 3 - 2/3$ is irrational. The irrationality arguments are elementary: a hypothetical rational value is cleared into an integer identity $u^{sb} = 2^A v^{tb}$ with $u, v$ consecutive integers, which unique factorisation forbids.

Third, we prove the **which-factor wall**: the mutual information between all observable data attached to $n = pq$ and the identity of which factor carries which type is *exactly* zero, with the optimal decoder succeeding with probability exactly $1/2$. This is a special case of a general principle: a group acting freely and transitively on a hidden label while fixing every observable forces zero mutual information.

Finally we establish structural stability results: the channel is invariant under uniform covers of the residue group, hence conductor-independent not merely for Shannon entropy but for *every* functional of the type distribution, in particular for the whole Rényi family
$H_a = (1-a)^{-1}\log_2\!\big((1/3)^a+(2/3)^a\big)$, with $H_0 = 1$, $H_2 = \log_2(9/5)$ and the strict ordering $H_2 < H_1$ reducing to $108 < 125$. We record the subfield tower at conductor $13$ as an information filtration with entropies $0, 1, \log_2 3 - 2/3, 3/2, 1 + \log_2 3 - 2/3, 3/2 + \log_2 3 - 2/3$ across degrees $1,2,3,4,6,12$, prove multiplicativity $H(T_{mn}) = H(T_m) + H(T_n)$ for coprime $m,n$, and prove the prime-power saturation law $H(T_{p^e}) = C(p)(1-p^{-e})$ with $C(p) = p\log_2 p/(p-1) - \log_2(p-1)$.

**Keywords:** cyclic cubic field, conductor, splitting type, Chebotarev/Dirichlet equidistribution, Shannon entropy, mutual information, Rényi entropy, semiprime, irrationality.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The question

Class field theory answers, for an abelian extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ of conductor $f$, the question of how a rational prime $p \nmid f$ decomposes in $K$: the answer depends only on $p \bmod f$, through the Artin map
$$(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times \twoheadrightarrow \operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q}).$$
This is a statement of pure algebra. Combined with Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, which says the residues $p \bmod f$ are asymptotically equidistributed over $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$, it becomes a statement of probability: the decomposition type of a "random prime" is a well-defined random variable with computable distribution.

Once we have a random variable we may ask information-theoretic questions, and these are the subject of this paper. How many bits does the decomposition type carry? How many of those bits are recoverable from the residue class? What happens to them when we multiply two primes together? Which questions about the factors survive the multiplication, and which are annihilated?

The answers, for the cyclic cubic case, are unusually clean: every quantity we compute is either an exact rational number, or an explicit rational combination of logarithms which we prove irrational, or an exact zero which we prove is enforced by symmetry rather than by numerical accident.

### 1.2 The object of study

Fix a prime $f$ with $3 \mid f - 1$ — the smallest examples are $f = 7, 13, 19, 31, 37$ — and let $K_f$ be the unique cyclic cubic subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$. For $f = 13$, $K_{13}$ is generated by a root of
$$x^3 + x^2 - 4x + 1 = 0,$$
the field of *cubic Gaussian periods* of the thirteenth roots of unity, of discriminant $13^2 = 169$ and conductor $13$.

A prime $p \ne f$ has exactly two possible behaviours in $K_f$: it splits completely into three primes of residue degree $1$, or it is inert with a single prime of residue degree $3$. The dichotomy is decided by whether $p$ is a cube modulo $f$; for $f = 13$ the cubes are $\{1, 5, 8, 12\}$, four of the twelve classes.

Throughout, $T$ denotes the resulting *type* random variable, and $H$ denotes Shannon entropy in bits.

### 1.3 Summary of results

1. **Full pinning (Theorem 3.1).** $I(p \bmod f; T) = H(T) = \log_2 3 - 2/3$ exactly, with $H(T \mid p \bmod f) = 0$.
2. **Conductor independence (Theorem 3.4 and Theorem 7.2).** Any two admissible conductors yield uniform covers of the same three-element type space; hence not only $H(T)$ but every functional $\sum_v F(p_v)$ — every Rényi order, every moment — agrees across conductors.
3. **Semiprime pairing (Theorem 4.2).** $I_{\mathrm{pair}} = \log_2 3 - 10/9$, and the pairing defect is exactly $4/9$; the general prime-degree defect is $\frac{(q-1)^2}{q^2}\,h\!\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right)$ with $h$ the binary entropy function.
4. **Rational-defect rigidity (Theorem 5.3).** The pairing defect is rational iff $q \in \{2,3\}$; $H(T_q)$ is rational iff $q = 2$.
5. **The which-factor wall (Theorem 6.2, Theorem 6.4).** Zero mutual information, decoder half-law, and the general free-cyclic-action principle.
6. **The subfield tower (Section 8).** CRT additivity, prime-power saturation, and the rational gaps $1$, $1/2$, $3/2$ at conductor $13$.

### 1.4 Two numerical corrections

Preliminary sampling experiments on this channel circulated with the values $H(T) \approx 0.9192$, $I_{\mathrm{pair}} \approx 0.4702$ and a which-factor information of $\approx 0.0001$. The exact values are
$$H(T) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac23 = 0.9182958\ldots < 0.9187, \qquad I_{\mathrm{pair}} = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9} = 0.4738514\ldots > 0.4737,$$
and the which-factor information is exactly $0$. The first two discrepancies are of the size expected from finite-sample plug-in entropy estimation (which is biased downward for $H$ and can be biased in either direction for $I$); the third is the classical positive bias of plug-in mutual-information estimators, which report a small positive number where the truth is zero. We record the corrections because the exact values are rational-plus-logarithmic and admit clean proofs.

## 2. Definitions

We work with finite probability spaces throughout; all entropies are in bits.

**Definition 2.1 (Entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information).** For a random variable $X$ on a finite set with law $(p_v)$,
$$H(X) = -\sum_v p_v \log_2 p_v, \qquad H(X\mid Y) = \sum_y \Pr[Y = y]\,H(X \mid Y = y),$$
$$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X) \ge 0 .$$
We write $h(x) = -x\log_2 x - (1-x)\log_2(1-x)$ for the binary entropy function, with $h(0) = h(1) = 0$.

**Definition 2.2 (Rényi entropy).** For $a \ge 0$, $a \ne 1$, $H_a(X) = \frac{1}{1-a}\log_2 \sum_v p_v^{\,a}$; $H_1$ is defined by continuity and equals $H$.

**Definition 2.3 (The residue group and the type map).** Let $f$ be a prime and $d \mid f-1$. The Artin map identifies $\operatorname{Gal}(K_{f,d}/\mathbb{Q})$ with the quotient
$$\Gamma_d = (\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times \big/ \big((\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times\big)^d \;\cong\; C_d,$$
where $K_{f,d}$ is the unique degree-$d$ subfield of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_f)$. For $p \ne f$ write $g(p) \in \Gamma_d$ for the image of $p \bmod f$. The **type** of $p$ is
$$T_d(p) = \operatorname{ord}\big(g(p)\big) \in \{e : e \mid d\},$$
the residue degree of any prime of $K_{f,d}$ above $p$; the prime splits into $d/T_d(p)$ primes. When $d = q$ is prime the type has two values, $1$ (*split*) and $q$ (*inert*), and we use those words.

**Definition 2.4 (The splitting-type channel).** Fix $d$. The **splitting-type channel** is the pair (input $R = p \bmod f \in (\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$, output $T = T_d(p)$) with $R$ uniform on $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$ and $T$ the deterministic function of $R$ described above. By Dirichlet's theorem this is the correct asymptotic model for a prime drawn uniformly from $\{p \le X : p \ne f\}$ as $X \to \infty$.

**Definition 2.5 (Uniform cover).** Let $\pi : A \to B$ be a surjection of finite sets equipped with the uniform measures. We call $\pi$ a **uniform cover** if all fibres $\pi^{-1}(b)$ have the same cardinality. A uniform cover is exactly a measure-preserving map between uniform spaces; consequently the push-forward of the uniform measure on $A$ is the uniform measure on $B$.

**Definition 2.6 (Semiprime model).** For $n = pq$ with $p, q$ distinct primes $\ne f$, drawn so that $g(p), g(q)$ are independent and uniform in $\Gamma_q$ (the correct model under Dirichlet equidistribution for factors drawn independently), the **observable** is the type $T(n)$ of the product, determined by $g(n) = g(p) + g(q)$; the **hidden pair** is the unordered multiset $\{T(p),T(q)\}$; the **hidden ordering** is the labelling of which of $p, q$ realises which type.

## 3. Full pinning at conductor 13

**Theorem 3.1 (Full Pinning).** Let $K$ be the cyclic cubic field of conductor $13$. Then for a prime $p \ne 13$:

1. $p$ splits completely in $K$ iff $p \bmod 13 \in \{1,5,8,12\}$, and is inert otherwise; there are exactly two types.
2. $\Pr[T = \textsf{split}] = 1/3$ and $\Pr[T = \textsf{inert}] = 2/3$.
3. $H(T) = \log_2 3 - 2/3 = 0.9182958\ldots$ bits.
4. $H(T \mid p \bmod 13) = 0$ and hence $I(p \bmod 13; T) = H(T)$ exactly.

*Proof sketch.* (1) The group $(\mathbb{Z}/13)^\times$ is cyclic of order $12$, generated by $2$. The cubes form the unique subgroup of order $4$, namely $\{1,5,8,12\}$ (indeed $2^3 = 8$, $2^6 = 12$, $2^9 = 5$, $2^{12}=1$). The Artin map sends $p \bmod 13$ to its class in the quotient $C_3$; $p$ splits iff that class is trivial, i.e. iff $p$ is a cube. Only $p=13$ ramifies. (2) Four of the twelve classes are cubes, and residues are equidistributed. (3) $-\frac13\log_2\frac13 - \frac23\log_2\frac23 = \frac13\log_2 3 + \frac23(\log_2 3 - 1) = \log_2 3 - \frac23$. (4) $T$ is a function of $R$, so conditioning on $R$ leaves a point mass; every conditional entropy term vanishes. $\square$

**Remark 3.2.** Part (4) is what we call *full pinning*: the channel is lossless. There is no approximation, no error term, and in particular no dependence on how the primes are ordered or truncated, provided the sampling is equidistributed.

**Proposition 3.3 (Arithmetic grounding).** The four cubes mod $13$ are the fourth powers... more precisely, $\{x^3 : x \in (\mathbb{Z}/13)^\times\} = \{1,5,8,12\}$ is the image of the cubing endomorphism, a subgroup of index $3$; the three cosets are $\{1,5,8,12\}$, $\{2,3,10,11\}$, $\{4,6,7,9\}$. Products of residues multiply cosets according to $C_3$.

*Proof sketch.* Direct verification in the cyclic group of order $12$ generated by $2$: the cubes are the elements $2^{3k}$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Conductor independence of the cubic channel).** Let $f, f'$ be primes with $3 \mid f-1$ and $3 \mid f'-1$. Then the cubic splitting-type channels at conductors $f$ and $f'$ have identical output distributions $(1/3, 2/3)$, identical entropy $\log_2 3 - 2/3$, and both are fully pinned.

*Proof sketch.* In each case the quotient map $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times \to \Gamma_3 \cong C_3$ is a group homomorphism onto a group of order $3$; the fibres are cosets of the kernel and therefore all of size $(f-1)/3$. So the map is a uniform cover of $C_3$ (Definition 2.5), and the push-forward of the uniform input law is the uniform law on $C_3$, independent of $f$. The type is *split* on the identity and *inert* on the other two elements, giving $(1/3,2/3)$ in all cases. $\square$

This is the first appearance of the mechanism that governs the entire paper: **the type distribution is a push-forward along a uniform cover, and uniform covers erase all information about the covering space.** Section 7 pushes this to its natural conclusion.

## 4. The semiprime pairing channel

Write $C_3 = \{0,1,2\}$ additively, so *split* $\leftrightarrow$ $0$ and *inert* $\leftrightarrow$ $\{1,2\}$, and $g(pq) = g(p) + g(q)$.

**Lemma 4.1 (Nine-configuration table).** With $g(p), g(q)$ independent uniform on $C_3$:

| hidden pair $\{T(p),T(q)\}$ | probability | conditional law of $T(n)$ | conditional entropy |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\{\textsf{split},\textsf{split}\}$ | $1/9$ | $\textsf{split}$ w.p. $1$ | $0$ |
| $\{\textsf{split},\textsf{inert}\}$ | $4/9$ | $\textsf{inert}$ w.p. $1$ | $0$ |
| $\{\textsf{inert},\textsf{inert}\}$ | $4/9$ | $\textsf{split}$ w.p. $1/2$ | $1$ |

Moreover the marginal of $T(n)$ is again $(1/3,2/3)$.

*Proof sketch.* $(0,0)$ has probability $1/9$ and sum $0$. The four configurations with exactly one zero coordinate have probability $4/9$ and nonzero sum. Among the four configurations with both coordinates nonzero, the sum vanishes exactly for $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$: two out of four. The marginal follows: $\Pr[T(n) = \textsf{split}] = 1/9 + 2/9 = 1/3$, as it must, since $g(n)$ is uniform on $C_3$ (the sum of an independent uniform with anything is uniform). $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Exact pairing information and rational defect).** For the cubic channel,
$$I\big(T(n); \{T(p),T(q)\}\big) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9} = 0.4738514\ldots \text{ bits},$$
$$H(T) - I_{\mathrm{pair}} = \tfrac49 \quad\text{exactly.}$$

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 4.1 the conditional entropy of the observable given the hidden pair is
$$H\big(T(n) \mid \{T(p),T(q)\}\big) = \tfrac19\cdot 0 + \tfrac49\cdot 0 + \tfrac49 \cdot 1 = \tfrac49 .$$
Since the marginal of $T(n)$ is $(1/3,2/3)$ we have $H(T(n)) = \log_2 3 - 2/3$, and
$$I_{\mathrm{pair}} = \big(\log_2 3 - \tfrac23\big) - \tfrac49 = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{10}{9}. \qquad \square$$

The defect is rational because the ambiguous configuration is an *exactly fair* binary choice. This is a low-degree accident, as the general formula shows.

**Theorem 4.3 (Prime-degree defect formula).** Let $q$ be a prime and consider the degree-$q$ channel, so that $\Pr[\textsf{split}] = 1/q$. Then
$$H(T_q) = \log_2 q - \frac{q-1}{q}\log_2(q-1),$$
$$H(T_q) - I_{\mathrm{pair}}(q) = \frac{(q-1)^2}{q^2}\; h\!\left(\frac{1}{q-1}\right) = \frac{(q-1)^2}{q^2}\left[\log_2(q-1) - \frac{q-2}{q-1}\log_2(q-2)\right].$$

*Proof sketch.* The hidden pair is $\{\textsf{split},\textsf{split}\}$ with probability $1/q^2$ (observable determined), mixed with probability $2(q-1)/q^2$ (observable determined to be inert, since $0 + g \ne 0$ for $g \ne 0$), and $\{\textsf{inert},\textsf{inert}\}$ with probability $(q-1)^2/q^2$. In the last case, given $g(p) \ne 0$ there is exactly one nonzero value of $g(q)$ making the sum vanish, out of $q-1$ equally likely ones; so the observable is a Bernoulli$(1/(q-1))$ variable, of entropy $h(1/(q-1))$. The defect equals this conditional entropy. Setting $q=3$ recovers $\frac49 h(\frac12) = \frac49$; setting $q = 2$ gives $\frac14 h(1) = 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.4.** As $q \to \infty$, the defect behaves like $\frac{\log_2 q}{q}(1+o(1)) \to 0$ while $H(T_q) \sim \frac{\log_2 q}{q} \cdot 1 + \ldots \to 0$; more precisely both $H(T_q)$ and the defect tend to $0$, and their ratio tends to $1$: at large degree, *all* of the information is destroyed by pairing. Concretely $H(T_q) = \frac{\log_2 q}{q}\big(1+o(1)\big)$ and the defect is $\frac{\log_2 q}{q}\big(1+o(1)\big)$, so $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(q) = o\!\big(H(T_q)\big)$.

## 5. Rigidity: which defects are rational?

The values $4/9$ and $0$ above are rational; $H(T_3) = \log_2 3 - 2/3$ is not. We now prove that this pattern is forced.

**Lemma 5.1 (Integer-identity clearing).** Let $u > v \ge 1$ be coprime positive integers and let $s, t$ be positive integers. If $s\log_2 u - t\log_2 v \in \mathbb{Q}$, then there are integers $A$ and $b \ge 1$ with
$$u^{\,sb} = 2^{A}\, v^{\,tb}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Write the rational value as $A/b$ with $b \ge 1$. Then $\log_2\!\big(u^{s}/v^{t}\big) = A/b$, so $u^{sb}/v^{tb} = 2^{A}$; clearing denominators (moving a negative power of $2$ to the other side if needed) gives the displayed identity with $A \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\square$

**Lemma 5.2 (Coprime power obstruction).** Let $u > v \ge 1$ be coprime with $u$ having an odd prime factor, and let $s,t,b \ge 1$. Then $u^{sb} = 2^{A}v^{tb}$ is impossible for any $A \in \mathbb{Z}$, unless $v$ also carries the same odd prime — which coprimality forbids. Symmetrically, if $u$ is a power of $2$ and $v > 1$ is odd, the identity is impossible.

*Proof sketch.* Let $\ell$ be an odd prime dividing $u$. Then $\ell \mid u^{sb}$ with multiplicity $\ge sb \ge 1$. On the right, $\ell \nmid 2^{A}$ and $\ell \nmid v$ by coprimality, so $\ell \nmid 2^A v^{tb}$. Contradiction. For the second statement, an odd prime dividing $v$ divides the right side but not the left. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Rational-defect rigidity).** Let $q$ be a prime.

1. $H(T_q) = \log_2 q - \frac{q-1}{q}\log_2(q-1)$ is rational **iff** $q = 2$ (value $1$). In particular $\log_2 3 - 2/3$ is irrational.
2. The pairing defect $H(T_q) - I_{\mathrm{pair}}(q)$ is rational **iff** $q \in \{2,3\}$, with values $0$ and $4/9$ respectively.

*Proof sketch.* (1) Suppose $H(T_q) \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $q$ is odd. Multiplying by $q$, the quantity $q\log_2 q - (q-1)\log_2(q-1)$ is rational. Apply Lemma 5.1 with $u = q$, $v = q-1$ (consecutive, hence coprime), $s = q$, $t = q-1$: we obtain $q^{qb} = 2^{A}(q-1)^{(q-1)b}$ with $b \ge 1$. Since $q$ is an odd prime, Lemma 5.2 applies and yields a contradiction. For $q = 2$: $H(T_2) = 1 - \frac12\log_2 1 = 1$.

(2) By Theorem 4.3 the defect is $\frac{(q-1)^2}{q^2}B_q$ with $B_q = \log_2(q-1) - \frac{q-2}{q-1}\log_2(q-2)$, and the prefactor is a nonzero rational, so the defect is rational iff $B_q$ is. For $q = 2$, $B_2 = h(1) = 0$. For $q = 3$, $B_3 = \log_2 2 - \frac12 \log_2 1 = 1$, giving the defect $4/9$. For $q \ge 5$, multiply $B_q$ by $q-1$: rationality of $B_q$ forces $(q-1)\log_2(q-1) - (q-2)\log_2(q-2)$ rational, and Lemma 5.1 with $u = q-1 \ge 4$, $v = q-2 \ge 3$ (again consecutive, hence coprime) yields
$$(q-1)^{(q-1)b} = 2^{A}(q-2)^{(q-2)b}.$$
Now split cases. If $q-1$ has an odd prime factor, Lemma 5.2 gives a contradiction directly. Otherwise $q-1 = 2^k$ is a power of two, so the left side is a power of two; but then $q - 2 = 2^k - 1 \ge 3$ is odd and $>1$, and any odd prime dividing it divides the right side and not the left — contradiction again. $\square$

**Remark 5.4.** No transcendence theory (no linear forms in logarithms, no Baker's theorem) is required: the argument is unique factorisation plus the coprimality of consecutive integers. This makes the rigidity statement completely elementary, and simultaneously explains *why* $q \in \{2,3\}$ escapes — those are precisely the degrees for which the surviving logarithm has argument $1$ or $2$, i.e. degenerates.

**Corollary 5.5.** The conductor-$13$ entropy $\log_2 3 - 2/3$ and the pairing information $\log_2 3 - 10/9$ are both irrational, while their difference is exactly $4/9$. Thus the *defect* is an exactly computable rational invariant of the channel even though neither of the two entropies it separates is.

## 6. The which-factor wall

The pairing analysis of Section 4 concerns the *unordered* pair of types. We now examine the residual question: which of the two factors carries which type?

**Definition 6.1.** Let $n = pq$ with the labelling of the factors as "first" and "second" chosen uniformly at random and independently of everything else — equivalently, work with the ordered pair $(g(p), g(q))$ and take $W \in \{0,1\}$ to be the indicator that the *first* factor is the split one, conditioned on the hidden pair being mixed. The **observable** $\mathcal{O}$ consists of the product class $g(n)$, the induced type $T(n)$, and the unordered pair $\{T(p),T(q)\}$ — indeed of any function of $n$ alone.

**Theorem 6.2 (Which-factor wall).** $I(\mathcal{O}; W) = 0$ exactly.

*Proof sketch.* The transposition $\sigma : (g(p), g(q)) \mapsto (g(q), g(p))$ is a measure-preserving involution of the hidden state space (the law is exchangeable). It fixes every observable: the product class $g(p)+g(q)$ is symmetric, hence so is $T(n)$, hence so is $n$ itself; and the unordered pair of types is symmetric by definition. It exchanges the two values of $W$. Therefore, conditionally on any value of $\mathcal{O}$, the law of $W$ is invariant under the swap of its two values, hence uniform, hence $H(W \mid \mathcal{O}) = H(W) = 1$ bit and $I = 0$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.3 (Decoder half-law).** Any decoder $\hat W = D(\mathcal{O})$, deterministic or randomised, satisfies $\Pr[\hat W = W] = 1/2$ exactly. In particular there is no strategy better than a coin flip, and none worse.

*Proof sketch.* For each observation, the conditional law of $W$ is uniform on two values, so every decision has conditional success probability exactly $1/2$; averaging preserves the value. Note the unusual two-sided conclusion: the wall is not merely an upper bound on the success rate, it pins the rate. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (General cyclic wall).** Let a finite group $G$ act on a hidden label set $L$ freely and transitively, and let the joint law of (hidden state, observable) be $G$-invariant with the observable $G$-fixed. Then the mutual information between the observable and the hidden label is exactly $0$, and the optimal decoder attains exactly $1/|L|$.

*Proof sketch.* Fix an observation $o$. The conditional law of the label is a probability measure on $L$ invariant under the action of $G$ (because the joint law is invariant and $o$ is fixed). A $G$-invariant measure on a set with a transitive action is uniform; freeness makes $|L| = |G|$. Hence $H(\text{label} \mid \mathcal{O} = o) = \log_2 |L|$ for every $o$, so the conditional entropy equals the unconditional entropy and the mutual information vanishes. The decoder statement follows as in Corollary 6.3. $\square$

Theorem 6.2 is the case $G = C_2$; the cyclic version covers hidden labellings of $k$ factors permuted cyclically, and shows that *no* congruence observable of a product can distinguish its factors' roles.

**Remark 6.5 (Interpretation).** The wall separates two kinds of ignorance. The $4/9$-bit pairing defect is *quantitative*: information exists in the joint distribution and is merely partially transmitted. The which-factor zero is *structural*: no information exists at all, because the observable is a function on the quotient by the symmetry group, and the hidden variable is a coordinate along the group orbit. Numerically the two look similar (a small number, a smaller number); mathematically they are different species. Any experiment reporting a small nonzero which-factor information (e.g. $10^{-4}$) is reporting estimator bias, not signal.

## 7. Invariance and the whole Rényi family

**Proposition 7.1 (Push-forward under uniform covers).** Let $\pi : A \to B$ be a uniform cover of finite sets carrying uniform measures, and let $\tau : B \to V$ be any map ("type"). Then the push-forward laws of $\tau \circ \pi$ and $\tau$ on $V$ coincide.

*Proof sketch.* $\Pr_A[\tau(\pi(a)) = v] = |\pi^{-1}(\tau^{-1}(v))|/|A| = c\,|\tau^{-1}(v)|/(c|B|) = \Pr_B[\tau(b)=v]$, where $c$ is the common fibre size. $\square$

**Theorem 7.2 (Functional invariance).** Under the hypotheses of Proposition 7.1, for *every* function $F : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}$,
$$\sum_{v \in V} F\big(\Pr[\tau\circ\pi = v]\big) = \sum_{v\in V} F\big(\Pr[\tau = v]\big).$$
Consequently Shannon entropy, all Rényi entropies, all min- and max-entropies, all $\ell^a$ norms and all moments of the type distribution are invariant.

*Proof sketch.* Immediate from equality of the push-forward probabilities, which is a stronger statement than equality of any single functional. $\square$

This is why the conductor drops out: the periodic transfer $(\mathbb{Z}/f')^\times \to (\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times \to \Gamma$ arising from divisibility of moduli, and more generally the quotient $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times \to \Gamma_d$, are uniform covers of $\Gamma_d$.

**Theorem 7.3 (Rényi spectrum of the cubic channel).** For every admissible conductor and every order $a \ne 1$,
$$H_a(T) = \frac{1}{1-a}\log_2\left(\left(\tfrac13\right)^{a} + \left(\tfrac23\right)^{a}\right).$$
In particular $H_0 = 1$ bit, $H_1 = \log_2 3 - 2/3$, $H_2 = \log_2 \frac95 = 0.8479969\ldots$, and $H_\infty = \log_2\frac32 = 0.5849625\ldots$. Moreover $H_2 < H_1$, and this inequality is equivalent to the integer inequality $108 < 125$.

*Proof sketch.* The formula is Definition 2.2 applied to $(1/3,2/3)$, and is conductor-independent by Theorem 7.2. $H_0 = \log_2 2 = 1$ counts the support. $H_2 = -\log_2(1/9+4/9) = \log_2(9/5)$. For the ordering, $H_2 < H_1$ reads $\log_2 9 - \log_2 5 < \log_2 3 - 2/3$, i.e. $\log_2 3 + 2/3 < \log_2 5$; multiplying by $3$ gives $\log_2 27 + 2 < \log_2 125$, i.e. $\log_2 108 < \log_2 125$, i.e. $108 < 125$. $\square$

**Remark 7.4.** The reduction of a strict inequality between two irrational entropies to a comparison of two integers is a useful pattern: monotonicity of Rényi entropy in the order is a general theorem, but here we obtain a *certificate* — the slack $125 - 108 = 17$ — that is checkable by inspection.

## 8. The subfield tower as an information filtration

Let $f = 13$. The subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{13})$ are indexed by the divisors $d$ of $12$, one for each degree $d \in \{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$, and are totally ordered by divisibility of $d$. The type $T_d$ of Definition 2.3 is the order of $g(p)$ in $\Gamma_d \cong C_d$, and its law is
$$\Pr[T_d = e] = \frac{\varphi(e)}{d} \qquad (e \mid d),$$
since a cyclic group of order $d$ has exactly $\varphi(e)$ elements of order $e$. Hence
$$H(T_d) = -\sum_{e \mid d} \frac{\varphi(e)}{d}\log_2\frac{\varphi(e)}{d}.$$

**Proposition 8.1 (Filtration).** If $d \mid d'$ then $T_d$ is a function of $T_{d'}$ up to the natural projection $\Gamma_{d'} \to \Gamma_d$, and $H(T_d) \le H(T_{d'})$. Observing in a larger subfield never loses information.

*Proof sketch.* The projection of cyclic groups $C_{d'} \to C_d$ intertwines the two type maps in the sense that the order in the quotient divides the order upstairs and is determined by the image; the entropy inequality is the data-processing inequality for the deterministic map $g \mapsto \bar g$. $\square$

**Theorem 8.2 (CRT additivity).** For coprime $m, n$, $H(T_{mn}) = H(T_m) + H(T_n)$.

*Proof sketch.* $C_{mn} \cong C_m \times C_n$ with the uniform measure factorising as a product, and the order of an element factorises as the product of the orders of its coordinates. So $T_{mn}$ is equivalent to the independent pair $(T_m, T_n)$, and entropy is additive on independent pairs. $\square$

**Theorem 8.3 (Prime-power saturation).** For a prime $p$ and $e \ge 1$,
$$H(T_{p^e}) = C(p)\left(1 - p^{-e}\right), \qquad C(p) = \frac{p\log_2 p}{p-1} - \log_2(p-1).$$

*Proof sketch.* The law of $T_{p^e}$ is supported on $1, p, \ldots, p^{e}$ with $\Pr[T = p^i] = \varphi(p^i)/p^e$, i.e. $p^{-e}$ for $i = 0$ and $(1-p^{-1})p^{i-e}$ for $1 \le i \le e$. Substituting into the entropy sum and evaluating the resulting finite geometric and arithmetico-geometric sums yields the closed form; the geometric structure produces the factor $1 - p^{-e}$, and the constant $C(p)$ is the $e \to \infty$ limit. $\square$

**Corollary 8.4 (The conductor-13 tower).** With $\eta = \log_2 3 - 2/3$:

| degree $d$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $6$ | $12$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $H(T_d)$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\eta \approx 0.918296$ | $3/2$ | $1+\eta \approx 1.918296$ | $3/2 + \eta \approx 2.418296$ |

The rational gaps along the $2$-part are $H(T_2) - H(T_1) = 1$, $H(T_4)-H(T_2) = 1/2$, and $H(T_{12}) - H(T_3) = 3/2$; the $3$-direction gaps are all equal to the irrational $\eta$. Here $C(2) = 2$ and $C(3) = \frac32\log_2 3 - 1 = 1.3774438\ldots$, so the $2$-tower saturates at $2$ bits ($1, \frac32, \frac74, \frac{15}{8},\dots$) and the $3$-tower at $C(3)$ ($\eta, \frac89 C(3) = 1.224393\ldots, \dots$).

*Proof sketch.* Combine Theorems 8.2 and 8.3 with $12 = 4\cdot 3$ and $6 = 2 \cdot 3$. $\square$

**Remark 8.5.** The filtration makes precise the sense in which "more field means more bits": each rung is a refinement of the observation, and the increments are exactly computable. The rationality pattern — rational in the $2$-direction, irrational in the $3$-direction — is Theorem 5.3 again, since $C(2) = 2$ is a degenerate case ($\log_2(p-1) = 0$) whereas $C(3)$ involves $\log_2 3$.

## 9. Algorithms

We record the three computational procedures the theory suggests. All are elementary; the point is that each computes an *exact* quantity.

**Algorithm A (Splitting-type oracle).** Given a prime $p$ and admissible prime conductor $f$, decide $T(p)$ in $O(\log p)$ bit operations for the reduction plus $O(\log f \cdot M(\log f))$ for one modular exponentiation:
1. $r \leftarrow p \bmod f$; if $r = 0$ return *ramified*.
2. Compute $c \leftarrow r^{(f-1)/3} \bmod f$ (Euler's criterion for cubic residuacity).
3. If $c = 1$ return *split*; else return *inert*.
Correctness: $r$ is a cube iff $r^{(f-1)/3} \equiv 1$, because the multiplicative group is cyclic of order $f-1$ and the cubes are the kernel of the $(f-1)/3$-power map composed appropriately. For $f = 13$, step 2 is a table lookup against $\{1,5,8,12\}$.

**Algorithm B (Exact channel statistics).** Given $f$ and $d \mid f-1$, produce the exact type law and its entropies:
1. Enumerate $(\mathbb{Z}/f)^\times$ and compute $g(x)$ for each $x$ (discrete log modulo $d$).
2. Bin the residues by $\operatorname{ord}(g(x))$; the bin sizes are $\varphi(e)\cdot\frac{f-1}{d}$.
3. Return the law $\big(\varphi(e)/d\big)_{e\mid d}$ and the entropies $H$, $H_a$.
Complexity $O(f\log f)$ naively, or $O(d)$ using the closed form $\varphi(e)/d$ — which is exactly the content of Theorem 3.4: one never needs to touch $f$ at all.

**Algorithm C (Semiprime pairing analysis).** For a prime degree $q$, compute $H(T_q)$, $I_{\mathrm{pair}}(q)$ and the defect exactly:
1. Build the $q \times q$ joint table of $(g(p), g(q))$, each cell of mass $q^{-2}$.
2. Fold to the hidden pair (three classes: SS, SI, II) and the observable ($g(p)+g(q) = 0$?).
3. Return $H(\text{obs})$, $H(\text{obs}\mid\text{pair})$ and their difference.
Complexity $O(q^2)$, or $O(1)$ via Theorem 4.3. Step 3's middle quantity is always $\frac{(q-1)^2}{q^2}h(\frac{1}{q-1})$; the algorithm is best used as a machine check of the closed form.

## 10. Applications and interpretation

**10.1 Sanity limits for factoring heuristics.** The which-factor wall is a rigorous, if modest, instance of the intuition that congruence data about $n$ is useless for separating its factors. Any procedure whose entire input is a set of local symbols of $n$ (splitting types in abelian extensions, Legendre/Jacobi-style symbols, residue classes) is a function on the quotient by factor permutation, hence subject to Theorem 6.4. Progress on factoring must break the invariance — as, for instance, sieve methods do by working with the ring structure rather than with symbols of $n$ alone.

**10.2 Exact benchmarks for entropy estimation.** The channel is a rare source of test problems with *closed-form* answers of three distinct flavours: an irrational entropy ($\log_2 3 - 2/3$), an irrational mutual information ($\log_2 3 - 10/9$), and a structural zero. An estimator that returns $0.9192$ for the first and $10^{-4}$ for the third is exhibiting precisely the two textbook biases (downward for plug-in entropy, upward for plug-in mutual information); the channel makes these visible with no modelling assumptions.

**10.3 Arithmetic as a source of exactly solvable channels.** Uniform covers (Theorem 7.2) let one transport a full information profile — every Rényi order at once — from a small quotient to any covering situation. Since Galois theory supplies uniform covers in abundance (any surjection of abelian Galois groups), the profile of a splitting-type channel is a property of the *Galois group and the type function*, never of the specific conductor. In particular, tables of such invariants may be computed once per group.

**10.4 Filtrations and side channels.** The subfield tower gives a concrete example of a nested family of observations with exactly known information content and exactly known increments. It is a useful toy model whenever one wants to reason about how much an adversary gains from moving to a finer observation, because both the total and the marginal gains are closed-form.

## 11. Discussion

Three features of this analysis deserve emphasis.

*Exactness.* Every quantity in the paper is exact. There are no asymptotic constants, no error terms, and no numerically-estimated values. Where a number is irrational we prove it irrational; where it is rational we exhibit the fraction; where it is zero we prove it zero by symmetry. This is possible because the underlying randomness is uniform on a finite abelian group, and all the maps involved are group homomorphisms or coarsenings thereof.

*Two species of information loss.* The pairing defect and the which-factor wall are both "loss", but they arise differently. The defect is a genuine conditional entropy: the ambiguous configuration ($\{$inert, inert$\}$) really is uncertain, and the uncertainty is a fair coin worth one bit, occurring with probability $4/9$. The wall is the vanishing of an information quantity for structural reasons — the observable factors through the quotient by a group that acts transitively on the unknown. It is worth insisting on the distinction because they respond differently to intervention: the defect shrinks if you obtain additional observables correlated with the ambiguous configuration, whereas the wall is impervious to *any* observable that respects the symmetry.

*Rationality as a low-degree accident.* The value $4/9$ is not a hint of hidden structure at degree $3$; Theorem 5.3 shows it is the last such value. The rationality obstruction is completely elementary — clear denominators, then use unique factorisation and the coprimality of consecutive integers — and it is pleasing that no transcendence theory is needed. The same phenomenon explains why the $2$-part of the subfield tower has rational entropies (the surviving logarithm has argument $1$) while the $3$-part does not.

A conceptual summary: the splitting-type channel of a cyclic field is a *lossless* channel from the residue class, a *lossy but exactly computable* channel from a product to the multiset of factor types, and a *totally uninformative* channel to the ordering of the factors. The three regimes — perfect, quantified, and structurally void — sit side by side in an object as small as the cubic field of conductor $13$.

## 12. Future work

Several directions remain open, and several posed earlier have now been settled.

**Settled.** (i) *Rational-defect rigidity*: the semiprime pairing defect at prime degree $q$ is rational precisely for $q \in \{2,3\}$, and the type entropy precisely for $q = 2$; the proof needs no linear forms in logarithms. (ii) *Full functional invariance*: not merely the Shannon entropy but every functional of the type distribution — hence the entire Rényi family — is conductor-independent, because the invariance is distributional rather than entropic. (iii) *The cyclic which-factor wall*: a free transitive action on the hidden label fixing the observable forces exactly zero mutual information, for cyclic groups of any order, not just the transposition case.

**Open.**

1. *Non-abelian channels.* For a non-abelian Galois extension the splitting type is a conjugacy class of Frobenius, and Chebotarev replaces Dirichlet; the type law is $(|C|/|G|)_C$. The same information questions can be posed: what is the analogue of full pinning (the "input" is now the Frobenius class, not a residue), and does an analogue of Theorem 7.2 hold along surjections of Galois groups? The uniform-cover mechanism should survive verbatim; the pairing analysis will not, since conjugacy classes do not multiply.

2. *Higher arity.* We analysed $n = pq$. For $n = p_1\cdots p_k$, the observable is the sum of $k$ uniform elements of $C_q$ and the hidden object is the multiset of types. The defect should have a closed form in terms of the distribution of the number of nontrivial summands; the which-factor wall generalises immediately by Theorem 6.4, but the *partial* orderings (e.g. "is $p_1$ split?") deserve separate treatment.

3. *Mixed towers and joint channels.* Observing simultaneously in several subfields of different conductors gives a joint channel; CRT additivity handles coprime degrees within one cyclotomic field, but the general joint entropy across independent conductors, and its interaction with the pairing defect, is unexplored.

4. *Effective equidistribution.* All statements here are for the idealised uniform model. Making them effective — bounding $|H(T \text{ on } p \le X) - H(T)|$ in terms of $X$ and $f$, unconditionally and under GRH — would turn exact identities into usable finite-$X$ inequalities and would quantify precisely how large a sample must be before an empirical estimate can distinguish $0.9183$ from $0.9192$.

5. *Defect spectra.* Theorem 4.3 gives the prime-degree defect. For composite degree $d$ the type is the order of a group element and the pairing analysis becomes a convolution on $C_d$; computing the resulting defect in closed form, and determining for which $d$ it is rational, is the natural sequel to Theorem 5.3.

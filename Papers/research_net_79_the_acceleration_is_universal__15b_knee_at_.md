## Abstract

We develop a rigorous theory of the *knee* of a positive attention profile — the least number of top-ranked keys whose mass clears a prescribed retention gate — and use it to explain, in exact mathematics, a phenomenon observed empirically in long-context memory budgeting: the ordering of two key-budget curves can *invert* as the context length grows, so that no profile is uniformly more memory-efficient than another.

Four groups of results are established. **(1) Ratio calculus.** For the one-parameter family of geometric profiles $w_i = r^i$, the retained mass is antitone in the decay ratio $r$ pointwise in the pair (context, budget); the proof is a rearrangement inequality on a head/tail double sum, not an analytic argument. Consequently the knee is monotone in $r$, uniformly in the context length, and a two-sided quantitative knee calculus follows: the exact certificate $r^K \le 1-\tau$ yields a context-free budget, while the flatness estimate $k/(n r^{n-1}) < \tau$ certifies failure. **(2) Pythagorean rigidity.** Attaching to a Pythagorean triple $a^2+b^2=c^2$ the decay ratios $a/c$ and $b/c$ places them on the unit circle. This forces $(a/c)^2 \le 1/2$ for the short leg and hence a *universal* budget of $13$ keys at gate $\tau = 0.985$, valid for every triple and every context; the bound is sharp, being attained exactly by the near-isosceles triple $(696,697,985)$ at context $64$. No such bound exists for long legs. Two triples can never be compared uniformly: the one with the smaller short-leg budget has the larger long-leg budget. **(3) The Pell branch.** Along the near-isosceles family $(3,4,5) \to (20,21,29) \to (119,120,169) \to (696,697,985) \to \cdots$, generated by $(a,c)\mapsto(3a+2c+1,4a+3c+2)$ and characterised by the invariant $c^2 = 2a^2+2a+1$, the two leg budgets squeeze monotonically towards each other at every context and gate, with measured gaps $10, 2, 1, 0$, meeting at the universal value $13$. **(4) Surface structure and crossover localisation.** A measured two-scale $\times$ four-context budget surface is shown to be neither additively nor multiplicatively separable, with an acceleration factor of $4$ at one scale and $19$ at the other and a sign change of the scale gap at the last grid step. Knee-ordering inversion is realizable by explicit honest profiles, is *equivalent* to a crossing of the retained-mass curves, and is localised: if $v$ has a context-free budget $K$ and $w$ is band-limited to $[c,M]$ with $c>0$, the inversion has occurred at every context $n \ge (K+1)M/(\tau c)$.

**Keywords:** retained mass, attention budget, knee, geometric decay, rearrangement inequality, Pythagorean triple, Pell branch, phase transition, crossover localisation.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The engineering question

A sequence model that attends over a context of length $n$ produces, for each query, a distribution of attention over the $n$ available keys. Retaining all $n$ keys is expensive in fast memory; retaining a top-$k$ subset is cheap. The natural question — *how large must $k$ be?* — is answered by a single integer once one fixes a quality criterion. Take the criterion to be that the retained keys carry at least a $\tau$-fraction of the total attention mass. The least such $k$ is the **knee**.

Empirically, knees measured on a grid of model scales and context lengths behave badly. A representative measured surface at gate $\tau = 0.985$ is

| scale | @512 | @1024 | @2048 | @4096 | increments |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| small | 16 | 20 | 24 | **40** | $+4, +4, +16$ |
| large | 16 | 16 | 18 | **56** | $0, +2, +38$ |

Two features stand out. The final increment is $4\times$ the previous one at the small scale and $19\times$ at the large scale: acceleration *amplifies* with scale rather than being delayed by it. And the ordering of the two rows reverses: the large scale is cheaper at contexts $\le 2048$ and dearer at $4096$.

### 1.2 What this paper proves

We do not attempt to derive measured numbers from first principles; the measured table is treated purely as a pair of four-point integer sequences, and every statement about it is a statement about those sequences. What we do prove is that the *qualitative* phenomena are exactly realizable, structurally forced, and quantitatively localisable within a clean theory of retained-mass knees, and that a strikingly rigid instance of the same phenomena lives inside elementary Pythagorean arithmetic.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 fixes definitions and the basic bracket calculus for knees. Section 3 establishes the ratio calculus for geometric profiles, including the rearrangement inequality that is the technical core. Section 4 develops the Pythagorean theory: the universal short-leg budget, its sharpness, the unboundedness of long-leg budgets and the forced inversion. Section 5 treats the Pell branch and the convergence of the two leg budgets. Section 6 analyses the measured surface and proves the realizability, rigidity and localisation results for inversions. Section 7 gives algorithms, Section 8 applications, Section 9 discussion and open problems.

## 2. Framework

### 2.1 Profiles, retained mass, knees

**Definition 2.1 (Attention profile).** An *attention profile* is a sequence $w : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $w_i > 0$ for all $i$. We interpret $w_i$ as the attention mass carried by the $i$-th most heavily attended key, so profiles are thought of as already sorted, though no monotonicity is assumed anywhere in the theory.

**Definition 2.2 (Head mass).** The *head mass* of $w$ at $n$ is $H_w(n) = \sum_{i < n} w_i$. For $n \ge 1$ and a positive profile, $H_w(n) > 0$.

**Definition 2.3 (Retained mass).** For a context length $n \ge 1$ and a key budget $k \ge 0$,
$$R_w(n,k) \;=\; \frac{H_w(\min(k,n))}{H_w(n)} \;\in\; [0,1].$$
The truncation at $\min(k,n)$ encodes that one cannot retain more keys than exist. In particular $R_w(n,n) = 1$ and $R_w(n,0) = 0$.

**Definition 2.4 (Gate and knee).** Fix a *gate* $\tau \le 1$. The *knee* is
$$k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) \;=\; \min\{\, k \in \mathbb{N} : R_w(n,k) \ge \tau \,\},$$
which is well-defined because $k=n$ always qualifies when $\tau \le 1$.

### 2.2 Bracket calculus

Three elementary facts are used throughout and we record them once.

**Lemma 2.5 (The knee passes).** For a positive profile, $n \ge 1$ and $\tau \le 1$, one has $\tau \le R_w(n, k^{\star}(w,n,\tau))$.

**Lemma 2.6 (Pass bounds the knee).** If $\tau \le R_w(n,k)$ then $k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) \le k$. Contrapositively, if $R_w(n,k) < \tau$ then $k < k^{\star}(w,n,\tau)$.

**Lemma 2.7 (Bracket).** If $R_w(n,K-1) < \tau \le R_w(n,K)$, then $k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) = K$.

*Proof.* Lemma 2.6 applied twice gives $K - 1 < k^{\star} \le K$. $\square$

Every exact knee value in this paper is certified by a bracket — a *pass* and a matching *fail* — so no quantitative claim is one-sided.

**Lemma 2.8 (Band lower bound).** Suppose $0 < c \le w_i \le M$ for all $i$, $n \ge 1$ and $\tau \le 1$. Then
$$k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) \;\ge\; \frac{\tau n c}{M}.$$

*Proof.* Write $k = k^{\star}(w,n,\tau)$. By Lemma 2.5, $H_w(\min(k,n)) \ge \tau H_w(n) \ge \tau n c$. On the other hand $H_w(\min(k,n)) \le \min(k,n) M \le kM$. Combining, $kM \ge \tau n c$. $\square$

Lemma 2.8 is the source of every *lower* bound on a knee in this paper, and its linear growth in $n$ is the mechanism behind every phase transition we exhibit.

## 3. Geometric profiles and the ratio calculus

**Definition 3.1 (Geometric profile).** For $0 < r < 1$, the *geometric profile with decay ratio $r$* is $g_r(i) = r^i$. Its head mass is $H_{g_r}(n) = \sum_{i<n} r^i$.

Small $r$ concentrates attention on the first keys; $r \to 1^-$ flattens the profile across the context.

### 3.1 The rearrangement inequality

**Lemma 3.2 (Cross-term rearrangement).** Let $0 < r \le s$ and $m \le n$. Then
$$\Big(\sum_{i<m} s^i\Big)\Big(\sum_{m \le j < n} r^j\Big) \;\le\; \Big(\sum_{i<m} r^i\Big)\Big(\sum_{m \le j < n} s^j\Big).$$

*Proof sketch.* Expand both sides as double sums over $(i,j) \in [0,m) \times [m,n)$ and compare termwise. For each such pair, $i < m \le j$, so $i \le j$; write $j = i + d$ with $d \ge 0$. Then
$$s^i r^{i+d} = (r^i s^i) r^d \;\le\; (r^i s^i) s^d = r^i s^{i+d},$$
using $r^d \le s^d$ and $r^i s^i \ge 0$. Summing the termwise inequalities gives the claim. $\square$

The hypothesis $i \le j$ is supplied for free by the head/tail split, and is precisely what the naive "compare numerator and denominator separately" argument lacks.

**Theorem 3.3 (Retained mass is antitone in the ratio).** Let $0 < r \le s$, $n \ge 1$ and $k \ge 0$. Then
$$R_{g_s}(n,k) \;\le\; R_{g_r}(n,k).$$

*Proof sketch.* Put $m = \min(k,n) \le n$. Both denominators $H_{g_r}(n), H_{g_s}(n)$ are positive, so the claim is equivalent after cross-multiplication to
$$H_{g_s}(m)\, H_{g_r}(n) \;\le\; H_{g_r}(m)\, H_{g_s}(n).$$
Split each of $H_{g_r}(n), H_{g_s}(n)$ as head-plus-tail at $m$. The head-head products $H_{g_s}(m)H_{g_r}(m)$ cancel on both sides, leaving exactly the inequality of Lemma 3.2. $\square$

Theorem 3.3 is strictly stronger than monotonicity of the knee: it holds pointwise in $(n,k)$, and it is what makes all subsequent comparisons uniform in the context length.

**Theorem 3.4 (The knee is monotone in the ratio).** Let $0 < r \le s < 1$, $n \ge 1$ and $\tau \le 1$. Then
$$k^{\star}(g_r, n, \tau) \;\le\; k^{\star}(g_s, n, \tau).$$

*Proof.* Let $K = k^{\star}(g_s,n,\tau)$. By Lemma 2.5, $\tau \le R_{g_s}(n,K)$, and by Theorem 3.3, $R_{g_s}(n,K) \le R_{g_r}(n,K)$. So $K$ passes for $g_r$, and Lemma 2.6 gives $k^{\star}(g_r,n,\tau) \le K$. $\square$

Thus *steepness is a total order on budgets*: any inversion of decay ratios becomes an inversion of key budgets, uniformly in context and gate. This single implication is used in Sections 4, 5 and 6.

### 3.2 Exact and two-sided control

**Theorem 3.5 (Closed form).** For $0 < r < 1$ and $n \ge 1$,
$$R_{g_r}(n,k) \;=\; \frac{1 - r^{\min(k,n)}}{1 - r^{n}}.$$

*Proof.* Both head masses are finite geometric sums; substitute $\sum_{i<m} r^i = (r^m-1)/(r-1)$ in numerator and denominator and simplify, which is legitimate since $r \ne 1$ and $r^n \ne 1$. $\square$

**Corollary 3.6 (Exact universal certificate).** For $0 < r < 1$, $n \ge 1$ and $k \ge 0$ we have $1 - r^k \le R_{g_r}(n,k)$. Consequently, if $r^K \le 1 - \tau$ then
$$k^{\star}(g_r,n,\tau) \le K \quad \text{for every } n \ge 1.$$

*Proof sketch.* For $k \le n$, $\frac{1-r^k}{1-r^n} \ge 1 - r^k$ since $0 < 1 - r^n \le 1$; for $k > n$ the retained mass is $1 \ge 1-r^k$. The consequence is immediate from Lemma 2.6. $\square$

We call this the *exact* certificate to distinguish it from the generic tail-sum certificate valid for any profile dominated by geometric decay, namely $r^K/(1-r) \le 1-\tau$. The latter loses a factor $1/(1-r)$; Section 4 quantifies the loss (it is the difference between $16$ and $13$ at the gate $\tau = 0.985$).

**Lemma 3.7 (Flatness upper estimate).** For $0 < r \le 1$, $n \ge 1$, $k \ge 0$,
$$R_{g_r}(n,k) \;\le\; \frac{k}{n\, r^{\,n-1}}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Each of the $\min(k,n) \le k$ numerator terms is at most $1$; each of the $n$ denominator terms is at least $r^{n-1}$ since $r \le 1$ and the exponents are $< n$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.8 (Failure certificate; flat profiles have no uniform budget).** If $0 < r \le 1$, $n \ge 1$, $\tau \le 1$ and $k/(n r^{n-1}) < \tau$, then $k < k^{\star}(g_r,n,\tau)$.

*Proof.* Combine Lemma 3.7 with Lemma 2.6. $\square$

**Theorem 3.9 (Exact knee by bracket).** Let $0 < r < 1$, $1 \le K \le n$, $\tau \le 1$, and suppose
$$\frac{1-r^{K-1}}{1-r^{n}} \;<\; \tau \;\le\; \frac{1-r^{K}}{1-r^{n}}.$$
Then $k^{\star}(g_r,n,\tau) = K$.

*Proof.* Rewrite both inequalities via Theorem 3.5 as $R_{g_r}(n,K-1) < \tau \le R_{g_r}(n,K)$ and apply Lemma 2.7. $\square$

Theorems 3.4, 3.6, 3.8 and 3.9 together constitute the *knee calculus*: monotonicity in the ratio, a context-free upper certificate, a lower certificate, and an exact evaluation rule. Everything that follows is an application.

## 4. Pythagorean decay ratios

### 4.1 The two ratios of a triple

**Definition 4.1.** A triple of integers $(a,b,c)$ is *Pythagorean* if $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. For $c > 0$ define the *leg ratios* $\rho_a = a/c$ and $\rho_b = b/c$, and the associated geometric profiles $g_{\rho_a}, g_{\rho_b}$.

**Lemma 4.2 (Unit-circle constraint).** If $(a,b,c)$ is Pythagorean with $c > 0$, then $\rho_a^2 + \rho_b^2 = 1$. If moreover $b > 0$, then $\rho_a < 1$.

*Proof.* Divide $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ by $c^2 > 0$. Since $\rho_b > 0$, $\rho_a^2 = 1 - \rho_b^2 < 1$. $\square$

The pair of ratios is therefore not free: it is a point on a quarter circle, and one coordinate determines the other.

**Theorem 4.3 (The Pythagorean squeeze).** If $(a,b,c)$ is Pythagorean with $0 < a \le b$ and $c > 0$, then
$$\rho_a^2 \le \tfrac12, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \rho_a \le 0.708.$$

*Proof.* From $a \le b$ and $c>0$ we get $\rho_a \le \rho_b$, so $1 = \rho_a^2 + \rho_b^2 \ge 2\rho_a^2$. The numerical consequence follows from $0.708^2 = 0.501264 > 1/2$ and positivity of $\rho_a$. $\square$

### 4.2 A universal short-leg budget

**Theorem 4.4 (Universal Pythagorean key budget).** Let $(a,b,c)$ be any Pythagorean triple with $0 < a \le b$, $c > 0$, and let $n \ge 1$ be any context length. Then, at the gate $\tau = 0.985$,
$$k^{\star}\big(g_{a/c},\, n,\, 0.985\big) \;\le\; 13.$$

*Proof.* Write $r = a/c$. By Theorem 4.3, $r^2 \le 1/2$ and $r \le 0.708$; in particular $r < 1$. Then
$$r^{13} = (r^2)^6 \cdot r \;\le\; \left(\tfrac12\right)^{6}\cdot 0.708 \;=\; \frac{0.708}{64} = 0.0110625 \;\le\; 0.015 = 1 - 0.985 .$$
Corollary 3.6 with $K = 13$ concludes. $\square$

Three features deserve emphasis. First, the bound is *uniform in the triple*: no primitivity, no size restriction, no parametrisation is used, only $a \le b$ and the Pythagorean relation. Second, it is *uniform in the context*: the certificate of Corollary 3.6 has no $n$ in it. Third, it is *exact-certificate dependent*: the generic tail-sum certificate $r^K/(1-r) \le 1-\tau$ with $r = 0.708$ requires
$$0.708^K \le 0.015 \cdot 0.292 = 0.00438,$$
i.e. $K = 16$. The three-key gap between $16$ and $13$ is precisely the $1/(1-r)$ loss.

**Theorem 4.5 (Sharpness).** The universal budget $13$ cannot be lowered: the Pythagorean triple $(696,697,985)$ satisfies
$$k^{\star}\big(g_{696/985},\, 64,\, 0.985\big) = 13,$$
so $12$ keys fail at that triple and context.

*Proof sketch.* $696^2 + 697^2 = 484416 + 485809 = 970225 = 985^2$, so the triple is Pythagorean with consecutive legs. With $r = 696/985 = 0.7065989\ldots$ and $n = 64$ one has $r^{64} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-10}$, so the denominator $1-r^{64}$ is essentially $1$, and
$$\frac{1 - r^{12}}{1-r^{64}} = 0.9845091\ldots < 0.985 \le 0.9890541\ldots = \frac{1 - r^{13}}{1-r^{64}}.$$
Theorem 3.9 with $K=13$ gives the value. (Both inequalities are exact rational comparisons of integers with a few hundred digits.) $\square$

### 4.3 No universal long-leg budget

**Theorem 4.6 (Long-leg budgets are unbounded).** For every $K \in \mathbb{N}$ there exist a Pythagorean triple $(a,b,c)$ with $0 < a \le b$, $c>0$, and a context length $n \ge 1$, such that
$$k^{\star}\big(g_{b/c},\, n,\, 0.985\big) \;>\; K.$$

*Proof sketch.* Use the *near-square* family
$$(a,b,c) = \big(2m+1,\; 2m(m+1),\; 2m(m+1)+1\big),$$
whose Pythagorean identity $(2m+1)^2 + (2m^2+2m)^2 = (2m^2+2m+1)^2$ is a polynomial identity. Take $m = 10K+10$ and $t = 2m(m+1)$, so the long-leg ratio is $r = t/(t+1)$, and take context $n = 2K+2$. Bernoulli's inequality gives
$$r^{\,2K+1} = \Big(1 - \tfrac{1}{t+1}\Big)^{2K+1} \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{2K+1}{t+1} \;\ge\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{10} = 0.9,$$
using $t \ge 10(2K+1)$. Hence $n r^{\,n-1} = (2K+2) r^{2K+1} \ge 0.9(2K+2)$, and
$$\frac{K}{n\, r^{\,n-1}} \;\le\; \frac{K}{0.9\,(2K+2)} \;<\; 0.985 .$$
Theorem 3.8 then yields $K < k^{\star}(g_r, n, 0.985)$. $\square$

Intuitively: as the long-leg ratio approaches $1$ the profile becomes flat, and a flat profile forces one to retain a constant *fraction* of the context — an unbounded number of keys.

### 4.4 Inversion is forced

**Theorem 4.7 (Complementary ratio inversion).** Let $(a_1,b_1,c_1)$ and $(a_2,b_2,c_2)$ be Pythagorean triples with all relevant entries positive. If $\rho_{a_1} < \rho_{a_2}$ then $\rho_{b_2} < \rho_{b_1}$.

*Proof.* Both pairs lie on the unit circle with positive coordinates: $\rho_{b_i} = \sqrt{1 - \rho_{a_i}^2}$, a strictly decreasing function of $\rho_{a_i}$ on $(0,1)$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.8 (Forced budget inversion).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.7, for every context $n \ge 1$ and every gate $\tau \le 1$,
$$k^{\star}(g_{\rho_{a_1}},n,\tau) \le k^{\star}(g_{\rho_{a_2}},n,\tau) \qquad\text{and}\qquad k^{\star}(g_{\rho_{b_2}},n,\tau) \le k^{\star}(g_{\rho_{b_1}},n,\tau).$$

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 3.4 to the ordered pair of short ratios, and again to the reversed pair of long ratios provided by Theorem 4.7. $\square$

So the triple that is cheaper on its short leg is never cheaper on its long leg. There is no uniformly steeper right triangle; the ranking of two triangles by key-efficiency depends on which leg one asks about.

**Theorem 4.9 (Explicit inversion).** At context $64$ and gate $0.985$ the exact knees are
$$k^{\star}(g_{3/5}) = 9, \quad k^{\star}(g_{20/29}) = 12, \quad k^{\star}(g_{21/29}) = 14, \quad k^{\star}(g_{4/5}) = 19,$$
so that $(3,4,5)$ beats $(20,21,29)$ on the short leg ($9 < 12$) and loses to it on the long leg ($19 > 14$).

*Proof sketch.* Each value is a bracket via Theorem 3.9. For instance $r = 4/5$, $n = 64$: $\frac{1-r^{18}}{1-r^{64}} = 0.9819862\ldots < 0.985 \le 0.9855890\ldots = \frac{1-r^{19}}{1-r^{64}}$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.10 (Intra-triple separation).** There exist a Pythagorean triple $(a,b,c)$ with $0 < a \le b$, $c > 0$, and a context $n \ge 1$, with
$$k^{\star}(g_{a/c},n,0.985) \;<\; k^{\star}(g_{b/c},n,0.985),$$
and moreover $k^{\star}(g_{b/c},n,0.985) > 13$, strictly exceeding the universal short-leg budget of Theorem 4.4.

*Proof.* Apply Theorem 4.6 with $K = 13$ to obtain a triple and context with long-leg knee $> 13$; the short-leg knee at that same context is $\le 13$ by Theorem 4.4. $\square$

This last statement is the sharpest form of the phenomenon: inversion does not require two objects. A *single* right triangle's two legs disagree, at the same context and gate, about how much memory is needed.

## 5. The Pell branch: where the two budgets meet

Theorem 4.4 gives a universal short-leg bound and Theorem 4.6 shows the long-leg gap can be enormous. This section identifies the arithmetic family along which the gap *closes*.

**Definition 5.1 (The near-isosceles branch).** Define pairs $(a_k, c_k)$ of (short leg, hypotenuse) by
$$(a_0, c_0) = (3,5), \qquad (a_{k+1}, c_{k+1}) = (3a_k + 2c_k + 1,\; 4a_k + 3c_k + 2).$$
The first four members are $(3,5), (20,29), (119,169), (696,985)$.

**Theorem 5.2 (Pell invariant and Pythagorean property).** For all $k$,
$$c_k^2 = 2a_k^2 + 2a_k + 1, \qquad a_k \ge 3, \qquad c_k \ge 5,$$
and consequently $(a_k,\, a_k+1,\, c_k)$ is a Pythagorean triple with consecutive legs.

*Proof sketch.* Induction. The base case is $25 = 18+6+1$. For the step, substitute the recurrence into $c_{k+1}^2 - 2a_{k+1}^2 - 2a_{k+1} - 1$ and reduce using the inductive invariant; the result is an algebraic identity. Positivity is immediate from the recurrence with nonnegative coefficients. The Pythagorean statement is the rewriting $a^2 + (a+1)^2 = 2a^2+2a+1 = c^2$. $\square$

**Theorem 5.3 (Monotone squeeze of the ratios).** For all $k$,
$$\frac{a_k}{c_k} \;<\; \frac{a_{k+1}}{c_{k+1}}, \qquad \frac{a_{k+1}+1}{c_{k+1}} \;<\; \frac{a_k+1}{c_k}.$$

*Proof sketch.* Cross-multiply. The first reduces to $a_k c_{k+1} < a_{k+1} c_k$, i.e. after substituting the recurrence, to an inequality that follows from the Pell invariant and $a_k \ge 3$, $c_k \ge 5$. The second is analogous. $\square$

Both ratios converge to $1/\sqrt2$ from opposite sides, the short one increasing, the long one decreasing.

**Theorem 5.4 (Monotone squeeze of the budgets).** For every $k$, every context $n \ge 1$ and every gate $\tau \le 1$,
$$k^{\star}\big(g_{a_k/c_k},n,\tau\big) \;\le\; k^{\star}\big(g_{a_{k+1}/c_{k+1}},n,\tau\big), \qquad k^{\star}\big(g_{(a_{k+1}+1)/c_{k+1}},n,\tau\big) \;\le\; k^{\star}\big(g_{(a_k+1)/c_k},n,\tau\big).$$

*Proof.* Theorem 3.4 applied to each ordering of Theorem 5.3. $\square$

So the short-leg budget is non-decreasing and the long-leg budget non-increasing along the branch, *simultaneously at all contexts and gates*: a structural squeeze, requiring no computation.

**Theorem 5.5 (Measured gaps and the meeting point).** At context $64$ and gate $0.985$, the branch has exact budgets

| $k$ | triple | short-leg knee | long-leg knee | gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | $(3,4,5)$ | 9 | 19 | 10 |
| 1 | $(20,21,29)$ | 12 | 14 | 2 |
| 2 | $(119,120,169)$ | 12 | 13 | 1 |
| 3 | $(696,697,985)$ | 13 | 13 | 0 |

In particular the fourth member has *equal* budgets on both legs, both equal to the universal bound $13$ of Theorem 4.4.

*Proof sketch.* Each of the eight entries is an independent bracket via Theorem 3.9; the equality at $k=3$ is a coincidence of two separately certified values, not a definitional identity. $\square$

The extremality of $(696,697,985)$ is not accidental. Among all $477$ primitive triples with hypotenuse at most $3000$, exactly two attain the universal budget $13$ at context $64$: $(696,697,985)$ and $(1748,1755,2477)$ — the two closest to the isosceles direction, with angular defects $|\theta - \pi/4|$ of $7.2\times10^{-4}$ and $2.0\times10^{-3}$ radians.

Two comments. First, this upgrades Theorem 4.5: the constant $13$ is not merely attained; it is the common limit at which the Pythagorean budget inversion of Theorem 4.8 *degenerates*. Second, the gap sequence $10, 2, 1, 0$ is strictly decreasing but not geometric — the squeeze is fast at first and then integer quantisation of the knee takes over, since once the two ratios are within a hair of $1/\sqrt2$ the knee cannot distinguish them.

## 6. The scale $\times$ context surface

### 6.1 The measured table as a combinatorial object

**Definition 6.1.** Let $S, L : \{0,1,2,3\} \to \mathbb{N}$ record the measured knees on the context ladder $512, 1024, 2048, 4096$:
$$S = (16, 20, 24, 40), \qquad L = (16, 16, 18, 56).$$
Write $\Delta f(j) = f(j+1) - f(j)$.

**Proposition 6.2 (Increments and amplification).**
$$\big(\Delta S(0), \Delta S(1), \Delta S(2)\big) = (4,4,16), \qquad \big(\Delta L(0), \Delta L(1), \Delta L(2)\big) = (0,2,38),$$
and $\Delta S(2) = 4\,\Delta S(1)$ while $\Delta L(2) = 19\,\Delta L(1)$, with $4 < 19$.

Thus the acceleration at the final context step is a factor $4$ at the small scale and $19$ at the large one: scale does not delay the acceleration, it amplifies it.

**Theorem 6.3 (No additive law).** There are no $f, g$ with $S(j) = f(0) + g(j)$ and $L(j) = f(1) + g(j)$ for all $j \le 3$.

*Proof.* Such a law forces $L(j) - S(j) = f(1) - f(0)$, a constant. But $L(0)-S(0) = 0$ and $L(3)-S(3) = 16$. $\square$

**Theorem 6.4 (No multiplicative law).** There are no rational-valued $f,g$ with $S(j) = f(0)g(j)$ and $L(j) = f(1)g(j)$ for all $j \le 3$.

*Proof.* From $j = 0$: $f(0)g(0) = 16 = f(1)g(0)$ and $g(0)\ne 0$, so $f(0) = f(1)$. Then $j=1$ gives $20 = f(0)g(1) = f(1)g(1) = 16$, a contradiction. $\square$

Hence no provisioning table of the form "one factor per model, one factor per context" can reproduce the surface, additively or multiplicatively.

**Proposition 6.5 (Sign change and crossover index).** $L(1) - S(1) = -4 < 0$ while $L(3) - S(3) = 16 > 0$; and $3$ is the least index $j$ with $S(j) < L(j)$.

**Proposition 6.6 (Least common budget).** $24$ is the least budget covering both scales at context $2048$, and $56$ is the least budget covering both at $4096$.

### 6.2 Inversion is realizable

The measured inversion could conceivably be an artefact. It is not: inversion of knee curves is a genuine possibility for honest positive profiles.

**Definition 6.7 (Witness profiles).**
$$v(i) = \Big(\tfrac12\Big)^{i}, \qquad w(i) = \Big(\tfrac1{16}\Big)^{i} + \tfrac1{1000}.$$
Both are strictly positive. Note $\tfrac1{1000} \le w(i) \le 1 + \tfrac1{1000}$ for all $i$: $w$ is band-limited with floor $c = 10^{-3}$ and cap $M = 1.001$.

**Lemma 6.8 (Short context: the steeper profile is cheaper).** At gate $0.9$ and context $2$,
$$k^{\star}(w,2,0.9) = 1, \qquad k^{\star}(v,2,0.9) = 2.$$

*Proof sketch.* $w(0) = 1.001$, $w(1) = 0.0635$, so $R_w(2,1) = 1.001/1.0645 = 0.9403475\ldots \ge 0.9$ while $R_w(2,0) = 0 < 0.9$; bracket gives $1$. For $v$: $R_v(2,1) = 1/1.5 = 0.666\ldots < 0.9$ and $R_v(2,2) = 1$; bracket gives $2$. $\square$

**Lemma 6.9 (The gapped profile has a context-free budget).** For every $n \ge 1$, $k^{\star}(v,n,0.9) \le 4$.

*Proof.* $v = g_{1/2}$ and $(1/2)^4 = 1/16 = 0.0625 \le 0.1 = 1 - 0.9$; apply Corollary 3.6. $\square$

**Lemma 6.10 (Long context: the floor profile is dearer).** $k^{\star}(w, 5000, 0.9) > 4$.

*Proof.* By Lemma 2.8 with $c = 10^{-3}$, $M = 1.001$, $n = 5000$, $\tau = 0.9$:
$$k^{\star}(w,5000,0.9) \;\ge\; \frac{0.9 \cdot 5000 \cdot 10^{-3}}{1.001} = \frac{4.5}{1.001} = 4.4955\ldots > 4. \qquad\square$$

**Theorem 6.11 (Inversion is realizable).** There exist positive profiles $v, w$ and contexts $n_1 < n_2$ with
$$k^{\star}(w,n_1,0.9) < k^{\star}(v,n_1,0.9) \qquad\text{and}\qquad k^{\star}(v,n_2,0.9) < k^{\star}(w,n_2,0.9).$$

*Proof.* Take $v,w$ as in Definition 6.7, $n_1 = 2$, $n_2 = 5000$, and combine Lemmas 6.8, 6.9, 6.10. $\square$

**The mechanism is the floor.** Profile $w$ has a much steeper head (ratio $1/16$ against $1/2$) and buys short-context efficiency with it. But its tail does not vanish: it is bounded below by $10^{-3}$, so the total mass grows linearly in $n$ and Lemma 2.8 forces a linearly growing budget. A steeper head purchases short-context efficiency and pays for it at long context. This is the structural reading of "the acceleration is universal, and amplifies with scale".

### 6.3 Rigidity: inversion certifies a retention crossing

**Theorem 6.12 (No inversion under retention domination).** If $R_w(n,k) \le R_v(n,k)$ for all $n,k$, then $k^{\star}(v,n,\tau) \le k^{\star}(w,n,\tau)$ for every $n \ge 1$ and $\tau \le 1$.

*Proof.* $\tau \le R_w(n,k^{\star}(w,n,\tau)) \le R_v(n,k^{\star}(w,n,\tau))$; apply Lemma 2.6. $\square$

**Theorem 6.13 (Inversion forces a crossing).** If $v,w$ are positive and there are contexts $n_1, n_2 \ge 1$ and a gate $\tau \le 1$ with $k^{\star}(w,n_1,\tau) < k^{\star}(v,n_1,\tau)$ and $k^{\star}(v,n_2,\tau) < k^{\star}(w,n_2,\tau)$, then
$$\exists\, n,k:\ R_v(n,k) < R_w(n,k) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists\, n,k:\ R_w(n,k) < R_v(n,k).$$

*Proof.* If the first failed, then $R_w \le R_v$ everywhere and Theorem 6.12 gives $k^{\star}(v,n_1,\tau) \le k^{\star}(w,n_1,\tau)$, contradicting the first hypothesis. Symmetrically for the second. $\square$

So the *only* way an inversion can arise is a genuine crossing of the retained-mass curves. Profiles comparable in retention are comparable in budget at every context.

### 6.4 Localising the crossover

**Theorem 6.14 (Crossover localisation).** Let $v$ be a profile with a context-free budget: $k^{\star}(v,m,\tau) \le K$ for every $m \ge 1$. Let $w$ be band-limited, $0 < c \le w_i \le M$. Let $0 < \tau \le 1$. Then for every context
$$n \;\ge\; \frac{(K+1)M}{\tau c}$$
we have $k^{\star}(v,n,\tau) < k^{\star}(w,n,\tau)$.

*Proof.* The hypothesis on $n$ forces $n \ge 1$ since the right-hand side is positive. Rearranging it gives $\tau n c / M \ge K+1$. Lemma 2.8 then gives $k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) \ge \tau n c / M \ge K+1$, so $k^{\star}(w,n,\tau) \ge K+1 > K \ge k^{\star}(v,n,\tau)$. $\square$

**Corollary 6.15 (Explicit bound for the witness pair).** For the profiles of Definition 6.7 at gate $0.9$, with $K = 4$, $c = 10^{-3}$, $M = 1.001$,
$$\frac{(4+1)\cdot 1.001}{0.9 \cdot 10^{-3}} = 5561.1\overline{1}, $$
so for every $n \ge 5562$ we have $k^{\star}(v,n,0.9) < k^{\star}(w,n,0.9)$.

The bound is an honest upper estimate, not a sharp one. Lemmas 6.9 and 6.10 already exhibit the inversion at $n = 5000$, and an exact evaluation of both knees locates the true crossover much earlier still, at $n = 123$, where the budgets read $k^{\star}(v) = 4$ against $k^{\star}(w) = 5$. The band estimate $\tau n c/M$ of Lemma 2.8 is tight only for a *flat* profile, and $w$ is very far from flat near its head, which is the source of the roughly $45\times$ overshoot. A sharper crossover analysis must be driven by the tail mass beyond the head rather than by the crude floor-to-cap ratio.

**The scaling law.** The crossover context scales like $M/(\tau c)$ — *inversely with the floor*. A profile whose tail floor is $10\times$ smaller postpones its phase transition $10\times$ further out. This is the structural reason a phase transition can appear absent at every context one can afford to test, and then arrive abruptly: the cliff is always present, and the floor determines only how far one must walk to reach it.

## 7. Algorithms

Three computational primitives suffice to reproduce every quantitative claim above.

### 7.1 Exact knee evaluation for a geometric profile

Given exact rational $r = p/q$ with $0 < r < 1$, context $n$ and gate $\tau$, compute
$$k^{\star} = \min\{\,k \le n : (1-r^{k}) \ge \tau\,(1-r^{n})\,\}$$
by scanning $k = 0,1,2,\dots$ and maintaining $r^k$ incrementally. Each comparison is a single rational cross-multiplication. Complexity: $O(k^{\star})$ multiplications of integers of $O(k^{\star} \log q)$ bits, so $O((k^{\star})^2\log q)$ bit operations with schoolbook arithmetic — negligible for the values arising here ($k^{\star} \le 20$). Correctness is Theorem 3.9: the scan returns the first pass, and the immediately preceding index is a certified fail.

### 7.2 Universal-budget certification

Given a decay bound $\bar r$ and a gate $\tau$, return the least $K$ with $\bar r^{\,K} \le 1-\tau$. By Corollary 3.6 that $K$ is a valid budget at *every* context for every profile with ratio $\le \bar r$. For Pythagorean short legs, $\bar r = 1/\sqrt2$ (rationally, $0.708$) and $\tau = 0.985$ return $K = 13$. Complexity $O(K)$.

### 7.3 Crossover localisation

Given $(K, c, M, \tau)$, return $\lceil (K+1)M/(\tau c) \rceil$; by Theorem 6.14 this context certifies the inversion. Constant time. The *empirical* crossover can then be bisected on $[1, \lceil (K+1)M/(\tau c)\rceil]$ using two knee evaluations per probe, in $O(\log n)$ probes — the bound of Theorem 6.14 supplies the right endpoint that makes bisection well-posed.

## 8. Applications

**Memory provisioning for long-context inference.** The results say precisely what a key-cache budget table may and may not look like. Propositions 6.3 and 6.4 rule out one-factor-per-axis tables. Proposition 6.5 says the sign of the scale advantage is itself context-dependent, so "bigger models are more memory-efficient per key" is a claim that must be qualified by a context range: true at $\le 2048$ in the measured surface, false at $\ge 4096$. Proposition 6.6 gives the operational consequence: a $24$-key cache covers both scales at $2048$, but nothing below $56$ covers both at $4096$.

**Profile design.** Theorem 3.4 says budget is a monotone functional of steepness, so any profile-shaping intervention can be evaluated by its effect on the effective decay ratio alone. Theorem 6.11 warns that comparing two shaping interventions at a single context is meaningless: the comparison can reverse. Theorem 6.14 says how far out one must test to be safe, and its $M/(\tau c)$ scaling says which parameter of the profile — the tail floor — controls the answer.

**Certified worst-case budgets.** Corollary 3.6 is a deployment-friendly certificate: a single inequality $r^K \le 1-\tau$, verifiable in closed form, yields a budget valid at every context length. Theorem 4.4 is the extreme instance — an entire infinite arithmetic family sharing one budget of $13$ keys.

**Pythagorean triples as a stress-test family.** Because the two leg ratios of a triple are rigidly coupled by the unit circle, triples furnish an inexhaustible, exactly computable supply of profile pairs whose budget orderings are *guaranteed* to invert (Theorem 4.8). This makes them an ideal ground-truth benchmark for any heuristic that claims to rank profiles by memory efficiency: such a heuristic must reproduce the inversion, and Theorem 4.9 provides a two-line concrete instance to test against.

## 9. Discussion and future directions

### 9.1 What the theory settles

The measured phenomena — acceleration at long context, amplification of the acceleration with scale, and inversion of the scale ordering — are all *realizable* features of retained-mass knees. Inversion is not merely possible but, in the Pythagorean setting, forced (Theorem 4.8); it is structurally *equivalent* to a crossing of the retention curves (Theorems 6.12, 6.13); and it is *localisable* by an explicit bound $(K+1)M/(\tau c)$ (Theorem 6.14). The unifying technical fact is that steepness totally orders budgets (Theorem 3.4), which is proved by a rearrangement inequality rather than by analysis and therefore holds uniformly in the context length.

### 9.2 What it does not settle

No measured number is derived from arithmetic here, and none is claimed to be. That the universal Pythagorean short-leg budget at gate $0.985$ is $13$, and that the crude tail certificate would give $16$, while $16$ also appears in the measured surface, is a coincidence of the gate value and is flagged as such.

### 9.3 Open problems

**(1) Sharpness of the crossover bound.** Theorem 6.14 certifies $n \ge (K+1)M/(\tau c) = 5562$ for the witness pair of Definition 6.7, whose true crossover is at $n = 123$. The band estimate is tight only for the flat profile, so the true crossover should be governed by the *tail mass past the head*, not by the floor-to-cap ratio. The exact closed form of Theorem 3.5 together with a floor decomposition makes an exact crossover equation available for mixture profiles $\alpha r^i + c$; solving it would replace the estimate by an equality.

**(2) Which pairs of triples reopen the gap.** Theorem 5.5 shows the short/long budget gap closing $10, 2, 1, 0$ along the near-isosceles branch, and Theorem 4.8 shows any two triples with distinct short ratios invert. The gap plausibly is a function of the *angular defect* $|\theta - \pi/4|$ of the triple, where $\theta = \arctan(b/a)$, so branches of the Berggren tree of primitive triples that move away from the isosceles direction should have gaps growing without bound. The monotone squeeze is proved for one branch, and the same ratio-monotonicity machinery applies verbatim to the other two Berggren children.

**(3) Gate dependence of the universal constant.** The constant $13$ is the universal short-leg budget at $\tau = 0.985$. It should equal
$$K(\tau) = \left\lceil \frac{\log(1-\tau)}{\log(1/\sqrt2)} \right\rceil,$$
a step function of the gate with jumps at $\tau = 1 - 2^{-j/2}$, and the jumps should be exactly the gates at which a near-isosceles Pell triple changes its knee. Exhaustive computation over all $477$ primitive triples with hypotenuse at most $3000$ agrees with this formula at $\tau = 0.9, 0.95, 0.975, 0.985, 0.99, 0.995$, where it returns $K = 7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 16$ respectively. All ingredients for a proof are in place — the closed form of Theorem 3.5 and the squeeze $\rho_a^2 \le 1/2$ — and only the ceiling arithmetic remains.

**(4) Beyond geometric profiles.** The rearrangement argument of Lemma 3.2 uses only that the head indices are below the tail indices and that $i \mapsto s^i/r^i$ is non-decreasing. It should therefore extend to any two profiles whose *ratio* $w_i / v_i$ is monotone — a log-supermodularity condition. That would give a comparison theorem for a far wider class than geometric profiles, with the Pythagorean applications carrying over unchanged.

**(5) Finer grids and crossover bisection.** The measured surface localises the crossover only to the interval $(2048, 4096]$. The bisection scheme of Section 7.3, together with the certified right endpoint of Theorem 6.14, gives a principled procedure for narrowing it; the theoretical question is whether the crossover context of a mixture profile is a *continuous* function of the floor, or whether knee quantisation makes it a step function with predictable jump loci.

## 10. Summary of results

1. **Antitonicity of retained mass in the decay ratio** (Theorem 3.3), by a head/tail rearrangement inequality, and the resulting **monotonicity of the knee** (Theorem 3.4), uniform in context and gate.
2. **Exact knee calculus for geometric profiles**: closed form (Theorem 3.5), context-free upper certificate $r^K \le 1-\tau$ (Corollary 3.6), flatness failure certificate (Theorem 3.8), and exact evaluation by bracket (Theorem 3.9).
3. **A universal Pythagorean short-leg budget of $13$ keys** at gate $0.985$, valid for every triple and every context (Theorem 4.4), **sharp** at $(696,697,985)$ (Theorem 4.5).
4. **No universal long-leg budget** (Theorem 4.6), via the near-square family $(2m+1, 2m(m+1), 2m(m+1)+1)$.
5. **Forced inversion** of budget orderings between any two triples (Theorems 4.7, 4.8), explicitly $9<12$ and $19>14$ for $(3,4,5)$ against $(20,21,29)$ (Theorem 4.9), and **inversion inside a single triple** (Theorem 4.10).
6. **Monotone convergence of the two leg budgets along the Pell branch** (Theorems 5.3, 5.4) with gaps $10,2,1,0$ meeting at the universal value $13$ (Theorem 5.5).
7. **Non-separability of the measured scale $\times$ context surface**, additively and multiplicatively (Theorems 6.3, 6.4), with acceleration factors $4$ and $19$ (Proposition 6.2) and a sign change of the scale gap at the last grid step (Proposition 6.5).
8. **Realizability of knee-ordering inversion** by explicit positive profiles (Theorem 6.11), its **equivalence to a retention crossing** (Theorems 6.12, 6.13), and its **localisation** at $n \ge (K+1)M/(\tau c)$ (Theorem 6.14), giving $5562$ for the witness pair (Corollary 6.15).

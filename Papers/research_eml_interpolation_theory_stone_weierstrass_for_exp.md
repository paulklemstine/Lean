## Abstract

We study approximation systems assembled from exponentials, logarithms, addition, and multiplication on compact subsets of finite-dimensional Euclidean space. Three layers of the theory are distinguished. First, the scalar feature $t\mapsto e^a\log(bt+c)$ separates arbitrary distinct real inputs while maintaining positive logarithm arguments; the construction extends coordinatewise to $\mathbb{R}^n$. Second, on every compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$, the unital algebra generated by the coordinate exponentials $x\mapsto e^{x_i}$ is uniformly dense in $C(K)$. This is a direct but useful Stone–Weierstrass connector between exponential features and universal approximation. Third, for a concrete expression-tree architecture, the square function has an exact width-two representation, while the genuinely exponential–logarithmic expression $\exp(2\log(x+\delta))$ approximates $x^2$ on $[0,1]$ with uniform error at most $2\delta+\delta^2$. In particular, $0<\delta\le1$ and $3\delta\le\varepsilon$ guarantee error at most $\varepsilon$. We emphasize that qualitative density alone provides no Jackson-type width rate. A rate of order $O(\varepsilon^{-n/\alpha})$ for Hölder targets requires an architecture-specific constructive theory, including local approximation, positivity control, and explicit complexity accounting.

## 1. Introduction

Approximation theory asks which elementary families can reproduce arbitrary continuous behavior on bounded domains. The classical Stone–Weierstrass theorem gives an exceptionally flexible answer: a unital real subalgebra of continuous functions on a compact space is dense if it separates points. Its hypotheses turn a global approximation problem into two local structural tests—constant reproduction and point separation.

This paper applies that viewpoint to exponential–logarithmic, or EML, expressions. Such expressions are generated from variables and constants using the operations $+$, $\times$, $\exp$, and $\log$, subject to the natural requirement that logarithmic inputs be positive wherever real-valued evaluation is intended. These primitives are natural in models of multiplicative growth, scale, entropy, and positive quantities. The mathematical question is not merely whether they are expressive, but precisely which architecture is expressive and what can be said about its complexity.

The main qualitative result concerns a particularly clean family. Given a compact set $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$, take the coordinate exponentials

$$
\phi_i(x)=e^{x_i},\qquad 1\le i\le n,
$$

and form all finite real algebraic combinations of them. This algebra contains constants and separates points because the exponential is injective. Stone–Weierstrass therefore implies uniform density in $C(K)$.

A complementary point-separation result uses a feature with an explicit logarithm:

$$
L_{a,b,c}(t)=e^a\log(bt+c).
$$

For every $x\ne y$, one may select $a=0$, $b=1$, and $c=|x|+|y|+1$. The shift makes both logarithm inputs positive, and strict monotonicity makes the outputs unequal. For vectors, one first chooses a coordinate on which the vectors differ.

The quantitative discussion requires a fixed resource model. We use finite expression trees and count leaves as width. Under this broad architecture, multiplication and repeated access to the variable are allowed. Consequently, $x^2=x\cdot x$ is exact at width two. Arbitrarily larger exact widths are obtained by adding zero leaves. We also analyze a representation that genuinely passes through logarithm and exponential:

$$
S_\delta(x)=\exp(2\log(x+\delta))=(x+\delta)^2.
$$

On $[0,1]$, its error relative to $x^2$ is bounded by $2\delta+\delta^2$. This example gives an explicit approximation guarantee while demonstrating why architectural choices matter: a target that is trivial in an algebraic expression tree may be nontrivial in a restricted shallow-feature model.

The paper makes no quantitative density claim for general Hölder functions. In particular, Stone–Weierstrass does not by itself imply a Jackson-type width bound $O(\varepsilon^{-n/\alpha})$. We identify the additional ingredients such a theorem would require and present practical algorithms for point separation and certified square approximation.

## 2. Preliminaries

### 2.1 Compact domains and uniform approximation

Let $K$ be a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Write $C(K)$ for the set of continuous functions $f:K\to\mathbb{R}$. The uniform norm is

$$
\|f\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in K}|f(x)|.
$$

A subset $A\subseteq C(K)$ is **uniformly dense** if, for every $f\in C(K)$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $g\in A$ satisfying

$$
\|f-g\|_\infty<\varepsilon.
$$

A **unital real subalgebra** $A\subseteq C(K)$ contains every constant function and is closed under pointwise addition, scalar multiplication, and multiplication. It **separates points** if, whenever $x,y\in K$ and $x\ne y$, there exists $g\in A$ such that $g(x)\ne g(y)$.

We use the following classical principle.

**Stone–Weierstrass Theorem.** Let $K$ be a compact Hausdorff space and let $A$ be a unital real subalgebra of $C(K)$. If $A$ separates points, then $A$ is uniformly dense in $C(K)$.

The theorem is qualitative: it supplies an approximant for every positive tolerance but does not bound the algebraic degree, expression size, number of generators, depth, or width needed.

### 2.2 Scalar exp–log features

For $a,b,c,t\in\mathbb{R}$ with $bt+c>0$, define

$$
L_{a,b,c}(t)=e^a\log(bt+c).
$$

The positivity condition is essential over the reals. Since $e^a>0$, the parameter $a$ scales the feature without altering strict monotonicity. If $b>0$, then $t\mapsto bt+c$ is strictly increasing, and composition with $\log$ remains strictly increasing wherever its input is positive.

### 2.3 Coordinate exponential algebra

For $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ compact, define

$$
\phi_i:K\to\mathbb{R},\qquad \phi_i(x)=e^{x_i},
$$

for $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$. Let $A_K$ be the smallest unital real subalgebra of $C(K)$ containing $\phi_1,\ldots,\phi_n$. Concretely, $A_K$ consists of finite real polynomial combinations of these features: each member can be written as

$$
p(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})
$$

for some real polynomial $p$ in $n$ variables. No logarithm is required for the density argument, although these generators belong naturally to the broader EML vocabulary.

### 2.4 Expression trees and width

An **EML expression** in one real variable is defined recursively:

1. every real constant $c$ is an expression;
2. the variable $x$ is an expression;
3. if $E$ is an expression, then $\exp(E)$ and $\log(E)$ are expressions;
4. if $E_1,E_2$ are expressions, then $E_1+E_2$ and $E_1E_2$ are expressions.

Evaluation follows the same recursion, with logarithms restricted to inputs for which the real logarithm is intended. The **leaf width** $w(E)$ is defined by

$$
w(c)=1,\qquad w(x)=1,
$$

$$
w(\exp(E))=w(\log(E))=w(E),
$$

and

$$
w(E_1+E_2)=w(E_1E_2)=w(E_1)+w(E_2).
$$

This width counts occurrences of constants and inputs. It is not automatically equivalent to hidden-unit count, parameter count, circuit size with shared subexpressions, or maximum layer width.

For $m\ge0$, define zero padding recursively by $P_0(E)=E$ and

$$
P_{m+1}(E)=0+P_m(E).
$$

Then $P_m(E)$ computes the same function as $E$ and has width $w(E)+m$.

## 3. Point Separation by Exp–Log Features

We first establish a constructive scalar result.

**Theorem 1 (Positive-domain scalar separation).** Let $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ with $x\ne y$. There exist $a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
b>0,\qquad bx+c>0,\qquad by+c>0,
$$

and

$$
L_{a,b,c}(x)\ne L_{a,b,c}(y).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Set

$$
a=0,\qquad b=1,\qquad c=|x|+|y|+1.
$$

Since $x+|x|\ge0$ and $y+|y|\ge0$, both $x+c$ and $y+c$ are strictly positive. If the feature values were equal, then

$$
\log(x+c)=\log(y+c).
$$

The logarithm is injective on $(0,\infty)$, hence $x+c=y+c$, which implies $x=y$, a contradiction. Therefore the outputs differ. $\square$

The choice of shift is deliberately symmetric and requires no prior ordering of $x$ and $y$. It also provides a positivity margin of at least $1$ after accounting for the other absolute value.

The vector version follows by selecting a differing coordinate.

**Theorem 2 (Coordinatewise vector separation).** Let $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with $x\ne y$. Then there are an index $i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}$ and parameters $a,b,c\in\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$
b>0,\qquad bx_i+c>0,\qquad by_i+c>0,
$$

and

$$
L_{a,b,c}(x_i)\ne L_{a,b,c}(y_i).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Distinct vectors differ in at least one coordinate, so choose $i$ with $x_i\ne y_i$. Apply Theorem 1 to the scalar pair $(x_i,y_i)$. $\square$

This theorem supplies the geometric part of an approximation argument: a single coordinate feature can witness every pairwise distinction. It does not assert that a fixed finite list of shifted logarithmic features by itself forms an algebra or is dense; algebraic closure and continuity must also be specified.

## 4. A Stone–Weierstrass Theorem for Coordinate Exponentials

We now pass from pairwise discrimination to global approximation.

**Lemma 3 (Coordinate exponentials separate points).** Let $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$, and let $A_K$ be the unital algebra generated by $\phi_i(x)=e^{x_i}$. If $x,y\in K$ and $x\ne y$, then some $g\in A_K$ satisfies $g(x)\ne g(y)$.

**Proof sketch.** Choose a coordinate $i$ for which $x_i\ne y_i$. The exponential function is injective on $\mathbb{R}$, so $e^{x_i}\ne e^{y_i}$. Taking $g=\phi_i$, which belongs to $A_K$ by construction, proves separation. $\square$

Each $\phi_i$ is continuous, so every algebraic combination in $A_K$ is continuous. The algebra is unital by definition. The Stone–Weierstrass theorem therefore gives the main qualitative result.

**Theorem 4 (Exponential Stone–Weierstrass density).** Let $K$ be a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. For every continuous function $f:K\to\mathbb{R}$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists a real polynomial $p$ in $n$ variables such that

$$
\sup_{x\in K}\left|p(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})-f(x)\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

Equivalently, the uniform closure of $A_K$ is all of $C(K)$.

**Proof sketch.** The generators $\phi_i$ are continuous, hence $A_K\subseteq C(K)$. The algebra contains constants and is closed under the required pointwise algebraic operations. By Lemma 3 it separates points of $K$. All hypotheses of the real Stone–Weierstrass theorem hold, so $A_K$ is uniformly dense in $C(K)$. $\square$

### 4.1 Interpretation through a change of variables

The coordinate exponential map

$$
\Phi:K\to(0,\infty)^n,\qquad \Phi(x)=(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})
$$

is continuous and injective. Because $K$ is compact and $(0,\infty)^n$ is Hausdorff, $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism between $K$ and its image $\Phi(K)$. The theorem may therefore be understood as ordinary polynomial approximation on the transformed compact set $\Phi(K)$, pulled back through $\Phi$. This interpretation also explains why injectivity is decisive: no information about $x$ is lost by exponentiating its coordinates.

### 4.2 Scope of the density statement

The theorem establishes existence of finite approximants, not a canonical construction. It does not estimate the degree of $p$, the magnitude of its coefficients, the numerical conditioning of evaluation, or an expression-tree width. Nor does it claim that a single hidden layer of scalar features $L_{a,b,c}$ has the same closure. Such a claim would require a separate architecture definition and proof.

The result nevertheless gives a robust qualitative bridge. Coordinate exponentials alone generate enough continuous observables to recover every continuous target uniformly after algebraic combination. Since exponentiation, addition, and multiplication are among the EML operations, every approximant belongs to the broad EML expression language.

## 5. Exact and Approximate Representations of the Square

### 5.1 Exact realization with multiplication

Let

$$
Q(x)=x\cdot x.
$$

By the width rules, $w(Q)=1+1=2$, and $Q(x)=x^2$ identically.

**Theorem 5 (Exact square at every width at least two).** For every integer $N\ge2$, there exists an EML expression $E_N$ with $w(E_N)=N$ such that

$$
E_N(x)=x^2
$$

for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Consequently,

$$
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|E_N(x)-x^2|=0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Take $E_N=P_{N-2}(Q)$. Zero padding does not change evaluation, so $E_N(x)=Q(x)=x^2$. Its width is

$$
w(E_N)=w(Q)+(N-2)=2+(N-2)=N.
$$

The uniform error is therefore zero on $[0,1]$, indeed on the entire real line. $\square$

The theorem is useful both positively and negatively. Positively, it gives an exact, constant-size representation. Negatively, it shows that polynomial examples cannot support nontrivial lower bounds in an architecture that freely permits multiplication and direct variable leaves.

### 5.2 Shifted exponential–logarithmic realization

To exhibit the behavior of logarithm and exponential explicitly, fix $\delta>0$ and define

$$
S_\delta(x)=\exp\bigl(2\log(x+\delta)\bigr).
$$

For $x\in[0,1]$, one has $x+\delta>0$, so the expression remains in the real logarithm’s domain. Moreover,

$$
\exp\bigl(2\log u\bigr)
=\exp\bigl(\log u+\log u\bigr)
=u^2
$$

for every $u>0$. Thus $S_\delta(x)=(x+\delta)^2$.

**Theorem 6 (Explicit shifted-square error).** If $\delta>0$, then for every $x\in[0,1]$,

$$
|S_\delta(x)-x^2|\le2\delta+\delta^2.
$$

**Proof sketch.** The positivity just noted gives $S_\delta(x)=(x+\delta)^2$. Expanding,

$$
S_\delta(x)-x^2=2x\delta+\delta^2.
$$

Because $x\ge0$ and $\delta>0$, this difference is nonnegative, so the absolute value may be removed. Since $x\le1$,

$$
2x\delta+\delta^2\le2\delta+\delta^2.
$$

This proves the bound. Equality occurs at $x=1$, so the bound is the exact uniform error for fixed $\delta$. $\square$

The final observation sharpens the interpretation: the supremum error is not merely bounded by $2\delta+\delta^2$; it equals that quantity because the error increases with $x$ on $[0,1]$.

**Corollary 7 (Tolerance selection).** Let $\varepsilon,\delta\in\mathbb{R}$ satisfy

$$
0<\delta\le1\qquad\text{and}\qquad 3\delta\le\varepsilon.
$$

Then

$$
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|S_\delta(x)-x^2|\le\varepsilon.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Since $0<\delta\le1$, one has $\delta^2\le\delta$. Theorem 6 yields

$$
|S_\delta(x)-x^2|\le2\delta+\delta^2\le3\delta\le\varepsilon.
$$

Taking the supremum preserves the inequality. $\square$

The shift $\delta$ has two roles. It guarantees a positive logarithm input even at $x=0$, and it controls approximation error. Decreasing $\delta$ improves approximation but moves the logarithm input closer to zero, where numerical sensitivity increases. Thus the symbolic estimate exposes a practical stability–accuracy tradeoff.

## 6. Algorithms

### 6.1 Constructive separation algorithm

Given two distinct scalar inputs, the following constant-time procedure produces valid parameters:

1. Input $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$ with $x\ne y$.
2. Set $a\leftarrow0$ and $b\leftarrow1$.
3. Set $c\leftarrow|x|+|y|+1$.
4. Return $(a,b,c)$.

The arithmetic cost is $O(1)$. The resulting arguments $x+c$ and $y+c$ are positive, and the output distinction follows from strict monotonicity of the logarithm. For vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$, scan coordinates until finding $x_i\ne y_i$, then apply the scalar procedure. This costs $O(n)$ comparisons in the worst case and constant additional storage.

### 6.2 Certified shifted-square approximation

Given a desired tolerance $\varepsilon>0$, one may select a conservative shift. For example, if $0<\varepsilon\le3$, choose

$$
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.
$$

Then $0<\delta\le1$ and $3\delta=\varepsilon$, so Corollary 7 applies. For arbitrary $\varepsilon>0$, a uniform rule that preserves strict positivity is

$$
\delta=\min\left\{\frac12,\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\right\}.
$$

This gives $0<\delta\le1$ and $3\delta\le\varepsilon$ whenever the second branch is active; when $\varepsilon\ge3/2$, the first branch gives $3\delta=3/2\le\varepsilon$. Evaluation of $S_\delta(x)$ takes constant arithmetic-transcendental work per sample. For a list of $m$ points, the runtime is $O(m)$ and storage can be $O(1)$ beyond the output.

For numerical work, the identity $S_\delta(x)=(x+\delta)^2$ can be used as a stable reference. Direct evaluation through $\exp$ and $\log$ demonstrates the architecture but may accumulate floating-point error near the logarithmic boundary.

## 7. Toward Quantitative Hölder Approximation

A function $f:K\to\mathbb{R}$ is Hölder continuous with exponent $\alpha\in(0,1]$ and constant $L$ if

$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\le L\|x-y\|^\alpha
$$

for all $x,y\in K$. The case $\alpha=1$ is Lipschitz continuity.

A natural target is an architecture-specific theorem asserting that every such $f$ admits error at most $\varepsilon$ with width

$$
O\left(\varepsilon^{-n/\alpha}\right).
$$

The exponent is suggested by grid interpolation. If a domain is divided into cells of diameter $h$, Hölder continuity bounds local variation by $Lh^\alpha$. Choosing $h$ on the order of $(\varepsilon/L)^{1/\alpha}$ controls this variation, while an $n$-dimensional grid contains on the order of $h^{-n}$ cells. Substitution yields the heuristic count $(L/\varepsilon)^{n/\alpha}$.

This heuristic is not a consequence of Theorem 4. A rigorous EML rate theorem would need at least the following ingredients.

1. **Domain geometry.** One must specify whether $K$ is a cube, a regular compact body, or an arbitrary compact set. Grid counts and extension results depend on this choice.
2. **Constructive local approximation.** A partition, triangulation, or partition of unity must be built with controlled overlap and error.
3. **Architectural realization.** Each local basis function must be represented or approximated by the permitted exp–log units. If multiplication and unrestricted composition are available, complexity can collapse for special functions.
4. **Positivity invariants.** Every logarithm must receive a positive argument throughout $K$, not merely at sampled points.
5. **Error propagation.** Approximation errors introduced in local basis functions must be combined without losing the desired exponent or stability.
6. **Resource accounting.** Leaf width, hidden units, parameters, depth, and coefficient precision must be distinguished explicitly.

The exact square theorem illustrates the fourth and sixth issues sharply. Under broad expression-tree width, $x^2$ is exact with two leaves. Under a single-hidden-layer model consisting only of sums of $e^a\log(bx+c)$, multiplication of two variable leaves may be unavailable, and the complexity problem changes completely.

Accordingly, the present results establish qualitative density and explicit special-case bounds, while the Jackson-rate program remains open pending a fixed architecture and a constructive multivariate argument.

## 8. Applications and Interpretation

### 8.1 Scientific surrogate models

Many scientific quantities are continuous on bounded parameter ranges. Theorem 4 guarantees that finite algebraic combinations of coordinate exponentials can approximate such response surfaces uniformly. Uniform control is stronger than average-case control: it bounds the error at every point in the domain.

### 8.2 Positive and multiscale data

Logarithms are natural for quantities varying over orders of magnitude, while exponentials naturally preserve positivity. The separation construction shows how a simple shift can keep logarithms well-defined without sacrificing the ability to distinguish inputs. In implementations, the positivity margin should be treated as a stability parameter rather than only a domain condition.

### 8.3 Interpretability through symbolic error bounds

The shifted-square model offers a transparent guarantee. Its output, target, and error can all be written in closed form:

$$
S_\delta(x)=(x+\delta)^2,
$$

$$
S_\delta(x)-x^2=2x\delta+\delta^2,
$$

and

$$
\|S_\delta-x^2\|_{\infty,[0,1]}=2\delta+\delta^2.
$$

Such identities permit parameter selection without statistical estimation. They also provide reference cases for testing optimization software intended for more restricted architectures.

### 8.4 Topological meaning of feature design

Point separation is an information-preservation condition. The map $x\mapsto(e^{x_1},\ldots,e^{x_n})$ embeds a compact domain into the positive orthant. Once embedded, polynomial combinations can approximate arbitrary continuous observables on the image. This separates feature design into two conceptual stages: construct an injective continuous representation, then use an algebra rich enough for Stone–Weierstrass.

## 9. Discussion

The theory reveals a hierarchy of claims that should not be conflated.

At the first level, a single exp–log feature separates two prescribed points. This is constructive and requires only strict monotonicity plus a positive shift. At the second level, the algebra generated by coordinate exponentials separates all points and contains constants, so it is dense in every $C(K)$ for compact $K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$. This is global and universal but qualitative. At the third level, particular targets admit explicit finite expressions and error estimates. The square function is exact under multiplication and has a simple shifted exp–log approximation. At a prospective fourth level, one would seek uniform complexity rates over regularity classes such as Hölder balls. That level requires substantially more machinery.

The role of multiplication deserves special emphasis. Algebraic closure is exactly what enables the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, but multiplication can make a network class much stronger than a conventional shallow architecture. A claim phrased only as “EML network width” is therefore ambiguous. If the model is an expression tree, one must specify whether repeated subexpressions may be shared. If the model is layered, one must specify allowed connectivity and activations. If coefficients are unrestricted real numbers, bit complexity remains unmeasured. Sound quantitative comparisons require all of these conventions.

The density theorem is also insensitive to numerical conditioning. Although exponentiation is injective, large positive or negative coordinates can produce scale disparities. Polynomial approximation on the transformed set may involve large coefficients. Future quantitative work should track not only existence and width but coefficient magnitude and sensitivity.

## 10. Future Work

The primary next objective is a genuine Jackson-type theorem for a restricted and explicitly defined EML architecture. A plausible program begins on the cube $[0,1]^n$: construct a grid or simplicial interpolant, establish the standard Hölder error, and approximate each local basis function by positive-domain exp–log subnetworks. The proof must track width, depth, coefficient size, and error under composition.

A normal-form theorem would clarify the relationship between algebra-generated expressions and layered networks. Such a theorem should translate finite algebraic combinations into a selected architecture with explicit resource overhead. Without it, algebraic density and neural-network complexity remain related but inequivalent statements.

Further useful directions include continuity and positivity invariants for nested logarithms; matching lower bounds under restricted depth or coefficients; exact representations of multivariate quadratic forms; and stability estimates for repeated compositions of exponentials and logarithms. Computational experiments become most informative after fixing such a restricted architecture and an optimization method. At that stage, numerical results can test the sharpness of theoretical rates, rather than merely confirm symbolic identities already known exactly.

## 11. Conclusion

Exp–log approximation rests on a simple geometric fact: monotone features can distinguish points. A positive shift makes the logarithm globally safe for any prescribed pair, and a differing coordinate extends the construction to vectors. Coordinate exponentials then provide an especially economical separating family. Their unital algebra is uniformly dense in the continuous functions on every compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

Concrete expression trees add quantitative information. With multiplication, $x^2$ is exact at width two and at every padded width above two. Through a genuine logarithm–exponential path, the shifted expression $\exp(2\log(x+\delta))$ has exact uniform error $2\delta+\delta^2$ on $[0,1]$, yielding an immediate tolerance rule.

Together, these results establish universal qualitative expressiveness and explicit special-case guarantees. They also locate the remaining challenge precisely: a rate such as $O(\varepsilon^{-n/\alpha})$ cannot be read off from Stone–Weierstrass. It must be earned through constructive approximation and architecture-sensitive complexity analysis.

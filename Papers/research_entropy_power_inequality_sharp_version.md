## Abstract

For a real entropy value $h$ in positive dimension $n$, define entropy power by $N_n(h)=\exp(2h/n)/(2\pi e)$ and entropy radius by $r_n(h)=\exp(h/n)/\sqrt{2\pi e}$. This paper develops the exact algebraic and geometric structure generated by these normalizations. First, entropy power is the square of entropy radius, so the entropy-power inequality is equivalent to the Euclidean growth law $r_X^2+r_Y^2\le r_S^2$. Second, strict monotonicity identifies the unique sharp entropy threshold

$$
B_n(h_X,h_Y)=\frac n2\log\!\left(\exp(2h_X/n)+\exp(2h_Y/n)\right),
$$

with equality precisely when $h_S=B_n(h_X,h_Y)$. Third, for a centered isotropic Gaussian with scalar variance $v>0$, entropy power equals $v$ and entropy radius equals $\sqrt v$; consequently independent isotropic Gaussian convolution attains equality in every positive dimension. Finally, if the output entropy exceeds the sharp boundary by $\delta$, then output power and the entropy-power deficit obey exact stability identities with factor $\exp(2\delta/n)$. These results isolate the analytic-algebraic core of the sharp entropy power inequality and clarify its relation to Euclidean geometry and the radius language of Brunn--Minkowski theory. The scope is deliberately scalar at the entropy level: the full measure-theoretic inequality for general independent random vectors requires additional convolution and regularity arguments.

## 1. Introduction

The entropy power inequality is one of the central structural principles of information theory. In its usual probabilistic form, it asserts that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent random vectors in $\mathbb R^n$ with suitable densities and finite differential entropies, then

$$
N(X+Y)\ge N(X)+N(Y),
$$

where

$$
N(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi e}\exp\!\left(\frac{2h(X)}{n}\right).
$$

The normalization makes $N(X)$ a variance-like quantity: when $X$ is a centered isotropic Gaussian with covariance $vI_n$, one has $N(X)=v$. The inequality is sharp, and Gaussian inputs with proportional covariance matrices form the expected equality class in the full theorem.

This paper studies the exact scalar structure underlying that statement. Given three entropy values $h_X,h_Y,h_S$, it asks four questions.

1. What geometry is encoded by the inequality $N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\le N_n(h_S)$?
2. What is the unique entropy value at which equality occurs?
3. Why do centered isotropic Gaussians saturate the inequality in every positive dimension?
4. How does an additive excess in entropy translate into quantitative slack in entropy power?

All four questions have exact answers. The key step is to take the positive square root of entropy power and call it entropy radius. This produces the identity $N_n=r_n^2$, turning entropy-power addition into a Pythagorean law. The equality threshold is a scaled log-sum-exp function. Gaussian entropy converts scalar variance into entropy power exactly. Finally, translation above the boundary becomes multiplication in power space, yielding a closed-form deficit.

The distinction between scalar and probabilistic statements is important. The results below completely characterize the algebra of entropy values and prove the full isotropic-Gaussian equality family. They do not, on their own, derive the entropy inequality for arbitrary random vectors from independence and convolution. That additional theorem requires measure-theoretic definitions and analytic inequalities. Isolating the scalar bridge is nevertheless useful: it separates universal exponential algebra from the deeper probabilistic input and gives a precise target for future extensions.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines entropy power, entropy radius, the sharp boundary, and the deficit. Section 3 proves the radius identity, Pythagorean equivalence, monotonicity, and exact boundary characterization. Section 4 treats isotropic Gaussians. Section 5 establishes exact stability identities. Section 6 gives numerical algorithms, Section 7 discusses the Brunn--Minkowski connection and applications, and Sections 8 and 9 address limitations and future work.

## 2. Definitions and elementary structure

Throughout, $n$ is a positive integer and all entropy arguments are real numbers. The constant $2\pi e$ is strictly positive.

### 2.1. Entropy power and entropy radius

**Definition 2.1 (Entropy power).** For $h\in\mathbb R$, the entropy power in dimension $n$ is

$$
N_n(h)=\frac{\exp(2h/n)}{2\pi e}.
$$

This is positive for every real $h$.

**Definition 2.2 (Entropy radius).** For $h\in\mathbb R$, the entropy radius in dimension $n$ is

$$
r_n(h)=\frac{\exp(h/n)}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}.
$$

It is also strictly positive. The term “radius” is motivated both by the square relation proved below and by analogy with normalized volume radii in convex geometry.

### 2.2. Sharp boundary and deficit

**Definition 2.3 (Sharp entropy boundary).** Given input entropies $h_X,h_Y\in\mathbb R$, define

$$
B_n(h_X,h_Y)
=\frac n2\log\!\left(
\exp(2h_X/n)+\exp(2h_Y/n)
\right).
$$

The logarithm is well-defined because the sum of exponentials is positive. The function is symmetric in $h_X$ and $h_Y$.

**Definition 2.4 (Entropy-power deficit).** Given $h_X,h_Y,h_S\in\mathbb R$, define

$$
D_n(h_X,h_Y,h_S)
=N_n(h_S)-N_n(h_X)-N_n(h_Y).
$$

The terminology follows the convention that the entropy power inequality is equivalent to $D_n\ge0$. Since $D_n$ measures the output power in excess of the sum of input powers, it may also be called the entropy-power slack.

**Definition 2.5 (Pythagorean radius growth).** Three nonnegative radii $r_X,r_Y,r_S$ satisfy Pythagorean radius growth if

$$
r_X^2+r_Y^2\le r_S^2.
$$

The definition is written for nonnegative radii because entropy radii are automatically positive, although the algebraic inequality itself makes sense for arbitrary real numbers.

### 2.3. Gaussian entropy

**Definition 2.6 (Centered isotropic Gaussian entropy).** For $v>0$, define

$$
h_G(n,v)=\frac n2\log(2\pi e v).
$$

This is the differential entropy of the centered Gaussian distribution on $\mathbb R^n$ with covariance matrix $vI_n$. The scalar $v$ is the variance in every coordinate and $\sqrt v$ is the common standard deviation.

## 3. The radius bridge and the sharp boundary

### 3.1. Power is squared radius

**Theorem 3.1 (Power-radius identity).** For every positive integer $n$ and every $h\in\mathbb R$,

$$
N_n(h)=r_n(h)^2.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Squaring Definition 2.2 gives

$$
r_n(h)^2
=\frac{\exp(h/n)^2}{2\pi e}.
$$

The exponential addition law implies $\exp(h/n)^2=\exp(2h/n)$, which is exactly Definition 2.1. $\square$

This elementary identity is the central connector. It gives an exact geometric restatement of the entropy power inequality.

**Theorem 3.2 (Pythagorean radius equivalence).** For every positive integer $n$ and all real $h_X,h_Y,h_S$, the following statements are equivalent:

1. $N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\le N_n(h_S)$;
2. $r_n(h_X)^2+r_n(h_Y)^2\le r_n(h_S)^2$.

Equivalently,

$$
r_n(h_S)\ge
\sqrt{r_n(h_X)^2+r_n(h_Y)^2}.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 3.1 separately to $h_X$, $h_Y$, and $h_S$. The first displayed inequality then becomes the second verbatim. Since entropy radii are positive, taking square roots preserves the order and yields the final formulation. $\square$

Thus the exponent two in entropy power has a precise geometric role: it is the exponent in Euclidean norm addition. The inequality can be read as saying that the effective output radius cannot lie inside the right triangle determined by the two input radii.

### 3.2. Deficit formulation

**Proposition 3.3 (Deficit equivalence).** For every positive integer $n$ and all real $h_X,h_Y,h_S$,

$$
N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\le N_n(h_S)
$$

if and only if

$$
D_n(h_X,h_Y,h_S)\ge0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Move the two input powers from the left side of the inequality to the right. The resulting expression is exactly Definition 2.4. $\square$

### 3.3. Strict monotonicity

**Theorem 3.4 (Strict monotonicity of entropy power).** For every positive integer $n$, the map $h\mapsto N_n(h)$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$.

**Proof sketch.** If $a<b$, positivity of $2/n$ gives $2a/n<2b/n$. Strict monotonicity of the exponential implies $\exp(2a/n)<\exp(2b/n)$. Division by the positive constant $2\pi e$ preserves the strict inequality. $\square$

The same reasoning shows that entropy radius is strictly increasing. Strict monotonicity will turn an equality in power space into a unique equality in entropy space.

### 3.4. Evaluation at the boundary

**Lemma 3.5 (Boundary power identity).** For every positive integer $n$ and all $h_X,h_Y\in\mathbb R$,

$$
N_n(B_n(h_X,h_Y))
=N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Substitute Definition 2.3 into the exponent of Definition 2.1:

$$
\frac{2}{n}B_n(h_X,h_Y)
=\log\!\left(\exp(2h_X/n)+\exp(2h_Y/n)\right).
$$

Exponentiation cancels the logarithm because its argument is positive. Dividing by $2\pi e$ distributes over the sum and produces the two input powers. $\square$

**Theorem 3.6 (Exact equality characterization).** For every positive integer $n$ and all real $h_X,h_Y,h_S$,

$$
N_n(h_S)=N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)
$$

if and only if

$$
h_S=B_n(h_X,h_Y).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Lemma 3.5 shows that the right side implies the left. Conversely, if the output power equals the sum of input powers, Lemma 3.5 gives $N_n(h_S)=N_n(B_n(h_X,h_Y))$. The injectivity supplied by strict monotonicity in Theorem 3.4 yields $h_S=B_n(h_X,h_Y)$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.7 (Sharp lower-bound characterization).** For every positive integer $n$ and all real $h_X,h_Y,h_S$,

$$
N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\le N_n(h_S)
$$

if and only if

$$
B_n(h_X,h_Y)\le h_S.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Replace the sum of input powers by $N_n(B_n(h_X,h_Y))$ using Lemma 3.5. The desired equivalence is then

$$
N_n(B_n(h_X,h_Y))\le N_n(h_S)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B_n(h_X,h_Y)\le h_S,
$$

which follows from strict monotonicity. $\square$

The word “sharp” is justified in a literal sense: $B_n(h_X,h_Y)$ is both sufficient and necessary, and no smaller output entropy can satisfy the inequality.

### 3.5. Useful boundary identities

The boundary is a dimension-scaled log-sum-exp. It is symmetric and obeys a translation law.

**Proposition 3.8 (Symmetry and common translation).** For all real $h_X,h_Y,c$,

$$
B_n(h_X,h_Y)=B_n(h_Y,h_X)
$$

and

$$
B_n(h_X+c,h_Y+c)=B_n(h_X,h_Y)+c.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Symmetry follows from commutativity of addition. For translation, factor $\exp(2c/n)$ from the sum inside the logarithm, then use $\log(ab)=\log a+\log b$ for positive factors. Multiplication by $n/2$ converts $\log(\exp(2c/n))$ to $c$. $\square$

For equal inputs, the boundary is explicit:

$$
B_n(h,h)=h+\frac n2\log 2.
$$

More generally, writing $m=\max(h_X,h_Y)$ gives the numerically stable form

$$
B_n(h_X,h_Y)
=m+\frac n2\log\!\left(
\exp(2(h_X-m)/n)+\exp(2(h_Y-m)/n)
\right).
$$

The exponentials in this expression lie in $(0,1]$, avoiding overflow in numerical evaluation.

## 4. The sharp isotropic-Gaussian family

The normalization of entropy power is designed around Gaussian distributions. The following identities make this exact.

**Theorem 4.1 (Gaussian entropy power equals variance).** Let $n$ be a positive integer and $v>0$. Then

$$
N_n(h_G(n,v))=v.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Substitution gives

$$
N_n(h_G(n,v))
=\frac{1}{2\pi e}
\exp\!\left(
\frac{2}{n}\cdot\frac n2\log(2\pi e v)
\right).
$$

The scalar factors cancel, and the exponential of the logarithm is $2\pi e v$. Division by $2\pi e$ leaves $v$. Positivity of $v$ ensures that the logarithm and cancellation are valid. $\square$

**Corollary 4.2 (Gaussian entropy radius equals standard deviation).** Under the same assumptions,

$$
r_n(h_G(n,v))=\sqrt v.
$$

**Proof sketch.** By Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 4.1, the square of the radius is $v$. Both the entropy radius and $\sqrt v$ are nonnegative, so they are equal. $\square$

Now suppose $X\sim\mathcal N(0,v_XI_n)$ and $Y\sim\mathcal N(0,v_YI_n)$ are independent, with $v_X,v_Y>0$. Their sum satisfies

$$
X+Y\sim\mathcal N(0,(v_X+v_Y)I_n).
$$

This standard convolution rule supplies the probabilistic input for the Gaussian equality family.

**Theorem 4.3 (Sharp entropy-power equality for isotropic Gaussians).** For every positive integer $n$ and all $v_X,v_Y>0$,

$$
N_n(h_G(n,v_X+v_Y))
=N_n(h_G(n,v_X))+N_n(h_G(n,v_Y)).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Apply Theorem 4.1 to the three variances. The left side is $v_X+v_Y$, while the right side is $v_X+v_Y$. $\square$

**Corollary 4.4 (Gaussian Pythagorean bridge).** Under the same assumptions,

$$
r_n(h_G(n,v_X+v_Y))^2
=r_n(h_G(n,v_X))^2+r_n(h_G(n,v_Y))^2.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\sqrt{v_X+v_Y}^{\,2}
=(\sqrt{v_X})^2+(\sqrt{v_Y})^2.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Replace each entropy power in Theorem 4.3 by squared entropy radius using Theorem 3.1, or apply Corollary 4.2 directly. $\square$

The result is dimension-independent in form. Dimension affects the numerical entropy $h_G(n,v)$, but normalization returns the scalar variance. The equality mechanism is therefore the familiar addition of independent Gaussian variances.

## 5. Exact stability above the sharp boundary

Classical stability questions ask whether a small deficit forces an object to lie near an equality class. The scalar setting permits an exact preliminary result: entropy excess above the boundary and power deficit determine one another by a closed formula.

Let

$$
h_S=B_n(h_X,h_Y)+\delta,
$$

where $\delta\in\mathbb R$ is the entropy excess. Positive $\delta$ means that output entropy lies above the sharp boundary; zero means equality; negative $\delta$ means it lies below.

**Theorem 5.1 (Exact multiplicative stability).** For every positive integer $n$ and all real $h_X,h_Y,\delta$,

$$
N_n(B_n(h_X,h_Y)+\delta)
=\exp(2\delta/n)
\bigl(N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\bigr).
$$

**Proof sketch.** The exponential addition law gives

$$
\exp\!\left(\frac{2(B_n+\delta)}{n}\right)
=\exp(2\delta/n)\exp(2B_n/n).
$$

After division by $2\pi e$, the second factor is $N_n(B_n)$. Lemma 3.5 replaces it by the sum of input powers. $\square$

**Theorem 5.2 (Exact additive deficit identity).** Under the same assumptions,

$$
D_n(h_X,h_Y,B_n(h_X,h_Y)+\delta)
=\bigl(\exp(2\delta/n)-1\bigr)
\bigl(N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\bigr).
$$

**Proof sketch.** Insert Theorem 5.1 into Definition 2.4 and factor the common sum of input powers. $\square$

**Corollary 5.3 (Strict slack from strict entropy excess).** If $\delta>0$, then

$$
D_n(h_X,h_Y,B_n(h_X,h_Y)+\delta)>0.
$$

**Proof sketch.** Positivity of $n$ and $\delta$ implies $2\delta/n>0$, hence $\exp(2\delta/n)>1$. The sum $N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)$ is strictly positive. The product in Theorem 5.2 is therefore strictly positive. $\square$

The identity may be inverted whenever $D>-N_n(h_X)-N_n(h_Y)$. Let $P=N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)>0$. Then

$$
\delta=\frac n2\log\!\left(1+\frac{D}{P}\right).
$$

For $\delta$ near zero, Taylor expansion gives

$$
D
=P\left(\frac{2\delta}{n}+O(\delta^2/n^2)\right).
$$

Thus the first-order sensitivity is $2P/n$. This local approximation is useful computationally, but Theorem 5.2 should be preferred whenever exact evaluation is possible.

This is a scalar stability identity, not yet a distributional stability theorem. It quantifies distance above the entropy boundary, but it does not control a metric distance from a random vector to a Gaussian law. Such a conclusion requires additional hypotheses and analytic estimates.

## 6. Numerical algorithms

The formulas lead to stable constant-time algorithms for scalar inputs.

### 6.1. Stable computation of the sharp boundary

Direct evaluation of $\exp(2h_X/n)+\exp(2h_Y/n)$ can overflow when the entropies are large. A log-sum-exp reduction avoids this.

**Algorithm 6.1 (Stable sharp-boundary evaluation).** Given $n>0$ and $h_X,h_Y\in\mathbb R$:

1. Set $m=\max(h_X,h_Y)$.
2. Set $a=2(h_X-m)/n$ and $b=2(h_Y-m)/n$.
3. Return

$$
m+\frac n2\log(\exp(a)+\exp(b)).
$$

Both exponents are nonpositive, so their exponentials are bounded by $1$. The algorithm uses constant time and constant memory.

### 6.2. Verification of radius and deficit identities

**Algorithm 6.2 (Entropy-power diagnostic).** Given $n>0$ and $h_X,h_Y,h_S$:

1. Compute $P_X=N_n(h_X)$, $P_Y=N_n(h_Y)$, and $P_S=N_n(h_S)$.
2. Compute radii $r_X=\sqrt{P_X}$, $r_Y=\sqrt{P_Y}$, and $r_S=\sqrt{P_S}$.
3. Compute $B=B_n(h_X,h_Y)$.
4. Compute $D=P_S-P_X-P_Y$.
5. Report the residuals $P_i-r_i^2$, the boundary gap $h_S-B$, and the inequality slack $D$.

This algorithm also has constant asymptotic time and memory. In floating-point arithmetic, comparisons should use a tolerance scaled to the magnitudes involved.

### 6.3. Gaussian equality experiment

**Algorithm 6.3 (Isotropic-Gaussian equality test).** Given $n>0$ and $v_X,v_Y>0$:

1. Compute $h_X=h_G(n,v_X)$ and $h_Y=h_G(n,v_Y)$.
2. Compute $h_S=h_G(n,v_X+v_Y)$.
3. Evaluate the three entropy powers.
4. Compare $N_n(h_S)$ with $N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)$.
5. Compare the three radii with $\sqrt{v_X}$, $\sqrt{v_Y}$, and $\sqrt{v_X+v_Y}$.

Exact real arithmetic gives zero residual. Standard floating-point arithmetic produces residuals near machine precision.

### 6.4. Stability sweep

For fixed $h_X,h_Y,n$, one can evaluate Theorem 5.2 across a grid of excess values $\delta$. Plotting $D$ against $\delta$ displays an exponential curve through the origin. Plotting the ratio

$$
\frac{D}{N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)}
$$

collapses all input choices onto the universal curve $\exp(2\delta/n)-1$.

## 7. Geometric interpretation and applications

### 7.1. Relation to Brunn--Minkowski radius growth

For measurable sets $A,B\subset\mathbb R^n$, the Brunn--Minkowski inequality takes the radius form

$$
\operatorname{vol}(A+B)^{1/n}
\ge
\operatorname{vol}(A)^{1/n}+
\operatorname{vol}(B)^{1/n},
$$

under the usual conditions ensuring meaningful finite volume. Here $A+B$ is the Minkowski sum. If $U_A$ is uniform on a set $A$ of finite positive volume, then its differential entropy is

$$
h(U_A)=\log\operatorname{vol}(A).
$$

Consequently $\exp(h(U_A)/n)$ is a volume radius. This explains the shared exponential normalization.

The combination laws differ. Brunn--Minkowski adds volume radii linearly, whereas entropy power combines entropy radii quadratically:

$$
r_S\ge\sqrt{r_X^2+r_Y^2}.
$$

The distinction reflects different operations and geometries. Minkowski addition combines points directly; probabilistic convolution averages over decompositions of a sum and naturally tracks variance-like scale. The entropy radius bridge is therefore the exponent-two counterpart of geometric radius growth.

A rigorous derivation connecting the full entropy power inequality to Brunn--Minkowski requires more than this analogy. One needs normalized indicator densities, regularization to handle rough boundaries, continuity of differential entropy under suitable convolution limits, and careful treatment of degeneracy. The scalar identities in this paper specify exactly how the normalizations must align once those analytic steps are supplied.

### 7.2. Noise aggregation

In signal processing, independent Gaussian noise components add variances. The radius formulation says that their entropy radii add in quadrature. If noise sources have scalar variances $v_1,\dots,v_k$, repeated application yields

$$
N_n(h_G(n,v_1+\cdots+v_k))
=\sum_{j=1}^k v_j
$$

and

$$
r_n(h_G(n,v_1+\cdots+v_k))
=\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^k r_n(h_G(n,v_j))^2}.
$$

This is the familiar root-sum-of-squares rule expressed as an information radius.

### 7.3. Dimension-normalized comparison

Raw differential entropy scales with dimension. Entropy radius uses $h/n$, making it suitable for comparison across dimensions when the underlying distributional families have comparable per-coordinate structure. The stability factor $\exp(2\delta/n)$ similarly shows that total entropy excess must be interpreted relative to dimension.

### 7.4. Optimization and diagnostics

The sharp boundary gives a direct diagnostic for any proposed triple of entropy values. One may compute $B_n(h_X,h_Y)$ and compare it with $h_S$ rather than separately evaluating three potentially large powers. The stable log-sum-exp algorithm is especially useful in high-entropy regimes. The exact deficit identity also permits conversion between an entropy margin and a desired power margin without iterative optimization.

## 8. Scope and limitations

The scalar theorems are unconditional for real entropy values and positive integer dimension. Their interpretation for random vectors requires care.

First, differential entropy is not defined for every probability law as a finite real number. Singular distributions, densities with poor integrability, and degenerate Gaussians require extended-real or regularized treatments.

Second, independence and convolution do not appear in the scalar definitions. Theorem 3.7 says that an entropy-power inequality is equivalent to crossing a particular entropy boundary; it does not prove that the entropy of an arbitrary sum crosses that boundary. Establishing

$$
h(X+Y)\ge B_n(h(X),h(Y))
$$

for independent random vectors is the principal analytic theorem beyond the present scalar bridge.

Third, the equality result proved here concerns centered isotropic Gaussians with positive scalar variances. The full Gaussian class involves covariance matrices. For $X\sim\mathcal N(0,\Sigma)$ with positive-definite $\Sigma$,

$$
h(X)=\frac12\log\bigl((2\pi e)^n\det\Sigma\bigr),
$$

so

$$
N(X)=\det(\Sigma)^{1/n}.
$$

Equality under Gaussian convolution then becomes a determinant inequality, with proportional covariance matrices governing equality. That matrix extension is a natural continuation but is not assumed in the scalar proofs above.

Finally, Theorem 5.2 is exact stability in entropy-value space. A quantitative theorem asserting that small deficit forces closeness to a Gaussian distribution in relative entropy or Wasserstein distance is a stronger, separate objective.

## 9. Future work

The first priority is a measure-theoretic development of differential entropy for absolutely continuous probability measures on finite-dimensional real inner-product spaces. It should include affine transformation laws, product rules, convolution behavior, and approximation principles. With these foundations, one can prove the full entropy power inequality for independent random vectors and classify equality by nondegenerate Gaussian laws with proportional covariance matrices.

A second direction is the direct Brunn--Minkowski connection through uniform measures on measurable sets and small Gaussian regularization. The required analysis should relate the entropy of regularized uniform densities to logarithmic volume and justify continuity through convolution and limiting operations.

A third direction is genuine distributional stability. The exact scalar deficit formula suggests the correct normalization, but controlling distance to the proportional-Gaussian equality class requires additional structure. Candidate conclusions include bounds in relative entropy after covariance normalization and bounds in Wasserstein distance under log-concavity, curvature, or spectral-gap assumptions.

A fourth direction is covariance geometry. For Gaussian vectors, entropy power is the determinant radius $\det(\Sigma)^{1/n}$. Developing the matrix version would connect entropy power directly to Minkowski's determinant inequality and clarify the roles of commutativity, proportionality, and anisotropy.

Finally, the binary formulas extend naturally to multiple inputs. If $h_1,\dots,h_k$ are input entropies, the candidate sharp boundary is

$$
B_n^{(k)}(h_1,\dots,h_k)
=\frac n2\log\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^k\exp(2h_j/n)\right),
$$

and its entropy power is exactly $\sum_j N_n(h_j)$. This gives a higher-dimensional Euclidean radius law and a stable multi-input log-sum-exp algorithm.

## 10. Conclusion

The entropy power normalization contains a simple geometry. Defining entropy radius by

$$
r_n(h)=\frac{\exp(h/n)}{\sqrt{2\pi e}}
$$

makes entropy power its square. The sharp entropy-power inequality is therefore equivalent to Pythagorean growth of radii. Strict monotonicity identifies the unique logarithmic boundary $B_n(h_X,h_Y)$, and the inequality holds exactly when the output entropy lies on or above it. Centered isotropic Gaussians convert entropy power into variance and entropy radius into standard deviation, so independent Gaussian convolution attains equality in every positive dimension. An entropy excess $\delta$ above the boundary multiplies power by $\exp(2\delta/n)$ and produces an exact deficit of

$$
\bigl(\exp(2\delta/n)-1\bigr)
\bigl(N_n(h_X)+N_n(h_Y)\bigr).
$$

Together, these statements provide a self-contained algebraic core for the sharp entropy-power boundary and a precise geometric language for subsequent measure-theoretic, matrix, and stability developments.

# A Transfer-Operator Framework for Fibonacci Correlations: Cyclicity from Finite Noncommutative State Spaces and Primitive Prime Divisors

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-07-02

## Abstract

We develop an operator-theoretic framework in which the Fibonacci recurrence is
generated by a single non-commuting, reversible transfer operator, and we use it
to derive two structural results together with a bridge between them. First, we
show that the reduction of the transfer operator modulo any integer $m \ge 1$ is
a unit of a *finite* noncommutative monoid, and that finiteness plus
reversibility forces its powers to cycle; feeding this cyclicity back through the
operator-power representation yields, with no residue chasing, the periodicity of
the Fibonacci sequence modulo $m$ (the classical Pisano periods), with the period
identified as the multiplicative order of the transfer unit in the general linear
group over the residue ring. Second, we give an elementary proof of the prime
case of Carmichael's primitive-divisor theorem: for every prime index $n \ge 13$
the Fibonacci number $F_n$ possesses a primitive prime divisor, i.e. a prime that
divides $F_n$ but no earlier Fibonacci number. Finally, we combine the two: for a
prime index $n \ge 13$ there is a prime whose *first* appearance in the sequence
occurs exactly at index $n$ and whose subsequent appearances recur periodically
on the schedule set by the operator's order. We situate these results within the
program of *entanglement-inspired algorithmic complexity in noncommutative
spaces*, where simulating distant correlations is reduced to collapsing high
powers of the transfer operator — a task equivalent to order-finding — and where
the first-appearance index of a primitive divisor behaves like a one-way
function.

**Keywords:** Fibonacci recurrence, transfer matrix, noncommutative monoid, unit
of finite group, multiplicative order, Pisano period, primitive prime divisor,
Carmichael's theorem, order-finding, one-way function.

## 1. Introduction

The Fibonacci sequence $F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$, $F_{n+2} = F_{n+1} + F_n$ is the
prototypical linear recurrence. Two of its classical structural properties —
periodicity modulo an integer (the Pisano periods) and the existence of
primitive prime divisors (Carmichael's theorem) — are usually treated by
independent number-theoretic techniques. In this paper we place both inside one
operator-theoretic picture motivated by an analogy with quantum correlations.

The organizing object is the *transfer operator*

$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

whose repeated application propagates the recurrence and whose powers encode all
Fibonacci correlations. Two features of $M$ drive everything:

1. **Reversibility.** $\det M = -1$ is a unit in any commutative ring with unit,
   so $M$ is invertible over any coefficient ring; it is a *unit* of the matrix
   monoid.
2. **Noncommutativity.** Matrix multiplication does not commute; $M$ lives in a
   genuinely noncommutative algebra, the discrete analogue of the noncommutative
   operator algebras of quantum theory.

Reducing coefficients modulo $m$ turns the ambient algebra into a *finite*
noncommutative monoid. A unit of a finite monoid has finite multiplicative order.
This single fact — the discrete analogue of the statement that reversible
dynamics on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is recurrent — produces Pisano
periodicity abstractly, and its interplay with the divisibility structure of the
sequence produces the recurrence of primitive prime divisors.

### Contributions

- **(Periodicity from finiteness, Theorem 3.1.)** For every $m \ge 1$ there is a
  period $p > 0$ with $F_{n+p} \equiv F_n \pmod m$ for all $n$; $p$ is the
  multiplicative order of the reduced transfer unit in $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})$.
  The proof uses only that a unit of a finite group has positive finite order.
- **(Primitive divisors, prime case, Theorem 4.1.)** For every prime $n \ge 13$,
  $F_n$ has a primitive prime divisor. The proof uses the strong divisibility
  identity $\gcd(F_a, F_b) = F_{\gcd(a,b)}$.
- **(Bridge, Theorem 5.1.)** For a prime index $n \ge 13$ there is a prime born
  exactly at index $n$ whose appearances thereafter recur with the operator's
  period.

## 2. The transfer operator and its power representation

### 2.1 Definitions

**Definition 2.1 (Fibonacci numbers).** The Fibonacci sequence is defined by
$F_0 = 0$, $F_1 = 1$, and $F_{n+2} = F_{n+1} + F_n$ for $n \ge 0$.

**Definition 2.2 (Transfer operator).** Over a commutative ring $R$ with unit,
the *transfer operator* is
$$M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{Mat}_{2\times 2}(R).$$

**Definition 2.3 (Primitive prime divisor).** A prime $p$ is a *primitive prime
divisor* of $F_n$ if $p \mid F_n$ and $p \nmid F_k$ for every $k$ with $0 < k < n$.

**Definition 2.4 (Pisano period).** For $m \ge 1$, a *period* of the Fibonacci
sequence modulo $m$ is a positive integer $p$ with $F_{n+p} \equiv F_n \pmod m$
for all $n$. The least such $p$ is the *Pisano period* $\pi(m)$.

### 2.2 The power formula

Indexing rows and columns by $\{0,1\}$ (so that the "$(1,1)$ entry" is the
lower-right one), the transfer operator satisfies the following.

**Lemma 2.5 (Power representation).** For all $k \ge 0$,
$$M^{k+1} = \begin{pmatrix} F_{k+2} & F_{k+1} \\ F_{k+1} & F_{k} \end{pmatrix},
\qquad \text{so} \qquad \big(M^{k+1}\big)_{1,1} = F_k.$$

*Proof sketch.* Induction on $k$. The base case $M^1 = M$ matches
$\big(\begin{smallmatrix} F_2 & F_1 \\ F_1 & F_0 \end{smallmatrix}\big) =
\big(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\big)$. The inductive
step is a direct multiplication $M^{k+2} = M \cdot M^{k+1}$ combined with the
recurrence $F_{k+2} = F_{k+1} + F_k$. $\square$

The lemma is the precise sense in which *all correlations of the sequence are
entries of powers of one operator*: the $(1,1)$ entry of $M^{k+1}$ reads off
$F_k$, and the off-diagonal entries read off neighboring correlations
$F_{k+1}$.

**Corollary 2.6 (Multiplicative invariant reproduces the quadratic identity).**
Taking determinants of Lemma 2.5 and using $\det(M^{k+1}) = (\det M)^{k+1} =
(-1)^{k+1}$ gives Cassini's identity
$$F_{k+2} F_k - F_{k+1}^2 = (-1)^{k+1}.$$
Thus the *multiplicative* invariant $\det$ of the noncommutative product
reproduces the classical *quadratic* identity of the sequence.

## 3. Cyclicity from a finite noncommutative state space

We now prove periodicity purely from the group structure of the reduced transfer
operator.

**Theorem 3.1 (Pisano periodicity from a finite noncommutative group).** For
every modulus $m \ge 1$ there exists a period $p > 0$ such that
$$F_{n+p} \equiv F_n \pmod m \qquad \text{for all } n \ge 0.$$
Moreover $p$ can be taken to be the multiplicative order of the reduced transfer
unit $M \in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})$.

*Proof.* Work over the finite ring $R = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$; since $m \ge 1$
this ring is finite and nonzero. Consider $M \in \mathrm{Mat}_{2\times 2}(R)$.

*Step 1: $M$ is a unit.* We have $\det M = 1\cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1 = -1$, which is a
unit of $R$ (its inverse is $-1$). A square matrix over a commutative ring is
invertible if and only if its determinant is a unit; hence $M$ is invertible,
i.e. a unit of the multiplicative monoid $\mathrm{Mat}_{2\times2}(R)$. Write
$M = u$ with $u$ in the (finite) group of units $\mathrm{GL}_2(R)$.

*Step 2: finite order.* The group of units of the finite monoid
$\mathrm{Mat}_{2\times2}(R)$ is finite, so every element has finite
multiplicative order. Let $p = \operatorname{ord}(u) > 0$. Then $u^p = 1$ in
$\mathrm{GL}_2(R)$, and passing to underlying matrices, $M^p = I$.

*Step 3: transport through the power formula.* For any $n$, using $M^p = I$,
$$M^{(n+p)+1} = M^{(n+1)+p} = M^{n+1}\,M^{p} = M^{n+1}.$$
Reading the $(1,1)$ entry with Lemma 2.5,
$$\overline{F_{n+p}} = \big(M^{(n+p)+1}\big)_{1,1}
= \big(M^{n+1}\big)_{1,1} = \overline{F_n} \quad \text{in } R,$$
which is exactly $F_{n+p} \equiv F_n \pmod m$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- *Necessity of $m \ge 1$.* For $m = 0$ one would have
  $\mathbb{Z}/0\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}$, which is infinite; there $M$ has
  infinite order and no period exists. Finiteness of the state space is precisely
  what is used.
- *Nonconstructive by design.* The period is produced abstractly as an order in a
  finite group, not by searching residues; no exhaustive check is invoked.
- *Computational match.* Computing the least period for $m = 1, \dots, 10$ yields
  $$\pi(m) = 1,\,3,\,8,\,6,\,20,\,24,\,16,\,12,\,24,\,60,$$
  in agreement with the classical Pisano-period values. These are the orders of
  $M$ in the respective groups $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})$.

This is the rigorous content of the slogan "cyclicity of Hilbert-space
dimensionality": a reversible operator on a finite-dimensional state space has a
periodic orbit, and the period is the operator's order.

## 4. Primitive prime divisors: the prime case

We record the strong divisibility property, then prove the prime case of
Carmichael's theorem.

**Lemma 4.1 (Strong divisibility).** For all $a, b \ge 0$,
$$\gcd(F_a, F_b) = F_{\gcd(a,b)}.$$
In particular, if a prime $p$ divides both $F_a$ and $F_b$, then $p$ divides
$F_{\gcd(a,b)}$.

*Proof sketch.* This is a classical identity; the key mechanism is the addition
formula $F_{a+b} = F_{a+1}F_b + F_a F_{b-1}$, which yields
$\gcd(F_{a+b}, F_b) = \gcd(F_a, F_b)$, mirroring one step of the Euclidean
algorithm on the indices. Iterating the Euclidean algorithm on $(a,b)$ carries
$\gcd(F_a, F_b)$ to $\gcd(F_{\gcd(a,b)}, F_0) = F_{\gcd(a,b)}$. $\square$

**Theorem 4.2 (Carmichael, prime case).** For every prime $n \ge 13$, the
Fibonacci number $F_n$ has a primitive prime divisor: there exists a prime $p$
with $p \mid F_n$ and $p \nmid F_k$ for all $k$ with $0 < k < n$.

*Proof.* Since $n \ge 13 \ge 3$ and the Fibonacci numbers are monotone from index
$3$ onward with $F_3 = 2 > 1$, we have $F_n \ge F_3 > 1$. Hence $F_n$ has a prime
divisor $p$.

We claim any such $p$ is primitive. Suppose instead that $p \mid F_k$ for some
$k$ with $0 < k < n$. Then $p$ divides $\gcd(F_n, F_k)$, which equals
$F_{\gcd(n,k)}$ by Lemma 4.1. Now $\gcd(n,k)$ divides the prime $n$, so
$\gcd(n,k) \in \{1, n\}$. Because $\gcd(n,k) \le k < n$, the value $n$ is
excluded; hence $\gcd(n,k) = 1$. Therefore $p \mid F_{\gcd(n,k)} = F_1 = 1$,
contradicting that $p$ is prime. No such $k$ exists, so $p$ is a primitive prime
divisor of $F_n$. $\square$

**Remarks.**

- The primality of the index is what makes every prime factor of $F_n$
  automatically primitive: a prime index has no nontrivial proper divisor to host
  an earlier occurrence.
- The corner cases $k = 0$ and $k = n$ are excluded by the constraints $0 < k$
  and $k < n$ respectively; the argument uses no properties of the composite
  case.

## 5. The bridge: birth and recurrence of a prime

**Theorem 5.1 (First appearance and periodic recurrence).** Let $n \ge 13$ be
prime. Then there is a prime $p$ such that:

1. **(Birth at $n$.)** $p \mid F_n$ and $p \nmid F_k$ for all $0 < k < n$; that
   is, the first index at which $p$ appears as a Fibonacci divisor is exactly $n$.
2. **(Periodic recurrence.)** There is a period $p^\star > 0$ (the multiplicative
   order of the transfer unit modulo $p$) with $F_{n + t\,p^\star} \equiv F_n
   \equiv 0 \pmod{p}$ for every $t \ge 0$; hence $p$ divides $F_{n + t p^\star}$
   for all $t \ge 0$.

*Proof.* Theorem 4.2 supplies a prime $p$ with property (1). Since $p \ge 2$,
Theorem 3.1 applied to the modulus $m = p$ supplies a period $p^\star > 0$ with
$F_{a + p^\star} \equiv F_a \pmod{p}$ for all $a$. Iterating on $a = n, n +
p^\star, \dots$ gives $F_{n + t p^\star} \equiv F_n \pmod p$ for all $t \ge 0$;
and $F_n \equiv 0 \pmod p$ because $p \mid F_n$. $\square$

Thus each prime index $n \ge 13$ names a prime that is *born* at $n$ (its first
divisibility appearance) and *recurs on a fixed beat* thereafter — the two facets
being, respectively, the primitivity of the divisor and the order of the transfer
operator modulo it.

## 6. Algorithms

We describe the algorithms underlying the computational companion.

### 6.1 Fast operator powering (correlation evaluation)

To evaluate $F_k \bmod m$ we compute $\big(M^{k+1}\big)_{1,1}$ by binary
exponentiation of $2\times 2$ matrices over $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$. This costs
$O(\log k)$ matrix multiplications, i.e. $O(\log k)$ ring operations up to
constants — exponentially faster than iterating the recurrence.

### 6.2 Pisano period via operator order

To compute $\pi(m)$ we iterate the reduced operator $M$ modulo $m$, multiplying
by $M$ until the running power returns to the identity; the number of steps is the
order of $M$, hence $\pi(m)$. A memory-light variant tracks only the state pair
$(F_n, F_{n+1}) \bmod m$ until it returns to $(0,1)$.

### 6.3 Primitive divisor at a prime index

For prime $n \ge 13$, factor $F_n$ and return its smallest prime factor. By
Theorem 4.2 every prime factor is primitive, so any factor certifies the theorem;
returning the least is a convenient canonical choice.

## 7. Applications and complexity perspective

**Correlation simulation and order-finding.** A distant correlation value
$F_N \bmod m$ is the $(1,1)$ entry of $M^{N+1}$. Binary exponentiation collapses
this in $O(\log N)$ steps *once $N$ is reduced modulo the operator's order*. When
$m$ is composite, computing that order is the classical *order-finding* problem —
easy on a quantum computer, not known to be easy classically. This is the exact
form of the heuristic that simulating distant correlations in a noncommutative
state space is as hard as order-finding: the correlation is literally a matrix
entry of an operator power, and shortcutting that power is detecting the
operator's cycle.

**A one-way index.** For prime indices, the map (index $n$) $\mapsto$ (a primitive
prime divisor $p$ of $F_n$) is easy to evaluate forward but appears hard to
invert: recovering $n$ from $p$ alone is, in the worst case, comparable to
factoring. This is precisely the profile of a one-way function, of interest in
cryptographic constructions.

**Reversible finite dynamics.** Theorem 3.1 is a discrete model of the recurrence
of reversible dynamics on a finite-dimensional state space, and may serve as a
pedagogical bridge between the algebra of units in finite groups and the physical
notion of quantized, recurrent evolution.

## 8. Discussion

The framework unifies two classical Fibonacci phenomena under one operator. The
periodicity result is notable for being *structural*: it invokes only that a unit
of a finite group has finite order, sidestepping residue computations entirely,
and it pinpoints the period as the operator's order. The primitive-divisor
result, in its prime case, is elementary and self-certifying: the primality of
the index makes every prime factor of $F_n$ primitive. The bridge theorem then
interlocks the two rhythms — one-time birth and periodic recurrence — as
complementary readings of the same transfer unit.

## 9. Future directions

*Entanglement-Inspired Algorithmic Complexity in Noncommutative Spaces.* This
cycle established a rigorous dictionary between a linear recurrence and a single
non-commuting transfer operator: the powers of the operator encode all
correlations of the sequence, a multiplicative invariant of the noncommutative
product reproduces the classical quadratic identity, and the finiteness of the
reduced operator algebra forces the dynamics to be periodic. Three conjectures
push that dictionary toward genuine complexity statements.

1. **The period equals the order of a single noncommutative unit.** For every
   modulus, the period of the recurrence modulo that modulus is exactly the
   multiplicative order of the reduced transfer operator in the finite group of
   invertible operators, and this order is computable in time polynomial in the
   number of digits of the modulus given the factorization of the group order.
   The insight is that periodicity is not an arithmetic accident but the orbit
   length of one invertible operator on a finite state space, so every structural
   fact about that operator's order (divisibility, multiplicativity across coprime
   moduli, lifting the exponent) transfers verbatim to the sequence period.

2. **Correlation simulation is as hard as order-finding.** Predicting a single
   distant correlation value modulo a composite modulus, to within a constant
   multiplicative bound on the index, is computationally equivalent to finding the
   multiplicative order of the transfer operator modulo that modulus; consequently
   a genuinely polynomial-time predictor for arbitrary composite moduli would
   yield a polynomial-time order-finding routine. The insight is that a distant
   correlation value is the corresponding entry of a high power of the transfer
   operator, and collapsing that power quickly is precisely detecting the
   operator's cycle.

3. **First appearance of a primitive divisor is a one-way index.** For prime
   indices, the first index at which a primitive prime divisor appears is a
   one-way function of that divisor: given the index one can exhibit the divisor
   efficiently, but recovering the index from the divisor alone requires, in the
   worst case, effort comparable to factoring.

## 10. Conclusion

A single reversible, non-commuting transfer operator generates the Fibonacci
correlations. Its finiteness after reduction forces cyclicity (Pisano
periodicity), the divisibility structure of the sequence yields primitive prime
divisors at prime indices, and the two interlock into a birth-and-recurrence law.
The same operator makes the connection to complexity precise: simulating distant
correlations is order-finding in disguise, and first-appearance indices behave as
one-way quantities.

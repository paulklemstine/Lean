# Lorentzian Berggren Geodesic Rigidity: Unique Decoding of Pythagorean Triples via Minkowski Geometry

## Abstract

We formalize a rigidity theorem for the Berggren semigroup — the free semigroup on three
generators that enumerates all primitive Pythagorean triples — by identifying it as a
positive cone inside the integral Lorentz group O(2,1;ℤ). We define two linear functionals
σ₁, σ₂ whose sign patterns uniquely identify the first generator applied to any positive
null triple, then prove by induction that the orbit map from words to Pythagorean triples
is injective. All results are machine-verified in Lean 4 with Mathlib.

**Keywords:** Pythagorean triples, Berggren tree, Lorentz group, null cone, orbit rigidity, formal verification

---

## 1. Introduction

### The Berggren Tree

Every primitive Pythagorean triple (a, b, c) with a odd, b even can be obtained from
the root triple (3, 4, 5) by a unique sequence of three linear transformations, discovered
independently by Berggren (1934) and Barning (1963). The three generators are:

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad
C = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 & 2 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \\ -2 & 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$$

Applied iteratively to (3, 4, 5), these generate an infinite ternary tree whose nodes
are exactly all primitive Pythagorean triples (with a chosen parity convention).

### The Lorentzian Connection

The key observation — which transforms the Berggren tree from number theory into
geometry — is that each generator preserves the Minkowski quadratic form

$$Q(v) = v_0^2 + v_1^2 - v_2^2$$

That is, if J = diag(1, 1, −1), then Mᵀ J M = J for each M ∈ {A, B, C}. This means
the Berggren generators lie inside the integral Lorentz group O(2,1;ℤ), and the
Pythagorean condition a² + b² = c² is simply the equation Q(a,b,c) = 0 — the null
cone of Minkowski space.

### Our Contribution

We prove that the map from Berggren words to Pythagorean triples is injective —
every primitive Pythagorean triple determines a unique word in {A, B, C}*. The
proof is constructive: we exhibit two linear functionals whose sign patterns at
any orbit point uniquely identify the first generator, enabling recursive decoding.

All proofs are machine-verified in Lean 4 using the Mathlib library.

## 2. Mathematical Framework

### 2.1 The Minkowski Form

**Definition.** The Minkowski metric tensor in (2+1) dimensions is

$$J = \text{diag}(1, 1, -1) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

A matrix M ∈ M₃(ℤ) *preserves the Minkowski form* if Mᵀ J M = J.

**Theorem 1** (Lean: `A_preservesMinkowski`, `B_preservesMinkowski`, `C_preservesMinkowski`).
*Each Berggren generator A, B, C preserves the Minkowski form.*

**Theorem 2** (Lean: `preservesMinkowski_mul`).
*If M and N preserve the Minkowski form, then so does MN. Hence the semigroup
generated by A, B, C lies inside O(2,1;ℤ).*

### 2.2 The Null Cone

**Definition.** A vector v ∈ ℤ³ lies on the *null cone* if Q(v) = v₀² + v₁² − v₂² = 0.
It is a *positive null triple* if additionally v₀, v₁, v₂ > 0.

**Theorem 3** (Lean: `preserves_nullcone`).
*Any Minkowski-preserving matrix M satisfies Q(Mv) = Q(v) for all v ∈ ℤ³.*

*Proof.* We show Q(v) = vᵀ J v (as a scalar), so Q(Mv) = (Mv)ᵀ J (Mv) = vᵀ(Mᵀ J M)v = vᵀ J v = Q(v). □

**Lemma 4** (Lean: `null_cone_lt`).
*If Q(v) = 0 and v₀, v₁, v₂ > 0, then v₀ < v₂ and v₁ < v₂.*

*Proof.* From v₂² = v₀² + v₁² > v₀² and both positive, v₂ > v₀. Similarly v₂ > v₁. □

### 2.3 Positivity Preservation

**Theorem 5** (Lean: `gen_preserves_positive`).
*Each generator maps positive null triples to positive null triples.*

The proof uses Lemma 4 to derive explicit inequalities. For instance, for generator A
applied to (x, y, z) with x² + y² = z² and x, y, z > 0:
- Component 0: x − 2y + 2z = x + 2(z − y) > 0 since z > y
- Component 1: 2x − y + 2z > 2x + y > 0 since 2z > 2y > y
- Component 2: 2x − 2y + 3z > z > 0

### 2.4 Hypotenuse Growth

**Theorem 6** (Lean: `hypotenuse_increase`).
*For any generator G and positive null triple v, (Gv)₂ > v₂.*

This ensures that non-empty words always produce triples with hypotenuse strictly
greater than 5, the hypotenuse of the root triple.

## 3. The Sector Separation Theorem

This is the geometric heart of the paper. We define two linear functionals:

$$\sigma_1(v) = v_0 + 2v_1 - 2v_2, \qquad \sigma_2(v) = 2v_0 + v_1 - 2v_2$$

**Theorem 7** (Lean: `sigma1_after_A` through `sigma2_after_C`).
*For any v ∈ ℤ³:*
| Generator G | σ₁(Gv) | σ₂(Gv) |
|:-----------:|:------:|:------:|
| A | v₀ | −v₁ |
| B | v₀ | v₁ |
| C | −v₀ | v₁ |

*Proof.* Direct computation from the matrix entries. For instance,
σ₁(Av) = (Av)₀ + 2(Av)₁ − 2(Av)₂
= (x − 2y + 2z) + 2(2x − y + 2z) − 2(2x − 2y + 3z)
= x + 4x − 4x + (−2y − 2y + 4y) + (2z + 4z − 6z) = x = v₀. □

**Corollary 8.** *For a positive null triple v (so v₀, v₁ > 0), the sign patterns are:*
- *After A: σ₁ > 0, σ₂ < 0*
- *After B: σ₁ > 0, σ₂ > 0*
- *After C: σ₁ < 0, σ₂ > 0*

*These three patterns are pairwise distinct.*

**Theorem 9** (Lean: `first_letter_eq`).
*If (genMatrix g) · u = (genMatrix h) · w for positive null triples u, w, then g = h.*

*Proof.* The sign pattern of (σ₁, σ₂) at the image uniquely determines the generator,
since the three patterns are distinct. □

## 4. Main Rigidity Theorem

**Theorem 10** (Lean: `berggren_orbit_injective`).
*The map w ↦ tripleOfWord(w) from Berggren words to ℤ³ is injective.*

*Proof.* By induction on w. If w = [], then tripleOfWord(w) = (3,4,5), which has
hypotenuse 5. Any non-empty word w' has tripleOfWord(w')₂ > 5 by Theorem 6,
so tripleOfWord(w) ≠ tripleOfWord(w').

If w = g :: t and w' = h :: t', then
(genMatrix g) · tripleOfWord(t) = (genMatrix h) · tripleOfWord(t').
By Theorem 5, both tripleOfWord(t) and tripleOfWord(t') are positive null triples.
By Theorem 9, g = h. Since genMatrix(g) has determinant ±1 (hence is invertible
over ℤ), we can cancel to get tripleOfWord(t) = tripleOfWord(t'), and by induction
t = t'. □

**Corollary 11** (Lean: `berggren_decode_unique`).
*The displacement profile (the orbit triple) uniquely determines the Berggren word.*

**Corollary 12** (Lean: `berggren_first_letter_unique`).
*Distinct generators always produce distinct orbit triples, regardless of the
continuation word.*

## 5. The Decoding Algorithm

The proof is constructive and yields an efficient recursive decoder:

```
DECODE(v):
  if v = (3, 4, 5): return ε (empty word)
  s₁ ← v₀ + 2v₁ − 2v₂
  s₂ ← 2v₀ + v₁ − 2v₂
  if s₁ > 0 and s₂ < 0:
    g ← A
  elif s₁ > 0 and s₂ > 0:
    g ← B
  else:  // s₁ < 0 and s₂ > 0
    g ← C
  v' ← (J · gᵀ · J) · v    // apply inverse
  return g · DECODE(v')
```

The algorithm terminates because each step strictly decreases the hypotenuse (Theorem 6).
The inverse is computed via the Minkowski identity G⁻¹ = J Gᵀ J, which holds for any
Minkowski-preserving matrix with determinant ±1.

## 6. Discussion: What Does This Mean?

### For a General Audience

Imagine you have a perfect right triangle with integer sides — like the famous 3-4-5
triangle. The ancient Babylonians knew many such triangles. In 1934, the Swedish
mathematician Berggren discovered that ALL such triangles can be organized into
an infinite family tree, where the 3-4-5 triangle sits at the root, and every other
integer right triangle is obtained by applying one of three simple transformations
to its parent.

What we've shown — and machine-verified — is that this family tree has a hidden
geometric structure: it lives inside *Minkowski spacetime*, the mathematical framework
Einstein used for special relativity. The condition for a right triangle (a² + b² = c²)
is exactly the condition for lying on the "light cone" in a 2+1-dimensional spacetime.
The three Berggren transformations are *Lorentz transformations* — the same symmetries
that relate different observers in special relativity.

The rigidity theorem says that this tree has *no collisions*: every integer right triangle
appears exactly once, and you can tell exactly which path through the tree leads to
any given triangle just by computing two simple numbers (σ₁ and σ₂) and checking
their signs. This is analogous to how GPS works: a small number of measurements
uniquely determines your position.

### For Physicists

The Berggren semigroup is a positive cone inside the arithmetic Lorentz group O(2,1;ℤ).
The orbit of (3,4,5) under this semigroup fills the positive integer null cone — the
discrete analogue of the future light cone. The sector separation functionals σ₁, σ₂
define a "causal partition" of the light cone into three disjoint sectors, one for each
generator. This is strongly reminiscent of causal diamond decompositions in Lorentzian
geometry.

The hypotenuse growth theorem (Theorem 6) says that each generator acts as a "boost"
that strictly increases the time-like coordinate z. In the continuous case, this would
correspond to a translation along a geodesic in the hyperbolic plane H², and the
growth rate would be the translation length. Our discrete version captures the essential
monotonicity needed for unique decoding.

### For Cryptographers

The rigidity theorem establishes that the Berggren semigroup acts *freely* on the root
triple — no two distinct semigroup elements have the same effect. This is precisely
the kind of one-way structure that underlies group-based cryptographic protocols:
computing the orbit point from the word is easy (matrix multiplication), but recovering
the word from the orbit point requires the specific algebraic structure we've formalized.

The decoding algorithm shows that this particular one-way function is invertible (so
not cryptographically secure as-is), but the general principle — recovering hidden
semigroup elements from spectral leakage — is central to post-quantum cryptographic
models based on arithmetic groups.

## 7. Formalization Details

The entire development consists of approximately 500 lines of Lean 4 code, using the
Mathlib library. Key design decisions:

1. **Integer matrices throughout**: All computations are over ℤ, avoiding the coercion
   headaches that arise with ℝ-valued proofs.

2. **`native_decide` for concrete verification**: The Minkowski preservation conditions
   Mᵀ J M = J and the inverse matrix identities M⁻¹ M = I are verified by computation.

3. **Algebraic proofs for universal statements**: The sigma formulas, null cone
   preservation, and positivity preservation are proved algebraically (using `ring`,
   `nlinarith`, and matrix rewriting).

4. **Structural induction for the main theorem**: The orbit injectivity proof uses
   induction on the word, with the hypotenuse growth providing the well-foundedness
   argument for the base case.

### Axioms Used

The proofs depend only on the standard Lean axioms: `propext`, `Classical.choice`,
`Quot.sound`, `Lean.ofReduceBool`, and `Lean.trustCompiler`. No additional axioms
or `sorry` statements remain.

## 8. Related Work

The Berggren tree has been studied extensively in elementary number theory. The
classical references are Berggren (1934) and Barning (1963). The connection to
the Lorentz group O(2,1) was noted by several authors, including Hall (1970) and
more recently in the context of Markov triples and hyperbolic geometry.

The formal verification of properties of the Berggren tree appears to be new.
Previous Lean formalizations of Pythagorean triples exist in Mathlib but do
not address the tree structure or the orbit rigidity question.

## 9. Conclusion

We have formalized a complete proof chain from the Lorentzian structure of the
Berggren generators to the unique decodability of Pythagorean triples from their
displacement profile. The sector separation theorem (Theorem 7) is the key new
ingredient: it identifies two specific linear functionals whose sign patterns
cleanly partition the positive null cone into three generator-specific sectors.

This result bridges discrete number theory (Pythagorean triples), Lorentzian
geometry (null cones and Lorentz isometries), and formal verification (machine-checked
proofs in Lean 4), demonstrating the power of geometric thinking in arithmetic
problems.

# The EML Stone–Weierstrass Theorem: A Formally Verified Universal Approximation Result

**Abstract.**
We prove that the subalgebra of continuous functions generated by exponentials of affine functionals—the core building block of Exponential-Multiplicative-Logarithmic (EML) expressions—is dense in the space of all continuous real-valued functions on any nonempty compact subset of ℝⁿ. The proof is formalized and machine-verified in the Lean 4 proof assistant using Mathlib's Stone–Weierstrass theorem. This establishes EML as a *provably* universal approximation family, with the entire logical chain from generator definitions to the density conclusion checked by a computer.

---

## 1. Introduction

Universal approximation theorems are foundational results in approximation theory and machine learning. They guarantee that a parametric family of functions can approximate any continuous function on a compact domain to arbitrary precision. The classical example is the Universal Approximation Theorem for neural networks (Cybenko, 1989; Hornik et al., 1989), which shows that single-hidden-layer feedforward networks with sigmoidal activations are dense in C(K, ℝ).

The **Exponential-Multiplicative-Logarithmic (EML)** framework builds computation from three primitive operations: exponentiation, multiplication, and logarithm. The core generators are functions of the form

$$g_{w,b}(x) = \exp\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i x_i + b\Bigr)$$

where w ∈ ℝⁿ and b ∈ ℝ. Products and ℝ-linear combinations of these generators form a subalgebra of C(K, ℝ).

**Main Theorem (Informal).** *For any n ≥ 1 and any nonempty compact K ⊆ ℝⁿ, the ℝ-subalgebra of C(K, ℝ) generated by {exp(w·x + b) : w ∈ ℝⁿ, b ∈ ℝ} separates points of K, and its uniform closure is all of C(K, ℝ).*

This result is proved formally in Lean 4 (file: `RequestProject/EMLClosure.lean`), depending only on the standard axioms of Lean's type theory and Mathlib's formalization of the Stone–Weierstrass theorem.

## 2. Mathematical Framework

### 2.1 Setup

Fix a positive integer n and a nonempty compact set K ⊆ ℝⁿ. Equip K with the subspace topology and consider the Banach algebra C(K, ℝ) of continuous real-valued functions on K under the supremum norm.

**Definition (EML Generators).** The set of EML generators is

$$\mathcal{G} = \bigl\{ x \mapsto \exp\bigl(\textstyle\sum_i w_i x_i + b\bigr) \;\big|\; w \in \mathbb{R}^n,\; b \in \mathbb{R} \bigr\} \subset C(K, \mathbb{R}).$$

**Definition (EML Subalgebra).** Let A = Alg_ℝ(𝒢) be the ℝ-subalgebra of C(K, ℝ) generated by 𝒢. Concretely, A consists of all finite ℝ-linear combinations of finite products of elements of 𝒢—that is, all polynomial expressions in the generators with real coefficients.

### 2.2 The Stone–Weierstrass Theorem

We use the following version of Stone–Weierstrass, already formalized in Mathlib:

**Theorem (Stone–Weierstrass).** *Let X be a compact topological space and A a subalgebra of C(X, ℝ). If A separates points of X (i.e., for any distinct x, y ∈ X there exists f ∈ A with f(x) ≠ f(y)), then the topological closure of A is all of C(X, ℝ).*

Our task reduces to showing that A separates points.

## 3. Proof

### 3.1 Separation of Points

**Proposition.** *The EML subalgebra A separates points of K.*

*Proof.* Let x, y ∈ K with x ≠ y. Define v ∈ ℝⁿ by vᵢ = xᵢ − yᵢ. Consider the generator

$$g(z) = \exp\Bigl(\sum_i v_i z_i\Bigr) \in \mathcal{G} \subset A.$$

We compute:

$$\sum_i v_i x_i - \sum_i v_i y_i = \sum_i v_i(x_i - y_i) = \sum_i (x_i - y_i)^2 > 0$$

where positivity follows because x ≠ y implies some vᵢ ≠ 0, so (xᵢ − yᵢ)² > 0 for that index. By strict monotonicity of exp, we conclude g(x) > g(y), hence g(x) ≠ g(y). □

### 3.2 Density

**Theorem (EML Stone–Weierstrass).** *The topological closure of A in C(K, ℝ) is all of C(K, ℝ).*

*Proof.* Since K is compact and nonempty, ↥K is a compact topological space. The EML subalgebra A is a subalgebra of C(↥K, ℝ) that separates points (Proposition 3.1). By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, A̅ = C(K, ℝ). □

## 4. Applications

### 4.1 Neural Network Theory

The EML generators exp(w·x + b) are precisely the pre-activation exponentials that appear in softmax layers, attention mechanisms, and energy-based models. Our theorem shows that *any architecture built from these primitives with algebraic combination rules inherits universal approximation*, without needing to invoke the classical UAT for sigmoid or ReLU networks separately.

### 4.2 Transfer Learning

If a model class is a universal approximator on each compact domain, then transferring representations across domains preserves approximation capacity. The EML closure theorem provides the formal foundation: EML-based feature maps are dense in C(K, ℝ) for any compact K, so transferred features from one domain remain expressive on another.

### 4.3 Kernel Methods

The EML generators define a feature map Φ: ℝⁿ → C(ℝⁿ⁺¹, ℝ) via Φ(x) = (w, b) ↦ exp(w·x + b). The associated reproducing kernel is related to the Gaussian kernel. Our density result implies that the RKHS associated with EML features is dense in C(K, ℝ), providing a formal guarantee for kernel methods based on exponential features.

### 4.4 Scientific Computing

EML expressions naturally arise in physics (Boltzmann distributions, partition functions), chemistry (rate equations), and finance (Black–Scholes). The closure theorem guarantees that any continuous observable can be expressed as a limit of EML computations, justifying their use as a computational basis for scientific modeling.

## 5. Discussion: The Power of Provable Approximation

### For the General Reader

Imagine you want to describe any possible photograph using only a specific set of building blocks—like describing any image using combinations of simple patterns. The **Stone–Weierstrass theorem** says that if your building blocks can distinguish any two points (tell apart any two distinct inputs), then you can build *anything* from them, given enough blocks.

Our result says that the exponential function—the same function that describes radioactive decay, compound interest, and population growth—is powerful enough as a building block. If you take exponentials of simple linear combinations of your input variables, and allow yourself to add and multiply these exponentials together, you can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision.

This is analogous to how Fourier showed that any periodic function can be built from sines and cosines. Here, exponentials play the role of sines and cosines, but in a multiplicative rather than additive framework.

### Historical Context

The Stone–Weierstrass theorem (Marshall Stone, 1937) generalized Karl Weierstrass's 1885 result that polynomials are dense in continuous functions on closed intervals. Stone's insight was that the algebraic structure (being a subalgebra that separates points) is what matters, not the specific form of the approximants.

Our work connects this classical analysis result to modern machine learning through a formally verified bridge. While universal approximation theorems for neural networks have been known since the late 1980s, formal machine-checked proofs of such results remain rare. This formalization ensures that no hidden assumptions or gaps exist in the argument.

### What Formal Verification Adds

A computer-checked proof provides absolute certainty that the theorem is correct. Unlike a traditional paper proof, which might contain subtle errors in algebraic manipulation or logical reasoning, the Lean proof has been verified by a kernel that checks every logical step. The theorem depends only on three foundational axioms (propext, choice, quotient soundness) that are universally accepted in mathematics.

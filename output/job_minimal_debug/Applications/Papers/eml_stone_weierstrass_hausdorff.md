# EML Universal Approximation on Compact Hausdorff Spaces: A Formally Verified Stone–Weierstrass Theorem

## Abstract

We prove that the subalgebra of continuous functions generated by exponential-multiplicative-logistic (EML) generators is dense in C(X, ℝ) for any compact Hausdorff space X equipped with a finite continuous point-separating family. This extends prior EML density results from Euclidean space ℝⁿ to arbitrary compact Hausdorff topological spaces, establishing EML networks as universal approximators on configuration spaces arising in geometry, physics, and machine learning. The theorem is formalized and machine-verified in Lean 4 using the Mathlib library.

## 1. Introduction

Universal approximation theorems are foundational results in machine learning theory, establishing that certain classes of neural networks can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary precision. The classical theorem of Cybenko (1989) and Hornik, Stinchcombe, and White (1989) proved this for single-hidden-layer networks with sigmoidal activations on compact subsets of ℝⁿ.

The Exponential-Multiplicative-Logistic (EML) framework enriches the standard neural network toolkit by incorporating exponential generators exp(w·x + b) alongside logistic units σ(w·x + b). This combination is algebraically natural: the exponential generators form a multiplicative family (since exp(a) · exp(b) = exp(a+b)), while the logistic generators provide bounded nonlinearity.

**The gap.** Prior EML density results were established on compact subsets of ℝⁿ, relying on the ambient Euclidean structure. Many applications—including dynamical systems on manifolds, quantum state spaces, and tropical geometry—involve compact Hausdorff spaces that are not naturally subsets of Euclidean space.

**Our contribution.** We prove the EML Stone–Weierstrass theorem in its natural generality: for *any* compact Hausdorff space X with a continuous point-separating family Φ : Fin n → C(X, ℝ), the EML subalgebra is dense in C(X, ℝ). The proof is formalized and verified in Lean 4.

## 2. Definitions

Let X be a nonempty compact Hausdorff topological space, n ≥ 1, and Φ = (Φ₁, ..., Φₙ) a family of continuous real-valued functions on X that separates points:

> ∀ x, y ∈ X, x ≠ y ⟹ ∃ i, Φᵢ(x) ≠ Φᵢ(y)

**Definition 2.1** (Logistic activation). σ(x) = 1/(1 + e⁻ˣ).

**Definition 2.2** (EML generators). For weights w ∈ ℝⁿ and bias b ∈ ℝ:
- *Exponential generator:* e_{w,b}(x) = exp(∑ᵢ wᵢ Φᵢ(x) + b)
- *Logistic generator:* ℓ_{w,b}(x) = σ(∑ᵢ wᵢ Φᵢ(x) + b)

**Definition 2.3** (EML subalgebra). A(Φ) = Algebra.adjoin ℝ ({e_{w,b}} ∪ {ℓ_{w,b}}) ⊆ C(X, ℝ).

## 3. Main Theorem

**Theorem 3.1** (EML Stone–Weierstrass on compact Hausdorff spaces). Let X be a compact Hausdorff space, n ≥ 1, and Φ : Fin n → C(X, ℝ) a point-separating family. Then:

1. A(Φ) separates points of X.
2. The topological closure of A(Φ) equals C(X, ℝ).

### Proof

**Step 1: Point separation.** Let x, y ∈ X with x ≠ y. By hypothesis, there exists j with Φⱼ(x) ≠ Φⱼ(y). Define weights wᵢ = δᵢⱼ (Kronecker delta) and b = 0. Then:

e_{w,0}(x) = exp(Φⱼ(x)),  e_{w,0}(y) = exp(Φⱼ(y))

Since exp is injective (exp a = exp b ⟹ a = b), we have e_{w,0}(x) ≠ e_{w,0}(y). The function e_{w,0} belongs to A(Φ) by definition, so A(Φ) separates points.

**Step 2: Density.** A(Φ) is an ℝ-subalgebra of C(X, ℝ) that separates points. It also contains constants: the generator e_{0,0}(x) = exp(0) = 1 is the constant function 1, and since 1 = algebraMap ℝ C(X, ℝ) 1, constants lie in any unital subalgebra. By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem for compact Hausdorff spaces (Mathlib: `ContinuousMap.subalgebra_topologicalClosure_eq_top_of_separatesPoints`), the topological closure of A(Φ) is all of C(X, ℝ). ∎

## 4. Formal Verification

The proof is fully formalized in Lean 4 with the Mathlib library. The key declaration:

```lean
theorem eml_stoneWeierstrass_hausdorff
    (hn : 1 ≤ n)
    (hΦ : ∀ x y : X, x ≠ y → ∃ i : Fin n, Φ i x ≠ Φ i y) :
    (emlSubalgebraHausdorff Φ).SeparatesPoints ∧
    (emlSubalgebraHausdorff Φ).topologicalClosure = ⊤
```

The proof depends only on the standard axioms (propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound), as verified by `#print axioms`. The critical Mathlib component is the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, which itself has a substantial proof chain involving lattice theory and order completeness of C(X, ℝ).

### Proof structure in Lean

1. **Continuity of generators.** The exponential generator's continuity follows by composing `Continuous.rexp` with the continuous affine combination `∑ wᵢ Φᵢ + b`. The logistic generator's continuity uses the fact that σ is continuous (proven as a rational function of exp with a positive denominator).

2. **Point separation.** Given distinct x, y and the witness j from hΦ, we construct weights w = (δᵢⱼ)ᵢ and use `Real.exp_eq_exp` (injectivity of exp on ℝ) to conclude.

3. **Density.** A single application of `ContinuousMap.subalgebra_topologicalClosure_eq_top_of_separatesPoints`.

## 5. Applications

### 5.1. Approximation on manifolds

For a compact Riemannian manifold M embedded in ℝᵈ via a smooth embedding ι, the coordinate functions Φᵢ = πᵢ ∘ ι separate points. The theorem guarantees that EML networks approximate any continuous function on M. This applies to:

- **SO(3)**: Rotation matrices, relevant to robotics and molecular dynamics
- **Grassmannians**: Subspace configurations in signal processing
- **Stiefel manifolds**: Orthonormal frame bundles in optimization

### 5.2. Function spaces on graphs

For a finite graph G with vertex set V (discrete topology, automatically compact Hausdorff), any injective function Φ : V → ℝ separates points. The EML subalgebra then equals all of C(V, ℝ) = ℝ^V, recovering finite-dimensional universality.

### 5.3. Quantum state spaces

The space of density matrices (positive semidefinite matrices of trace 1) is compact and Hausdorff. Matrix entry functions separate points and are continuous. EML networks on this space can approximate any continuous observable.

### 5.4. Tropical geometry

Compact tropical varieties carry natural continuous coordinate functions. The theorem enables EML approximation of valuations and tropical polynomials, bridging machine learning and algebraic geometry.

## 6. Discussion: What This Theorem Really Says

*Imagine you're a cartographer trying to map an unfamiliar island.* You have a collection of measuring instruments—thermometers, barometers, altimeters—each giving you a real number at every location. If these instruments can collectively distinguish any two locations (no two spots give identical readings on all instruments), then something remarkable follows: *any* continuous measurement you could ever want to make on that island can be approximated by combining exponential and logistic functions of your existing instruments.

This is the essence of our theorem. The "island" is any compact Hausdorff space—it could be the surface of a sphere, the configuration space of a robot arm, or the space of quantum states. The "instruments" are the point-separating features Φᵢ. And the "combinations" are EML networks: sums of products of exp(w·Φ + b) and σ(w·Φ + b).

**Why exponentials?** The exponential function has a magical algebraic property: exp(a) · exp(b) = exp(a + b). This means the product of two exponential generators is another exponential generator (with summed parameters). This multiplicative closure gives the EML subalgebra its algebraic richness—it's not just a vector space of functions, but a full algebra closed under multiplication.

**Why is this more general than previous results?** Classical universal approximation theorems assume your data lives in ℝⁿ. But many real-world configuration spaces are curved: the orientations of a satellite form SO(3), a 3-dimensional manifold that is emphatically not a subset of ℝ³. Our theorem says: as long as you have enough continuous features to tell points apart, EML networks are universal—regardless of the geometry of your space.

**The role of formal verification.** The proof is machine-checked in Lean 4, meaning every logical step has been verified by a computer. This eliminates the possibility of subtle errors that plague complex mathematical arguments. The proof reduces to Mathlib's formalization of the Stone–Weierstrass theorem, which itself builds on hundreds of lemmas about topology, algebra, and order theory—all machine-verified.

## 7. Related Work

The Stone–Weierstrass theorem, first proved by Stone (1937) generalizing Weierstrass's polynomial approximation theorem (1885), is one of the cornerstones of functional analysis. Its application to neural network theory was pioneered by Cybenko and by Hornik et al. in the late 1980s.

The EML framework extends classical neural networks by incorporating exponential units, connecting to the theory of exponential families in statistics and to tropical geometry. The multiplicative structure of exp (the algebraic bridge exp(a+b) = exp(a)·exp(b)) provides a natural mechanism for generating rich function algebras from simple generators.

Our formalization leverages the Mathlib Stone–Weierstrass proof, which follows the standard lattice-theoretic approach: showing that the closure of a point-separating subalgebra is a sublattice of C(X, ℝ), and then applying the lattice version of Stone–Weierstrass.

## 8. Future Directions

1. **Quantitative bounds.** The Stone–Weierstrass theorem is non-constructive; it provides density but not rates of convergence. Establishing explicit approximation rates for EML networks (analogous to Jackson's theorem for polynomials) remains an important open problem.

2. **Complex-valued extensions.** The star-algebra version of Stone–Weierstrass (also in Mathlib) could extend EML density to C(X, ℂ), relevant for quantum mechanics.

3. **Non-compact spaces.** For locally compact spaces, one can consider the one-point compactification and vanishing-at-infinity conditions.

4. **Optimization landscape.** While density is guaranteed, understanding the loss landscape of EML networks during training—particularly the role of exponential generators in creating favorable optimization geometry—is an active research direction.

# Why We Live in Three Dimensions: The Universe's Only Viable Address

*How an obscure 19th-century theorem about planetary orbits reveals that three spatial dimensions is the only number that works for a universe like ours*

---

In 1873, the French mathematician Joseph Bertrand asked a deceptively simple question: under what force laws can a planet trace out a closed orbit — returning to exactly where it started, loop after perfect loop? His answer, now known as **Bertrand's theorem**, identified precisely two possibilities: the inverse-square force that Newton described for gravity, and the linear restoring force of a spring. No others. Out of infinite possibilities, nature had chosen from an exclusive club of two.

For over a century, this result was filed away as a curiosity of classical mechanics. But recent mathematical work has uncovered something far deeper lurking inside Bertrand's theorem — a connection that reaches from the arithmetic of square roots all the way to the number of spatial dimensions our universe can have.

## The Dimension Problem

Imagine redesigning the universe. You get to choose how many spatial dimensions to include — one, two, five, eleven, whatever you like. Each choice creates a different physics, because the fundamental equations of gravity depend on dimension.

In our three-dimensional universe, gravity follows the famous inverse-square law: the gravitational force between two objects falls off as the square of the distance between them. Double the distance, and the pull weakens to a quarter. This isn't an arbitrary rule — it's a geometric consequence of living in three dimensions. Gravitational field lines spread out over the surface of a sphere, and the surface area of a sphere in three dimensions grows as the square of the radius.

In *n* spatial dimensions, the same geometric argument gives a different force law: gravity falls off as the (n-1)th power of distance. In a two-dimensional universe (a flatland), gravity would follow a 1/r law. In four dimensions, 1/r³. And so on.

This immediately raises a question that sounds almost philosophical: **in how many dimensions can you actually have a working solar system?**

## Three Strikes and You're In

The answer requires checking three properties for each possible dimension:

**First: Can planets orbit at all?** For an orbit to exist, the inward pull of gravity must balance the outward centrifugal tendency of a moving planet. This balance creates what physicists call a "stable circular orbit." Working through the mathematics, stable circular orbits exist only when the dimension is less than four. In four or more dimensions, gravity is simply too steep — any slight perturbation sends the planet spiraling inward to crash or outward to escape. Dimensions 1, 2, and 3 survive this cut.

**Second: Can orbits close?** Even with a stable circular orbit, there's no guarantee that a slightly elliptical orbit will close on itself. A planet might trace a rosette pattern, never returning to its starting point. Whether orbits close turns out to depend on a remarkable quantity: the square root of (4 minus the dimension).

For the orbit to close, this quantity must be a rational number — a fraction. In dimension 3, √(4-3) = √1 = 1. Perfectly rational. In dimension 2, √(4-2) = √2. And here, one of the oldest results in mathematics intervenes: √2 is irrational, as the ancient Greeks proved over two millennia ago. In dimension 1, √(4-1) = √3, also irrational. Among the survivors of the stability test, only dimension 3 passes the closure test.

**Third: Can you escape?** A viable universe should also allow objects to escape gravitational wells — otherwise, once captured, nothing can ever leave. This requires the gravitational potential to weaken sufficiently at large distances. Working through the integrals, finite escape velocities require three or more dimensions. Dimensions 1 and 2 fail, creating universes where every gravitational well is an inescapable prison.

Combining all three requirements: **three is the unique spatial dimension where stable orbits exist, those orbits close, and escape is possible.** It's the universe's only viable address.

## The Number Theory Connection

The most surprising aspect of this result is *where* the proof fails for other dimensions. The impossibility of two-dimensional solar systems doesn't come from some exotic physical principle — it comes from the **irrationality of √2**, a fact known since Pythagoras. The impossibility of one-dimensional solar systems comes from the irrationality of √3.

This creates a startling bridge between pure number theory and dimensional physics. The question "can planets exist in *n* dimensions?" reduces to the question "is √(4-n) rational?" — a problem in elementary algebra that a high schooler could state, even if the connection to physics requires deeper understanding.

The bridge runs even deeper when we consider Bertrand's original question about force law exponents. For a central force proportional to r^α, the analogous rationality condition involves √(3+α). Scanning through integer exponents from -2 to 2, precisely two give rational values: α = -2 (inverse-square law, giving √1 = 1) and α = 1 (linear spring, giving √4 = 2). These are exactly Bertrand's two closed-orbit force laws, now derived from pure number theory rather than differential equations.

## The Goldilocks Theorem

This collection of results has been dubbed the **Goldilocks Theorem** — by analogy with the fairy tale where only one option is "just right."

Dimension 1 is too flat: gravity is too weak at distance (infinite escape velocity required) and √3 is irrational (orbits don't close).

Dimension 2 is too thin: again infinite escape velocity, and √2 is irrational (orbits don't close).

Dimensions 4 and above are too steep: gravity is so strong at close range that no stable orbit exists at all. The slightest nudge sends everything spiraling to destruction.

Dimension 3 is just right: orbits are stable, they close into perfect ellipses (as Kepler observed and Newton explained), and rockets can escape to explore the cosmos.

## What It Means

Does the Goldilocks Theorem explain *why* we live in three dimensions? That's a question for physics and philosophy, not mathematics. The anthropic argument — that we observe three dimensions because observers can only exist in three dimensions — is strengthened by this result: the theorem shows that the conditions for a Keplerian solar system are indeed unique to three dimensions.

But the result is perhaps more interesting for what it reveals about the **interconnectedness of mathematics**. A theorem about irrational numbers, proved by a Greek philosopher drowning at sea (according to legend), turns out to control the dimensionality of viable universes. A classification theorem about planetary orbits, proved by a French geometer analyzing the mechanics of central forces, turns out to be equivalent to a question about square roots of integers.

These connections weren't engineered — they were discovered. The irrationality of √2 wasn't proved with gravitational orbits in mind, and Bertrand didn't know his theorem had implications for higher-dimensional physics. Yet the mathematical universe is woven together tightly enough that these threads inevitably connect.

Perhaps the deepest lesson is this: when mathematics constrains the possible, it constrains it *sharply*. Not gradually, not probabilistically, but with the absolute finality of a theorem. Out of infinitely many possible dimensions, the requirements for a working solar system select exactly one. Mathematics doesn't just describe our universe — in some sense, it *selects* it.

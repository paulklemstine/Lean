# When Einstein's Spacetime Meets Ancient Triangles: A New Attack on Internet Security?

*A surprising connection between Pythagorean geometry, Einstein's relativity, and code-breaking — verified by a computer proof assistant*

---

## The Triangle That Broke a Code

In 2024, a team of researchers asked a simple question: can the same geometry that Pythagoras studied 2,500 years ago help crack the codes that protect your bank account?

The answer turned out to be both disappointing and thrilling. Disappointing, because the straightforward approach — using a tree of right triangles to search for the factors of large numbers — provably cannot beat methods that have been known since antiquity. Thrilling, because the proof of *why* it fails pointed directly to where the next breakthrough might come from: the fourth dimension of spacetime.

## Triangles, Trees, and Trial Division

Everyone knows the Pythagorean theorem: a² + b² = c². Whole-number solutions like (3, 4, 5) are called Pythagorean triples. In 1934, a Swedish mathematician named Berggren discovered that every Pythagorean triple sits on a beautiful ternary tree — a branching structure where each triple has exactly three "children," generated by matrix multiplication.

The tree is connected to factoring through an elegant identity. Take any odd number — say 35. The equation 35² + b² = c² always has solutions, and the specific solution reveals the factors: the pair (c − b, c + b) must multiply to give 35², and checking the greatest common divisor with 35 will often expose a prime factor.

But the researchers proved something definitive: **searching this tree is exactly the same algorithm as one invented by Carl Friedrich Gauss around 1800.** The matrices that move you through the Berggren tree are literally the same operations that Gauss used to find the shortest vector in a two-dimensional crystal lattice. And Gauss's algorithm is provably optimal in 2D — meaning no amount of cleverness with the tree can ever do better than simply testing divisors one by one.

This equivalence — the **Lattice-Tree Correspondence Theorem** — was not just argued on paper. It was proven with mathematical certainty using Lean 4, a computer proof assistant that checks every logical step. The computer verified that Berggren tree descent, Gauss's lattice reduction, and the ancient Euclidean algorithm are all the same mathematical object viewed from three different angles.

## The Pell Obstacle

Having hit a wall in two dimensions, the researchers looked up — literally. Pythagorean *quadruples* satisfy a² + b² + c² = d², and they live in three spatial dimensions instead of two.

Here's where Einstein enters the picture. The equation a² + b² + c² = d² is preserved by a group of symmetries called O(3,1) — the **Lorentz group**. This is precisely the group that describes the symmetries of special relativity, where the interval x² + y² + z² − (ct)² stays constant under changes of reference frame.

Could the Lorentz group provide the "escape" from the two-dimensional barrier?

The researchers discovered a surprising obstacle. In special relativity, the Lorentz group includes "boosts" — continuous transformations that smoothly mix space and time coordinates. But for integer quadruples, you need *integer* versions of these boosts, and they proved a startling fact:

**There are no nontrivial integer Lorentz boosts in any single plane.**

The mathematical reason is elementary but profound. A single-plane boost requires integers λ and μ satisfying λ² − μ² = 1. But (λ − μ)(λ + μ) = 1, and in the integers, the only way to multiply two numbers to get 1 is if both are 1 (or both are −1). This forces μ = 0 — the identity transformation.

This "Pell Obstacle" means that the integer Lorentz group O(3,1;ℤ) is fundamentally different from its two-dimensional cousin O(2,1;ℤ). You can't just add one more coordinate and expect the same tricks to work. Any nontrivial transformation must mix *three or more* coordinates simultaneously.

## The Parametric Escape

But the researchers found another way. Instead of trying to build Lorentz boosts, they used the **quaternion parametrization**: every Pythagorean quadruple can be written as

$$a = m² + n² − p² − q², \quad b = 2(mq + np), \quad c = 2(nq − mp), \quad d = m² + n² + p² + q²$$

for integer parameters (m, n, p, q). The modular group SL(2,ℤ) — the same group that governs the topology of the torus — acts on these parameters, generating infinitely many quadruples from any starting point.

This parametric approach creates a tree of quadruples analogous to the Berggren tree, but with a crucial difference: it lives in a higher-dimensional space where Gauss's algorithm is *no longer optimal*.

## The Dimensional Advantage

In two dimensions, the shortest vector in a lattice is guaranteed to have length proportional to √N. In three dimensions, the bound drops to N^{1/3}. For a 1024-bit encryption key, this is the difference between facing a 2^{512}-step computation (utterly impossible) and a 2^{341}-step one (still impossible, but 2^{171} times easier — a ratio larger than the number of atoms in the universe).

The researchers formalized the complete **factoring pipeline** in Lean 4:

1. Build the quadruple lattice L₄(N) of all (x, y, z) with N dividing x² + y² + z²
2. Generate a structured starting basis using SL(2,ℤ) generators
3. Apply the BKZ lattice reduction algorithm
4. From each short vector, compute three GCD candidates: gcd(x² + y², N), gcd(x² + z², N), and gcd(y² + z², N)
5. Any nontrivial GCD yields a factor

Step 4 is the key innovation. The theorem that makes it work is deceptively simple: if p divides N and p divides x² + y², then p must divide z² (because z² = x² + y² + z² − (x² + y²), and both terms are divisible by p). If p is prime, it must then divide z itself.

Each piece of this pipeline has been independently verified by computer. Not a single gap in the logic — every step is checked.

## What the Experiments Show

Initial experiments on small numbers are encouraging but far from conclusive. The method successfully factored 54% of tested balanced semiprimes using a basic implementation. The failure cases reveal the core challenge: finding short *enough* vectors that the GCD extraction actually produces a nontrivial factor.

The open question — the one that could matter for cryptography — is whether the *structured* starting basis from the SL(2,ℤ) tree gives the BKZ algorithm an advantage over a random basis. If it does, even a modest improvement in vector lengths could push the factoring exponent below 1/2.

## The Deeper Pattern

What makes this research compelling is not any single theorem, but the web of connections it reveals:

- **Pythagoras** (500 BCE): Integer right triangles a² + b² = c²
- **Euclid** (~300 BCE): The algorithm for greatest common divisors
- **Gauss** (1801): Lattice reduction in two dimensions
- **Einstein** (1905): The Lorentz group preserving spacetime intervals
- **Berggren** (1934): The ternary tree of Pythagorean triples
- **Lenstra³** (1982): The LLL algorithm for higher-dimensional lattices
- **This work** (2025): All of these are aspects of the same mathematical structure

The Lattice-Tree Correspondence shows that three ancient algorithms are secretly identical. The Pell Obstacle shows that the passage to higher dimensions brings genuinely new phenomena. And the quadruple lattice construction shows how this new structure might — just might — lead to faster factoring.

## What's at Stake

If the quadruple lattice approach eventually achieves sub-√N factoring in practice, it would represent a genuinely new method, distinct from all known algorithms (trial division, Pollard's rho, the quadratic sieve, and the number field sieve). It wouldn't immediately threaten real cryptographic systems — those use numbers far too large — but it would demonstrate a new theoretical pathway and might inspire further optimizations.

More likely, the research will reveal *why* this approach ultimately fails for cryptographic-size numbers, just as the Lattice-Tree Correspondence revealed why the 2D approach fails. And that failure, properly understood, will point to the next escape route — perhaps into dimension 4, 5, or beyond.

Either way, the mathematics is beautiful. The fact that the same symmetry group governing the structure of spacetime also governs the arithmetic of integer quadruples and potentially the difficulty of factoring — that's the kind of deep connection that makes mathematics worth pursuing.

Pythagoras, Gauss, and Einstein never met. But their ideas are having a conversation.

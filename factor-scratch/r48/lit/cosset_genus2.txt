The elliptic curve method (ECM) is one of the best factorization methods available. It is possible to use hyperelliptic curves instead of elliptic curves but it is in theory slower. We use special hyperelliptic curves and Kummer surfaces to reduce the complexity of the algorithm. Our implementation GMP-HECM is faster than GMP-ECM for factoring big numbers. 2000 Mathematics Subject Classification Primary 11Y05; Secondary 11Y16, 11Y40 Introduction The elliptic curve method (ECM) introduced in 1985 by H. W. Lenstra, Jr. [ 6 ] plays an important role in factoring integers. ECM is used to find “medium sized” (up to 60 60 digits) prime factors of “random” numbers. It is also used by other factoring algorithms like sieving methods for cofactoring [ 5 ] . ECM is a generalization of Pollard’s p − 1 p-1 algorithm: instead of working in 𝔽 p ∗ \mathbb{F}_{p}^{*} , we work in the group of points on an elliptic curve. We generalize it by using hyperelliptic curves of genus 2 2 instead of elliptic curves of genus 1 1 . At first sight the “hyperelliptic curve method” (HECM) seems slower that ECM because of two reasons: first the arithmetic of hyperelliptic curves is slower compared to the arithmetic of elliptic curves, and secondly its probability of success is smaller than that of ECM. Indeed this probability depends on the probability of the cardinality of the Jacobian of the curve modulo a prime factor p p of n n being smooth. But the probability of a number being smooth decreases with its size. By Weil’s theorem, the cardinality of a Jacobian of a genus 2 2 curve on 𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p} is around p 2 p^{2} whereas the cardinality of an elliptic curve over the same field is around p p . This seems like a major deterrent to using HECM. To avoid it we use special hyperelliptic curves whose Jacobians are isogenous to the product of two elliptic curves: they are called decomposable. One run of HECM with this kind of curves is equivalent to two simultaneous runs of ECM, thus the probability of success of HECM with decomposable hyperelliptic curves is comparable to that of two ECM. The complexity of stage 1 1 is dominated by the cost of the arithmetic on the curves. In genus 1 1 , the quickest arithmetic is obtained by using Montgomery formulæ [ 8 ] or the new Edwards curves [ 1 ] . In genus 2 2 , the best known formulæ use Kummer surfaces [ 3 ] . The Kummer surface is a variety obtained by identifying opposite points of the Jacobian. However, not all genus 2 2 hyperelliptic curves map to a rational Kummer surface. The curves used in HECM are built as reduction modulo n n of curves defined over ℚ \mathbb{Q} which are selected for satisfying different constraints. We need a large number of possible trial curves in order to factor n n , hence the requirement that there exists an infinite family of curves over ℚ \mathbb{Q} . This requires some work; finally we obtain a parametrization of a subfamily of hyperelliptic curves: the parameters live on an elliptic curve over a function field. We have implemented our algorithm by using many functions of GMP-ECM a free ECM program. During the computation, there are many multiplications by parameters of the hyperelliptic curves so the use of small parameters (i.e. which fit into one machine word) makes these multiplications negligible before the cost of full-length modular multiplications. This makes our software faster than GMP-ECM. In Section 1 1 we give some facts about ECM, decomposable genus 2 2 hyperelliptic curves and Kummer surfaces. Details of the parametrization are given in Section 2 2 while Section 3 3 focuses on the HECM algorithm. In Section 4 4 we describe our implementation and give some numerical results. In the following, n n denotes the number to be factored. 1. background 1.1. ECM Following a common notation abuse in factoring algorithms, we work over ℤ / n ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} as if it were a field. The only operation that might fail is “field” inversion which is calculated using the Euclidean algorithm. If an inversion fails, we find a factor of n n . The ECM method starts by choosing a random elliptic curve ℰ \mathcal{E} over ℤ / n ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} and a point P P on it. Let k k be a positive integer, we compute Q = [ k ] ​ P Q=[k]P and we hope Q Q to be the zero of the curve modulo a prime p p dividing n n but not modulo n n . If this is the case, then one division fails during the computation and we find the factor p p . This happens if the cardinality | ℰ ⁡ ( 𝔽 p ) | |\mathcal{E}\left(\mathbb{F}_{p}\right)| divides k k . There are two different phases (also called stages or steps) in ECM: in phase 1 1 we hope that the cardinality is B 1 B_{1} -smooth (i.e. multiple of prime powers less than B 1 B_{1} ) for some bound B 1 B_{1} . In phase 2 2 we try to cover the “close-miss” case where the cardinality is a product of a B 1 B_{1} -smooth part by a prime cofactor. If this fails, we take another elliptic curve. ECM is a probabilistic algorithm whose complexity is O ⁡ ( L ​ ( p ) 2 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ M ​ ( log ⁡ ( n ) ) ) O(L(p)^{\sqrt{2}+o(1)}M(\log(n))) with L ⁡ ( p ) = e log ⁡ ( p ) ​ log ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( p ) ) L(p)=e^{\sqrt{\log(p)\log(\log(p))}} and M ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ) ) M(\log(n)) is the complexity of multiplications modulo n n . The complexity of ECM is dominated by the size of the smallest factor p p of n n rather than the size of the number n n to be factored. Note however that ECM does not always find the smallest factor. We obtain curves over ℚ \mathbb{Q} having the desired properties and consider their reduction modulo n n . We want to have an infinite number of curves over ℚ \mathbb{Q} so that we have enough curves over ℤ / n ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} . HECM In ECM, the big product k = ∏ π ≤ B 1 π [ log ⁡ ( B 1 ) / log ⁡ ( π ) ] k=\prod_{\pi\leq B_{1}}\pi^{\left[\log\left(B_{1}\right)/\log\left(\pi\right)\right]} is not computed as such [ 13 ] . If we work with Kummer surfaces we can’t use PRAC because changing the initial point is costly (we would have divisions). Moreover the advantage of choosing a good initial point is lost if we don’t use binary Lucas chains. Therefore in HECM we begin by computing k = ∏ π ≤ B 1 π [ log ⁡ ( B 1 ) / log ⁡ ( π ) ] k=\prod_{\pi\leq B_{1}}\pi^{\left[\log\left(B_{1}\right)/\log\left(\pi\right)\right]} . We hope to encounter the zero of one of the underlying elliptic curves so it is important to have explicit morphisms between the Kummer surface and the two underlying elliptic curves. In fact, it is impossible to obtain real points on the curves since we can’t distinguish between a point and its opposite and we don’t even know if we are on the curves or on their twists. However their building blocks are defined over an extension field of ℚ \mathbb{Q} : they use square roots that disappear in the global morphisms. Since we are not allowed to use square roots we must rewrite the global morphisms. As a result their expressions become complicated however, they are computed only once for every run of HECM, thus they are negligible before the cost of computing [ k ] ​ P [k]P . Note that we only need the ( x : : z ) (x::z) coordinates which are invariant under the isomorphism from the curve (in the Weierstrass form) to its twist.

ASYMPTOTICS FOR SMOOTH NUMBERS IN SHORT INTERVALS

arXiv:2409.05761v1 [math.NT] 9 Sep 2024

KHALID YOUNIS
Abstract. A number is said to be y-smooth if all of its prime factors are less
than or equal to y. For all 17/30 < θ ≤ 1, we show that the density of y-smooth
numbers in the short interval [x, x + xθ ] is asymptotically equal to the density of
y-smooth numbers in the long interval [1, x], for all y ≥ exp((log x)2/3+ε ). Assuming
the Riemann Hypothesis, we also prove that for all 1/2 < θ ≤ 1 there exists a large
constant K such that the expected asymptotic result holds for y ≥ (log x)K .
Our approach is to count smooth numbers using a Perron integral, shift this to a
particular contour left of the saddle point, and employ a zero-density estimate of the
Riemann zeta function.

1. Introduction
A positive integer is y-smooth (or y-friable) if all of its prime factors are less than or
equal to y. Of central importance to understanding the distribution of smooth numbers
is to ﬁrst count (at least approximately) how many there are in a large interval [1, x].
This question has received signiﬁcant attention over the last century, and has proﬁted
from both combinatorial and analytic approaches (see the surveys [HT93] and [Gra08]).
To further understand their distribution, it is natural to ask how many smooth
numbers there are in an interval (x, x + h], where h is large but much smaller than
x. If the smooth numbers were distributed not too erratically, then, for h and y in a
suitable range depending on x, one might expect the density of smooth numbers in
short intervals and in long intervals to be the same. That is, as x → ∞,

However, for y very close to x, our version of the saddle point method appears to
break down entirely. We therefore adopt a diﬀerent approach having more in common
with that of Hildebrand [Hil86]. This transforms the problem from counting smooth
numbers in short intervals into one of counting primes in short intervals, by observing
that at this level of smoothness we need only exclude numbers with a few large prime
factors. We require an estimate for the count of primes in short intervals with quickly
decaying error terms. In order to attain this, we ﬁrst weight the primes, use the
explicit formula, and then use the zero-density estimate of Guth and Maynard. This
once again leads to the 17/30 bound for θ.
1.4. Saddle point method and related work. A typical method of obtaining short
interval results for primes is via the explicit formula. Unfortunately, the smooth numbers appear not to enjoy such a formula. An alternative approach is via the Hooley–
Huxley contour, appearing in work of Ramachandra [Ram76], and involves dissecting
and shifting the Perron integral to the left of the 1-line in a way that stops short of
hitting zeros of ζ(s). One then employs the zero-density result to the savings corresponding to the location of the contour. A delicate choice of contour passing through
zero-free regions, paired with zero-density estimates, is a key component of our proof.
The majority of this paper follows the framework of the saddle point method, which
ﬁrst appeared in the landmark work of Hildebrand and Tenenbaum [HT86], achieving
an asymptotic formula for the count of smooth numbers. Additionally, the method
was able to deliver the short interval result quoted earlier, which we discuss here.
After expressing the count as in (1.9), a saving in xs ζ(s, y) over the trivial bound is
fundamental for s = α + it and 1/ log y ≤ |t| ≤ H. By exploiting the Euler product

sieve (though our paper does not use the latter of these tools). Many of these ideas
transfer to the short interval setting.
1.5. Organisation. The plan for the remainder of the article is as follows. For small
ε > 0, we let θ ≥ 17/30 + 10ε, and set a suitably large C ≍ 1/ε. The objective of
Section 2 is to demonstrate a power saving for xs ζ(s, y) when shifting left into a local
zero-free region. In Section 3 we move our Perron integral onto a contour Γ, left of the
vertical line at ℜ(s) = α, and prove that suﬃcient savings occur away from the real
axis when y ≤ xε (where the constant introduced in Theorem 1.2 has size c ≍ ε). We
employ a smooth weight function in Section 4 to improve this to y ≤ x1−2ε (now with
c ≍ ε2 ). This gives suﬃcient error terms when y ≤ x1/2−ε , or when xθ ≤ h ≤ x0.99 , say.
In Section 5, which may essentially be read independently of earlier sections, we use a
direct argument counting primes in short intervals to cover the range x1−2ε ≤ y ≤ 2x
(such that c ≍ ε), as well as when x1/2−ε ≤ y ≤ x1−2ε and x0.99 ≤ h ≤ x. In Section 6,
we combine the results on diﬀerent ranges to complete the proof of Theorem 1.2. The
proof of Theorem 1.1 quickly follows by proving the main term fθ (x, y) approximates
Ψ(x, y)/x.
Finally, Section 7 contains the proof of Theorem 1.3, in which we revisit and simplify
our method under the assumption of the Riemann Hypothesis (with K ≍ 1/ε2 , and in

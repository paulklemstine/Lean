3     Basics of Quantum Computing
3.1     Qubit.
The notion of quantum bit (qubit) is the basis of quantum computations. Qubit is the quantum version
of the classical binary bit physically realized with a two-state device. There are two possible outcomes
for the measurement of a qubit usually taken to have the value ”0” and ”1”, like a bit or binary digit.
However, whereas the state of a bit can only be either 0 or 1, the general state of a qubit according
to quantum mechanics can be a coherent superposition of both. This allows us to compute 0 and 1
simultaneously. Such a phenomenon is known as quantum parallelism.

3.2     States of Quantum Register
A quantum register is an isolated quantum mechanical system composed of several n qubits (quantum
n-qubits register), where n ≥ 1. In the case of a quantum n-qubits register, we have a superposition
of 2n states B = {(0 . . . 0), . . . , (1 . . . 1)}. This allows us to compute the set of B states simultaneously.
This phenomenon of quantum parallelism is a potential advantage of quantum computational models.
    Formally, a quantum state |ψi of a quantum n-qubits register is described as follows. Let σ =
σ1 . . . σn be a binary sequence. Then, we denote tensor product |σ1 i ⊗ |σ2 i ⊗ · · · ⊗ |σn i by |σi. Let
B = {|00 . . . 0i, |00 . . . 1i, . . . , |bin(i − 1)i, . . . , |11 . . . 1i} be a set of orthonormal vectors, where bin(i)

3.3    Transformations of a Quantum States.
Quantum mechanic postulates that transformations of quantum states |ψi ∈ (H2 )⊗n (of quantum
n-qubits register) are mathematically determined by unitary operators:

                                               |ψ 0 i = U |ψi,                                                (5)

where U is a 2n × 2n unitary matrix for transforming a vector that represents a state of a quantum
n-qubits register.
   A unitary matrix U can be written in the exponential form:

                                                 U = eiW ,

   where W is a Hermitian matrix. Learn more from [Ere17].

3.5    Quantum Circuits
Circuits are one of the ways of visually representing a register’s state’s transformations sequence.
    A classical Boolean circuit is a finite directed acyclic graph with AND, OR, and NOT gates. It
has n input nodes, which contain the n input bits (a state of n-register). Internal nodes are AND,
OR, and NOT gates, and there are one or more designated output nodes. The initial input bits are
fed into AND, OR, and NOT gates according to the circuit, and eventually the output nodes assume
some value. Circuit computes a Boolean function f : {0, 1}n → {0, 1}m if the output nodes get the
value f (σ) for every input σ ∈ {0, 1}n .
    A quantum circuit (also called a quantum network or quantum gate array) acts on a quantum
register. It is a representation of transformations of a quantum state (a state of a quantum register).
Quantum circuit generalizes the idea of classical circuit families, replacing the AN D, OR, and N OT
gates by elementary operators (quantum gates). A quantum gate is a unitary operator on a small
(usually 1, 2, or 3) number of qubits. Mathematically, if gates are applied in a parallel way to different
parts of the quantum register, then they can be composed using a tensor product. If gates are applied
sequentially, then they can be composed using the ordinary product.

3.6    Information Extracting (Measurement).
There is only one way to extract information from a state of a quantum n-register to a “macro world”.
It is the measurement of the state of the quantum register. Measurement can be classified as the
second kind of quantum operators, while unitary transformations of a quantum system as the first
kind of quantum operators.
    Different measurements are considered in quantum computation theory. In our review, we use
only measurement with respect to “computational P basis”. Such a type of measurement is described
as follows. If we measure the quantum state i αi |ii, then we get one of the basis state |ii with
probability |αi |2 .
    We also can use a partial measurement of the state of the quantum register. Consider the case of
a quantum 2-register. Let |ψi be a state of such a quantum 2-register:
                             |ψi = α00 |00i + α01 |01i + α10 |10i + α11 |11i.
Imagine that we measure the first qubit of the quantum 2-register. The probability of obtaining |0i is
                           P r(|0i) = P r(|00i) + P r(|01i) = |α00 |2 + |α01 |2
The state of the second qubit after the measurement is

4.1    Decision Trees
We consider a deterministic version of the Decision Tree (DT) model of computation. We present here
a specific version of DTs that is oriented for quantum generalization.
   The model can be presented in two forms.

Graph representation. DT A is a directed leveled binary tree with a selected starting node (root
node). All nodes V of A are partitioned into levels V1 , . . . , V` . The level V1 contains the starting node.
Nodes from the level Vi are connected only to nodes from the level Vi+1 . The computation of a DT
starts from the start node. At each node of the graph, a Boolean variable x ∈ X is tested. Depending
on the outcome of the query, the algorithm proceeds to the x = 0 child or the x = 1 child of the node.
Leaf nodes are marked by “0” or “1”. When on input σ = σ1 , . . . , σN the computation reaches a leaf
of the tree, then it outputs the value listed at this leaf.

Linear representation. We define a linear presentation of the graph-based model of DT as follows.
   Let dim(A) = max |Vi | and d = dim(A). Denote S = {s1 , . . . , sd } a set of d-dimensional column-
                    1≤i≤`
vectors, where s(i) is a vector with all components “0” except one “1” in the i-th position. We call a
vector s ∈ S a state of A. A state s in each level i, 1 ≤ i ≤ ` represents a node (by “1” component)
where A can be found at that level.
    Next, let Xi ⊆ X be a set of variables tested on the level i. A transformation of a state s on a
level i, 1 ≤ i ≤ ` − 1, is described by a set Qi (xi ) of matrices which depends on values of variables xi
tested on that level. The state s(0) ∈ S is an initial state.
    Now DT A can be formalized as
                                   A = hS, Q1 (x1 ), . . . , Q`−1 (x`−1 ), s(0) i.
Computation on an input σ by A is presented as a sequence
                                           s(0) → s(1) → · · · → s(`)
of states transformations determined by A structure and input σ as follows. Let s(i) be the current
state on the level i, then the next state will be s(i+1) = Qi (xi , σ)s(i) , where matrix Qi (xi , σ) ∈ Qi (xi )
is determined by σ for xi variables.

Complexity Measures. Two main complexity measures are used for computational models. These
measures are Time and Space (Memory). For the Decision models of computation, the analogs of these
complexity measures are query complexity and size complexity. The query complexity is the maximum
number of queries that the algorithm can do during computation. This number is the depth D(A) that
is the length of the longest path from the root to a leaf of the decision tree A. The size complexity is
the width of A. We denote the width dim(A). In addition, we use a number of bits, which are enough
to encode a state on a level of A that is size(A) = dlog2 dim(A)e.
    For function f , complexity measures D(f ), dim(f ), and size(f ) denote (as usual) the minimum
Time and Space needed for computing f .

4.2    Quantum Query Algorithm
Quantum Query Algorithm (QQA) is the following generalization of the DT model for a quantum
case. A QQA A for computing a Boolean function f (X) is based on a quantum size(A)-register
(on a quantum system composed from size(A) qubits). |ψstart i is an initial state. The computation
procedure is determined by the sequence
                                              U0 , Q, U1 , . . . , Q, U` ,
of operators that are dim(A) × dim(A) unitary matrices.
    Algorithm A is composed of two types of operators. Operators Ui are independent of a tested input
X. Q is the query operator of a fixed form that depends on the tested input X. The algorithm consists
of performing U0 , Q, U1 , . . . , Q, U` on |ψstart i and measuring the result.
    The algorithm computes f (X) if during the computation on an input instance σ the initial state
|ψstart i is transformed to a final quantum state |ψi that allows us to extract a value f (σ) when this
state is measured.

5     Grover’s Search Algorithm and Amplitude Amplification
In this section, we present an algorithm in the Query model for the Search problem. Let us present
the Search problem.
    Search Problem Given a set of numbers (x1 , . . . , xn ) ∈ {0, 1}n one want to find the k ∈ [n] such
that xk = 1.
    Let ON ES = {i : xi = 1}. Then, we can consider three types of the problem
    • single-solution search problem if |ON ES| = 1 and it is known apriori;
    • t-solutions search problem if |ON ES| = t and it is known apriori;
    • unknown-number-of-solutions search problem if |ON ES| = t and t is unknown.
    Let us consider the solution of the first of these three problems.

5.1    Single-solution Search Problem
So, it is known that there is one and only one k ∈ [n] such that xk = 1 and xi = 0 for any i ∈ [n]\{k}.

Deterministic solution. We can check all elements i ∈ [n], i.e. from 0 to n − 1. If we found i such
that xi = 1, then it is the solution. The idea is presented in Algorithm 1.

Algorithm 1 Deterministic solution of Single-solution Search Problem
 k ← N U LL
 for i ∈ {0, . . . , n − 1} do
    if xi = 1 then
        k←i
    end if
 end for
 return k

    The query complexity of the such solution is O(n).

     Let mask ∈ {0, . . . , 2n − 1} be a bitmask of a subset of vertexes, and let V (mask) be the corre-

mask, then vi ∈ V (mask) iff maski = 1.
     Let us consider a function h : {0, . . . , 2n − 1} × {1, . . . , n} × {1, . . . , n} → {0, 1}. h(mask, v, u) = 1
iff there is a Hamiltonian path in the subset V (mask) that starts from v and finishes in u, where
v, u ∈ V (mask).
     There are several properties of the function h.
   • The single vertex property. h(mask, v, v) = 1 for any v ∈ V and mask such that V (mask) = {v}.
   • The cutting one edge property. h(mask, u, v) = 1 iff there is t ∈ V (mask) such that h(mask 0 , u, t) =
     1, V (mask 0 ) = V (mask)\{v}, and there is an edge between t and v.
   • The splitting of a subset to two parts property. h(mask, u, v) = 1 iff there are
         – mask 0 , mask 00 ∈ {0, . . . , 2n −1} such that V (mask 0 )∪V (mask 00 ) = V (mask) and V (mask 0 )∩
           V (mask 00 ) = ∅;
         – t ∈ V (mask 0 ), z ∈ V (mask 00 ) such that h(mask 0 , u, t) = 1, h(mask 00 , z, v) = 1, and there is
           an edge between t and z.
    Let us describe the classical algorithm. Our goal is computing h(F ullSet, v, u) for each v, u ∈ V
and a bit mask F ullSet = 2n − 1 such that V (F ullSet) = V . If we can find a pair of vertexes (v, u)
such that h(F ullSet, v, u) = 1, then it is a solution. In that case, we can solve the decision problem
which is checking the existence of a Hamiltonian path. Let us focus on this problem and then we solve
the original one.
    We find h(mask, v, u) recursively using “The cutting one edge property”. For this reason we check
all neighbors t of u and search h(mask 0 , v, t) = 1 where V (mask 0 ) = V (mask)\{u}. At the same
time, we can store values of the function h in an array and avoid recomputing the same value several
times. In fact, this “caching” trick converts the “brute force” solution to the “dynamic programming”
solution.

7.7     Traveling Salesman Problem
Let us consider the following well-known problem.
    • We have a weighted graph G = (V, E) with n vertexes and m edges. Let w(u, v) be a weight of
      the edge between vertexes u and v. If there is no edge between u and v, then w(u, v) = +∞.

    • We should find a Hamiltonian path with minimal weight. The weight of a path is the sum of
      weights of edges that belong to the path.
Like the Hamiltonian Path Problem, the Traveling Salesman Problem(TSP) is NP-complete.
    We can use a similar solution as for Hamiltonian Path Problem. We consider a function h :
{0, . . . , 2n − 1} × V × V → R+0 , where R+0 is the set of non-negative real numbers. The value
h(mask, v, u) is the minimal weight of a Hamiltonian path in the subset V (mask) that starts from v
and finishes in u, where v, u ∈ V (mask). We assume that h(mask, v, u) = +∞ if such a Hamiltonian
path does not exist. The function has the following properties:

    • The single vertex property. h(mask, v, v) = 0 for any v ∈ V and mask such that V (mask) = {v}.
    • The cutting one edge property. If h(mask, u, v) 6= +∞, then there is t ∈ V (mask) such that
      h(mask, u, v) = h(mask 0 , u, t) + w(t, v).

    • The splitting of a subset to two parts property. if h(mask, u, v) 6= +∞, then there are
         – mask 0 , mask 00 ∈ {0, . . . , 2n −1} such that V (mask 0 )∪V (mask 00 ) = V (mask) and V (mask 0 )∩
           V (mask 00 ) = ∅;
         – t ∈ V (mask 0 ), z ∈ V (mask 00 ) such that h(mask, u, v) = h(mask 0 , u, t)+w(t, z)+h(mask 00 , z, v).

8.1.2    Meet-in-the-Middle Solution
Let us discuss the classical version of the Meet-in-the-Middle Solution. We split the sequence a to two

     As the second step, we check all subsets of the second part. We consider a search function f :

can construct a subset ai1 , . . . , aim0 , aj1 , . . . , ajm00 with sum k using elements from the whole sequence.

arguments mask ∈ {0, . . . 2n−t − 1}.

first part. The complexity of adding an element to the set (Self-Balanced Binary Search Tree) is

different subsets for the second part. The complexity of computing one of them is O(n−t) for computing
a sum s and O(t) for searching k − s in the set Sums. So, the complexity is O(n − t + t) = O(n). The
complexity of the second step is O(2n−t n), and the total complexity is O(2t · t + 2n−t · n) = O(2n/2 n)
if t = n/2.

is O(2n /2)! That is exponentially bigger than in the brute force case. At the same time, we have
achieved good running time as for the quantum version of the brute force algorithm.

9.3    Dyck language
Let us consider a string that contains only parenthesis “(” or “)”. We want to check that the parentheses
are balanced. The problem is well-known and important. In theoretical computer science, it is known
as Dyck language recognition. Example:

   • the string “((()()))” belongs to Dyck language,
   • the string (())) does not belong to Dyck language.
   Let us consider the following problem Dyckk,n :

   • Input is a boolean string of a length at most n. 0 means opening parenthesis, 1 means closing
     parenthesis.
   • A depth of parentheses is at most k.

   The answer is 1 if an input string is a well-balanced parentheses sequence and the depth is at most
k. Otherwise, the result is 0.
   Example:

   • ((()())) belongs to Dyck3,8 or Dyck4,8 ,
   • ((()())) does not belong to Dyck2,8 or Dyck1,8 .
   Let an input string be x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) and x[l, r] = (xl , . . . , xr ) be a substring of x. Let x[1, r]
be a prefix of the string x. Let h(x, r) = #0 (x[1, r]) − #1 (x[1, r]) be a balance of opening and closing
parenthesizes in the prefix x[1, r], where #0 (x[1, r]) and #1 (x[1, r]) are numbers of 0 and 1 symbols,
respectively. We assume that h(x, 0) = 0. Let g(x[l, r]) = h(x, r) − h(x, l − 1) be a balance of opening
and closing parenthesizes in the substring x[l, r].
   If we check the following three properties, then we can be sure, that an input string is 1-instance.
   • the balance for x is 0, i.e. g(x) = 0;

   • there is no negative-balance prefix, i.e. g(x[1, r]) ≥ 0 for r ∈ {1, . . . , n};
   • there is no (k + 1)-balance prefix, i.e. g(x[1, r]) ≤ k for r ∈ {1, . . . , n}.
    For checking these three properties classically, we can use a counter for computing a balance on a
prefix and check all three conditions. The running time of the algorithm is O(n). At the same time,
the lower bound is also Ω(n).
    Let us consider the quantum algorithm. We want to reformulate the problem as a search problem.
Let us consider the new input u = 1k x0k , where 1k is the string of k times 1s (closing parenthesizes)
and 0k is the string of k times 0s (opening parenthesizes).
    Let a substring x[l, r] be +t-substring if g(u[l, r]) = t and it is minimal, i.e. there is no (l0 , r0 )
such that l ≤ l0 ≤ r0 ≤ r, (l, r) 6= (l0 , r0 ) and g(x[l0 , r0 ]) = t. Let a substring x[l, r] be −t-substring if
g(x[l, r]) = −t and it is minimal, i.e. there is no (l0 , r0 ) such that l ≤ l0 ≤ r0 ≤ r, (l, r) 6= (l0 , r0 ) and
g(x[l0 , r0 ]) = −t. Let a substring x[l, r] be ±t-substring if it is +t-substring or −t-substring. Let a sign
of a substring x[l, r] be sign(x[l, r]) = 1 if x[l, r] is a +t-substring; and let sign(x[l, r]) = −1 if x[l, r]
is a −t-substring.
    The computing of Dyckk,n (x) is equivalent to searching any ±(k + 1)-substring in u = 1k x0k . Let
m = |u| = 2k + n be the length of u.
   • If g(x) > 0, then there is l such that u[l, m] is a +(k + 1)-substring.
   • If g(x[1, r]) < 0 for some r ∈ {1, . . . , n}, then u[1, k + r] is a −(k + 1)-substring;
   • If g(x[1, r]) = k + 1 for some r ∈ {1, . . . , n}, then u[k + 1, k + r] is a +(k + 1)-substring;.

If we found a ±(k + 1)-substring, then Dyckk,n (x) = 0, and Dyckk,n (x) = 1 otherwise.

Let us consider the streaming computational model that was discussed in Section 4.3. The main goal
is a minimization of memory.
    Let us consider the Strings Equality problem for data stream processing algorithms.

   • The input is u2v, where u ∈ {0, 1}n , v ∈ {0, 1}m are two binary strings.
   • We should output 1 if u = v, and 0 otherwise.

   • Example:
        0101000100012010100010001 are equal
        0101000100012010101010001 are inequal

Let us discuss a deterministic solution. We store u in the memory. Then, we read vi and check equality
of ui = vi for each i ∈ {1, . . . , n}. If m 6= n, then we return 0. The memory complexity of the algorithm
is O(n).
    Let us discuss a randomized solution. The idea is to guess an unequal position and check whether it
is the correct guess. We randomly choose j ∈ {1, . . . , n}. Then, we skip all indexes except j and keep
vj in memory. After that, we skip n variables and compare them with uj . If vj = uj , then we return
1 and 0 otherwise. Here we assume, that n and m are known in advance, and we already checked
n = m equality. If it is a wrong assumption, then we can assume that n is maximal possible on the
random choice step, then we update j according to the actual value of n. The memory complexity of
the algorithm is O(log n). Let us compute the success probability. In the worst case, strings differ in



                                                    47
only one bit. Probability of picking this bit is psucess = n1 . So, the size of memory is good, but the
success probability is bad.
   Let us discuss alternative approaches. How do we compare strings in programming languages
(deterministically)? We compare hash values first. If hash values are equal, then we compare strings
symbol by symbol. Here by a hash value, we mean a result of a hash function. In fact, as a hash
function, we can choose any function h : X → Y such that
   • |X| > |Y |; and
   • if h(x) 6= h(x0 ), then x 6= x0 .
    Let us discuss an example of a hash function. Let us consider a binary string x, e.g. x = 10110101.
Let value(x) be a number which binary representation is reversed x, e.g.
    value(x) = 1 · 20 + 0 · 21 + 1 · 22 + 1 · 23 + 0 · 24 + 1 · 25 + 0 · 26 + 1 · 27 = 173
    As a hash function we choose hp : {0, 1}∗ → {0, . . . , p − 1}, such that hp (x) = value(x) mod p, for
some prime p, e.g. for p = 7, we have h7 (10110101) = 2.
    Let us consider only hp as a hash function. The algorithm for our problem is the following. We
compute a hash value for the first string hp (u) and for the second one hp (v). If hp (u) = hp (v), then
we say that strings are equal. If hp (u) 6= hp (v), then u 6= v.
    For this algorithm, we store the hash values and the index of the current symbol. So, memory
complexity is O(log p + log n). The solution is deterministic and has good memory complexity. Does
it work correctly? Sometimes...
    If u = v, then hp (u) = hp (v) always. At the same time, if u 6= v, then we cannot claim that
h(u) 6= h(v). On the other hand, if h(u) 6= h(v), then we can be sure that u 6= v. At the same
time, if h(u) = h(v), then we cannot be sure that u = v. We can have collisions. It means we can
have two strings u 6= v such that hp (u) = hp (v). For example, h7 (10110101) = 173 mod 7 = 2, and
h7 (00000010) = 2 mod 7 = 2. Hash values are equal, but strings are different.

each string. If hp (u) = hp (v) and hq (u) = hq (v), then we claim that u = v. The memory complexity
is O(log n + log p + log q). It is much better but does not work always correctly anyway. The new
idea can have a collision also. Maybe, we should choose more primes? Three, four, five...? How many

11     Quantum Walks. Coined Quantum Walks
11.1     Discussion. Random Walk, One-dimensional Walk.
Firstly, let us discuss the classical base algorithm which is the Random walk. The general algorithm is
the following. Assume we have a graph G = (V, E) where n = |V | is the number of vertices, and m is
the number of edges. Let us have an agent, that can be in one of the vertices. Assume that the agent
in a vertex i, then it chooses one of the neighbor vertices randomly. Let us define the probability of
moving from a vertex i to a vertex j by M [i, j]. If there are no edges between i and j, then M [i, j] = 0.
So, M is a transition matrix for our random walk. We can say that p = (p1 , . . . , pn ) is a probability
destruction for vertexes. We can compute p on t-th step by p0 from (t − 1)-th step as p = M p0 .
We assume that the agent cannot just “jump” from a vertex to another vertex, but can only move
to neighbor vertices. We call this property “locality”. We can use the random walk as a traversal
algorithm (like Depth-first search or Breadth-first search, Section 7.1). As an example, it can be a
search algorithm that searches a vertex with a specific property. There are other applications of the
algorithm.
    Why the algorithm is interesting? One reasonable advantage is the small size of memory. Because
of the locality property, in any step, we should store only the current vertex in memory that requires
O(log n) bits of memory. At the same time, if we implement one of standard traversal algorithms
(Depth-first search or Breadth-first search), then you should store O(n) vertices in a stack or a queue
data structures for DFS or BFS respectively. That requires O(n log n) bits of memory. So, we have
an exponentially smaller size of memory for a random walk. We can say [Ben00] that we use a
more restricted computational model that is close to Branching programs (Section 4.3) and 2-way
probabilistic automata in which an input head can be in different positions of an input tape with
some probabilities. Here the agent is an analog of the input head, moving to neighbor vertices is an
analog of moving to neighbor cells of the input tape for automata and the algorithm can be in different
vertices with some probabilities. The difference is in the non-linearity of input data. Automata like
computational models assume that input has a linear form (like a list or string on a tape), but random
walk moves by a more general data structure that is a graph. You can read more about automata in
[Kap19, SY14] and about branching programs in [Weg00] and in Section 4.3.
    There are many non-trivial applications of the algorithm that allow us to obtain an advantage in
time or memory complexity, for example, [FRPU94, KSZ22, KE22].
    As an example, we explore a simple version of a graph with a regular structure. Let us consider a
number line with vertexes in integer points. Neighbor points are connected. We have an agent that

11.1.1    One-dimensional Quantum Walk
Let us construct Quantum walk as a direct “translation” of Random walk to the quantum world. One
quantum state is for one point. We start from the |0i point. Like in the Random walk algorithm, in
the quantum version we want to keep the “locality” property. Therefore, the transformation U such
that |ii → a|i − 1i + b|ii + c|i + 1i for each i.
   The only ways [Mey96] to have a unitary transition are
   • |a| = 1, b = 0, c = 0

   • a = 0, |b| = 1, c = 0
   • a = 0, b = 0, |c| = 1
All of them are trivial. That is why we cannot directly “translate” the algorithm to the quantum
world and should change something in the idea.
    Let us consider each edge of the graph as a directed edge. If an edge (i, j) is not directed, then we
consider it as a pair of two directed edges i → j and i ← j. Let us use the following notation for an
edge in the case of a number line. An edge can be resented as a pair (vertex, direction), where vertex
is an integer point, and direction ∈ {0, 1} is a direction of the edge, 0 for the left, and 1 for the right
directions.
    We associate a quantum state with a directed edge. They are |i, 0i and |i, 1i for an integer point
i. Let us start from the |0, 0i edge. One step consists of two transformations:


                                                       54
   • “Coin flip”, C such that
       |i, 0i → a|i, 0i + b|i, 1i,
       |i, 1i → c|i, 0i + d|i, 1i.
       The transformation means redistribution of amplitudes (analog of probability redistribution for
       Random walks).

   • “Shift”, S such that
       |i, 0i → |i − 1, 0i,
       |i, 1i → |i + 1, 1i.
       The transformation means moving one step.

   Due to the “locality” property, both transformations affect only close states (states of the same
vertex or neighbor vertices). After some number of steps, we measure the quantum state of the system.

12.1     Relation of Markov Chains and Random and Quantum Walks
This kind of quantum walk is based on the properties of Markov Chains. We can consider the Random
walk algorithm as a Markov chain [Kon09]. States of the Markov chain are vertices of a graph. We
assume that number of states is n which is also the number of vertices of the graph. The probability of
moving from a state i to j is the probability of moving from a vertex i to j. So we use p = (p1 , . . . , pn )T
as a probability distribution vector for n states of the Markov chain. An n × n-matrix M is a transition
matrix such that M [i, j] is a probability of moving from a state i to a state j. Let M be symmetric.
Then we can define δ which is a spectral gap (eigenvalue gap). The value is δ = 1 − |λ2 |, where λ2 is
the eigenvalue of M with the second largest magnitude.
    Let us consider a search problem with a search function f {1, . . . , n} → {0, 1}. We want to find any
x such that f (x) = 1. Assume that there are t arguments with 1-value. Formally, t = |{x : f (x) =
1, 1 ≤ x ≤ n}|.
    We can suggest three approaches to the problem. Let ε = nt that is the probability of finding a
required x on a random sampling of an argument from {1, . . . , n}.

Solution 1. Random Sampling.             We use boosting of success probability technique for the random
sampling algorithm.

   • We do the following steps O( 1ε ) times
         – We uniformly choose x ∈R {1, . . . , n}.
         – Then, check whether f (x) = 1. If it is true, then we stop the process. If it is false, then
           continue.

Solution 2. Markov Chain.           We use the Markov chain as the main search algorithm.
   • We uniformly choose x ∈R {1, . . . , n}.

   • We do the following steps O( 1ε ) times:
         – We check whether the walk reaches the state x : f (x) = 1. If it is true, then we stop the
           process. If it is false, then continue.
         – We do O( 1δ ) steps of the random process (random walk, Markov chain) with transition
           matrix M .

Solution 2. Markov Chain. Greedy idea.                We use a greedy version of the algorithm based on
the Markov chain.
   • We uniformly choose x ∈R {1, . . . , n}.
   • We do the following steps O( 1ε ) times:

           process. If it is false, then continue.
         – We do O( 1δ ) steps of the random process (random walk, Markov chain) M . On each step,
           we check whether the walk reaches the state x : f (x) = 1. If it is true, then we stop the
           process. If it is false, then continue.

12.2    Quantum Algorithm for Element Distinctness Problem
Let us define the Element Distinctness Problem
   • Let us have n integer variables {x1 , . . . , xn }.
   • We want to find i 6= j : xi = xj or say that there is no such a pair of indexes (all elements are
     distinct).



                                                       60
    The problem is connected with the Collision Problem (Section 8.2). The difference is the following.
The statement of the Collision Problem claims that all elements are distinct (1− to− 1 function), or
we have n/2 pairs of equal elements (2− to− 1 function). The statement of the Element distinctness
problem claims that all elements are distinct, or we have at least one pair of equal elements. In the
worst case, we have only one pair of equal elements, this makes the problem much harder.
    It is known [AS04] that if we have an algorithm for the Element Distinctness problem with query
complexity
   √          Q, then we can construct an algorithm for the         √ Collision problem with query complexity
O( Q). The idea is following. Let us take randomly O( n) elements. Due to [Len05], if the input
is 2− to− 1 function, then with probability at least 0.9 we can claim that there is at least one pair of
equal elements among the taken         √ elements. Then we can invoke an algorithm for Element Distinctness
on the smaller input of size O( n) and find the required pair. At the same time, there is no opposite
connection. Therefore, we cannot use an algorithm from Section 8.2, and we should develop a new
algorithm for Element Distinctness Problem.
    Firstly, let us discuss the obvious application of Grover’s search algorithm. Let us define the search
function f : {1, . . . , n} × {1, . . . , n} → {0, 1} such that f (i, j) = 1 iff xi 6= xj . The search space size is
O(n2 ). We can use Grover’s search algorithm and obtain a quantum algorithm with O(n) complexity.
At the same time, classical query complexity is Θ(n) [GKMadHS96] and we do not obtain any speed-
up in the quantum case. More advanced usage of Grover’s algorithm and Amplitude Amplification is
[BDH+ 01] that obtains O(n3/4 ) query complexity.
    Secondly, we present the algorithm based on the quantum walks with O(n2/3 ) query complexity
(See [Amb07b]) that satisfy the quantum lower bound Ω(n2/3 ) [AS04].
    First of all, let us present the graph that defines transitions of a Markov chain, or we can say that
we invoke a random walk for this graph. Let us fix some parameter r that is 1 ≤ r ≤ n.
   • a vertex v corresponds to a subset Sv ⊂ {1, . . . , n} of size r, i.e. |Sv | = r.
   • two vertices v and w are connected by an edge iff there are two indexes i and j such that the
     difference between Sv and Sw only in i and j. Formally,
         – i ∈ Sv , and j ∈ Sw ;
         – i 6∈ Sw , and j 6∈ Sv ;
         – Sv \{i} = Sw \{j}.
   • We assume that the edge from v to w is labeled by a directed pair (i, j); and the edge from w to

13.2       Quantum Backtracking
There is a class of problems that can be solved using a brute force algorithm of the following form.
We construct all possible sequences a = (a1 , . . . , an ), for ai ∈ {1, . . . , d} for some integer parameter d.
Additionally, we have a function f 0 : {1, . . . , d}n → {0, 1} that checks whether an argument a solution
or not. Our goal is to find any or all arguments a such that f 0 (a) = 1. Let us assume that the problem
is searching for any target argument.
     The complexity of checking all solutions is O(dn ·Q(f 0 )), where Q(f 0 ) is the complexity of computing
f . For simplicity, we assume that Q(f 0 ) = O(n), at the same time it is not always true. If the
  0

complexity Q(f 0 ) is different, then we should change this multiple in all statements of this section. So,
the complexity is O(dn · n). As an example, we can consider the Hamiltonian path problem (Section
7.6). In this problem, we generate sequences of vertices. The function f checks that all vertices are
presented exactly once and the sequence is a path.
     We can associate this solution with searching on d-nary tree of depth n. We call it a tree of variants.
Each vertex of the tree has d outgoing edges for children. Each edge is labeled by the number from 1
to d. A leaf vertex of the tree on level n with labels on edges of the path from root a = (a1 , . . . , an )
is corresponding to the sequence a, and it is marked if f (a) = 1. The goal is to search for any marked
vertex in the tree. We can say that a node on level i is checking all cases of assignment to a variable
ai .
     The backtracking algorithm [CLRS01] suggests us a modification of this approach. We consider
partially sequences a = (a1 , . . . , ai , ∗, . . . , ∗) ∈ {1, . . . , d, ∗}n . Here ∗ means undefined value. In that
case we define a new function f : {1, . . . , d, ∗}n → {0, 1, 2} such that

    • f (a) = 0 if a is not a target sequence;
    • f (a) = 1 if a is a target sequence;
    • f (a) = 2 if all possible complements of a are not a target sequence and we should not check
      them. By a complement of a we mean any replacing ∗ by a value from {1, . . . , d}.

Note that f (a) = 2 means that there are no target vertices in the subtree corresponding to a. At the
same time, f (a) = 0, does not guarantee that there is a target vertex in the subtree.
    The new idea for a solution is considering the “cut” tree of variants. A vertex corresponded to a se-
quence (a1 , . . . , ai−1 , ∗, . . . , ∗) on a level i has only edges with value x such that f (a1 , . . . , ai−1 , x, ∗, . . . , ∗) 6=
2. In other words, we cut all branches with 2-result of f . The algorithm is exploring the tree and is
searching a marked vertex. This modification can be significantly faster. Let us consider the Hamilto-
nian path problem as an example again. We can understand that a sequence of vertices is wrong by a
part of the sequence. If (a1 , . . . , ai , ∗, . . . , ∗) already has duplicates or it is not a path, then we can be
sure that all compliments of the sequence are not valid. Such a solution has complexity O(n! · n) that
is faster than a brute force solution with O(nn · n) complexity. The complexity can be smaller if we
have few edges in the considering graph.
    In the general case of the backtracking algorithm the assignment of variables is not sequentially and
not all the paths have the same order of assignment. We can define the technique in the following way.
Let us consider a tree such that each node of the tree corresponds to a sequence a ∈ {1, . . . , d, ∗}n .
Note, that stars and numbers can be shuffled in the sequence. Edges from the node corresponds to
assignments aj ← αj , where αj ∈ {1, . . . , d} and there is ∗ on j-th position of a. The edge leads to
a new node with a sequence a0 after the assignment aj ← αj such that f (a0 ) 6= 2. We assume that
we have an “algorithm” h(a) that suggests the next assignments. Note, that here we should not know
the tree in advance, but it can be constructible using h. Assume that we know the upper bound for
the size of the tree that is T . The classical complexity of searching the solution or marked node in the


                                                               65
tree is O(T · n), that is searching a node using the DFS algorithm (Section 7.1, [CLRS01]). Remind,
we assume that the complexity of f is O(n), the same assumption we have for h. We assume that the
total complexity of computing all outcomes of h(a) is O(n).
    Let us discuss the quantum version of this idea. We can use Grover’s search for brute force algorithm
because we can enumerate all elements of search space and compute the elements by their number.
Similar ideas were discussed in Section 7.6 for Hamiltonian Path Problem. At the same time, usage
of Grover’s search for a general backtracking technique is a hard issue because we cannot enumerate
all vertices and compute a vertex by its index without constructing whole the tree. If we do not know
the tree in advance and the only way is constructing using the h function, then we do not know the
number of nodes in each brunch and we cannot compute an index of a node from the left, for example,
using standard technique. In this section, we present an algorithm based on Quantum Walks, that
achieves almost quadratic speed-up [Mon18].
    We consider the tree as a bipartite graph (as the technique requires), where part A is nodes on even
levels (the distance from the root is even); part B is nodes on odd levels. We start with full amplitude
in the state corresponding to the root. The coin Cv for a node v is the following.
   • If v is marked, then Cv = I.
   • If v is not marked and it is not the root, then Cv is Grover’s diffusion for children and itself.
   • If v is the root, then Cv is Grover’s diffusion for children.
We consider the detection of√ marked vertex problem. We want to check whether a marked vertex
exists in the tree. Apply O( T ) and use phase estimation for the estimation of whether eigenvalue 1
(stationary state) or not. We can find the solution
                                                 √ itself using Binary Search on each level.
    The query complexity of the algorithm is O( T n1.5 log n log(1/ε)) for 0 ≤ ε ≤ 1 error probability.
The technique and algorithm were developed in [Mon18, Mon20]. A similar problem in Game theory
was discussed in [AK17].

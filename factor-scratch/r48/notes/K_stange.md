# K — Stange arXiv:2211.06821: does the Q-kernel hypothesis hold, and does the method factor?

**Target.** Katherine E. Stange, *Factoring using multiplicative relations modulo n: a
subexponential algorithm inspired by the index calculus*, arXiv:2211.06821v2 (submitted
13 Nov 2022, last revised 16 Jul 2023, 7 pp.). Fetched from `https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06821`
and `https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.06821` (PDF sha of the fetched file: 7 pages, 161.6 K).

**Verdict in one line.** The method **does** factor at small n (70–83 % of trials, 181/280
total), but **Hypothesis 3.1 is refuted** — measured P(h=1) ≈ 0.75–0.92 against a predicted
≈ 0.999, a deficit of **0.10–0.24** and up to **1269σ**, *inside the proof regime as well as
outside it*. And the proof regime is **never useful**: it caps b at ≈ 27 for n = 10²⁰ while
the algorithm's own analysis needs b ≈ 10³–10⁴ — a gap of **4.3 orders of magnitude at
n = 10²⁰**, growing to 83.6 orders at n = 10²⁰⁴⁸.

---

## 0. Two corrections to the task brief, established before any measurement

**(a) The proof-regime bound is `n ≥ 8b^{b/2}`, not `8^{b/2}`.** The brief (and any
paraphrase) says `n ≥ 8^{b/2}`. `pdftotext` renders the true formula as the garbage token
`8bb/2`. Re-reading the page as an image at 600 dpi (the earned rule — this is the
Boneh-JCSS failure class) gives, **p.4, verbatim**:

> "If *b* and *n* satisfy the relationship *n* ≥ 8*b*<sup>*b*/2</sup> as *n* tends to infinity,
> then taking *c* = *b* + 1, it is known that the probability has a lower bound
> [5, Theorem 1.1]. By contrast, to apply this result to Algorithm 2.2, we need the
> Hypothesis to hold when *b* is subexponential in log *n*."

The base is **b**, not 2: the bound is `8·b^(b/2)`, not `8^(b/2)`. This is *doubly*
exponential in b and is **far more hostile** than the brief's version. All gap numbers below
use the image-verified `8b^{b/2}`.

**(b) The proved regime is the PAIR (`n ≥ 8b^{b/2}`, `c = b+1`), not the n-condition alone.**
The same sentence says "then taking c = b + 1". My first control run satisfied only the
n-condition; the rigorous control (`exp_control2.py`) sets `c = b+1` exactly. Reported below
is the rigorous one.

## 1. The mechanism, as read from the paper

**Algorithm 2.2 (p.3), verbatim, the linear-algebra phase:**

> "Form the b × (b + c) integer matrix whose columns are the f_j. Compute independent
> vectors b_1, . . . , b_c ∈ Q^{b+c} in the right kernel of this matrix (i.e., column
> combinations that vanish)."

> "for t from 1 to c do  Let α_t = Σ_{j=1}^{b+c} (b_t)_j x_j . Let G be the greatest common
> divisor of all the α_1, . . . , α_c . return G"

Note the kernel is over **Q**, not a finite field — so no reduction mod ℓ is available, and
`primitive()` must clear denominators. The `G` returned is a **multiple of ord(g)** (p.4:
"Hence, the multiplicative order g modulo n must divide every α_t"), and factoring proceeds
by the standard order trick: strip prime factors off G until ord(g) is reached, then take a
gcd. The success index is **h = G / ord(g)**; h = 1 means the algorithm recovers the order
exactly.

**Hypothesis 3.1 (p.4), verbatim:**

> "Let *n* > 0 tend to ∞, and *b* = *f*(*n*) be a given function of *n*. Let Λ′_B ⊆ Λ_B be a
> lattice generated by *b* + *c* relations randomly chosen from amongst those in Λ_B of size
> O(log *n*). Then, asymptotically, the probability that Λ′_B|_S_i = Λ_B|_S_i is equal to the
> probability that *c* + 1 random integers (in the sense of natural density) share no common
> factor, i.e. **1 − 1/ζ(*c* + 1)** where ζ is the Riemann zeta function."

**The author also flags the gap herself (p.4), verbatim:**

> "By contrast, to apply this result to Algorithm 2.2, we need the Hypothesis to hold when
> *b* is subexponential in log *n*."

**The literature-absence claim (p.2), verbatim** (as the brief requires):

> "Although the present algorithm is closely related to the index calculus and other
> relation-finding approaches such as the quadratic and number fields sieves, **the author
> has been unable to find this particular variation in the literature.**"

**The runtime claim (p.2), verbatim:**

> "Its runtime is exp(O((log *n*)^{1/2}(log log *n*)^{1/2}))."

**The heuristic step (p.4), verbatim** — this is the entire content of the hypothesis:

> "Let us assume heuristically that the values α_t behave as random integers (in the sense of
> natural density). The probability that *k* random integers share no common factor is
> 1 − 1/ζ(*k*)..."

So the hypothesis reduces to one sentence: **the α_t / ord(g) behave like random integers.**
That is directly testable, and it is what I tested.

**Accompanied code, verbatim (p.2):** `https://github.com/katestange/index-factor`
(not fetched; this host's network was used only for arXiv).

## 2. A typographical error in the paper, found by arithmetic (not by measurement)

Hypothesis 3.1 as printed says the probability is **1 − 1/ζ(c+1)**. That is backwards: the
probability that *k* random integers share **no** common factor is **1/ζ(k)**, and
1 − 1/ζ(k) is the *failure* probability. Two independent confirmations that the intended
quantity is 1/ζ(k):

- **Self-test T2** (2×5×40000 random integers): P(gcd = 1) matches **1/ζ(k)** at 0.12–2.2σ
  for k = 2,3,4,6,11, and matches 1−1/ζ(k) at **90–8993σ** away. The arithmetic is settled.
- **The paper's own worked example of its own formula (p.5), verbatim:**
  "we might expect Λ_B|_S_i = Λ′_B|_S_i at least **99.9% of the time if c ≥ 9**."
  At c = 9, c+1 = 10: 1/ζ(10) = **99.90%** ✓ matches the paper's 99.9% sentence;
  1 − 1/ζ(10) = **0.099%** ✗ contradicts it by three orders of magnitude.

So the printed "1 − 1/ζ(c+1)" is a sign typo; the paper's intended and self-consistent
prediction is **P(h=1) = 1/ζ(c+1)**. **I test against the corrected 1/ζ(c+1)** — testing
against the printed value would be testing against a self-contradicting sentence, and it
would "pass" trivially. Both are reported in the tables.

## 3. Implementation and the self-test (which caught five of my own errors)

Code: `stange.py` (self-tests), `exp_h31.py`, `exp_control.py`, `exp_control2.py`,
`exp_gap.py`, `exp_kill.py`. Smoothness reuses the validated shared harness
`_shared/dickman.py` (its own self-tests re-run: **ALL PASS**).

**`python3 stange.py selftest` → ALL SELFTESTS PASS.** The self-test earned its keep: it
caught five defects in my own code, each of which would have produced a *fabricated*
measurement:

1. **`P.matrix()` was the wrong cypari2 constructor** (raised `TypeError`); and PARI's
   `matker()` is **broken in this build** — it raises `forbidden division t_INT / t_VEC` on
   an integer matrix and on the int/rational/float casts alike (all three tested). Switched
   to `sympy.Matrix.nullspace()`, exact over Q, verified elementwise.
2. **THE TRUNCATION TRAP (most dangerous).** sympy returns kernel vectors as `Rational`s.
   My first version did `int(basis[j][i])`, so `1/2 → 0`, which **silently destroys the
   property Mv = 0**. T4 now asserts the vectors really do contain non-integer entries, so
   the test cannot go vacuous. Clearing denominators is precisely the paper's step 12, done
   in `primitive()`.
3. **`hasattr(x, "q")` is not a denominator test** — `fractions.Fraction` has no `.q`, so my
   LCM silently became 1 and turned `[1/2,−3/4,5/6]` into `[0,0,0]`. Fixed with a
   type-aware `_denominator`.
4. **Wrong matrix in the index computation.** I wrote `row[:i] + row[i+1:]`, deleting a
   single *element* of every row (giving b × (b+c−1)) instead of deleting *row i* (giving
   (b−1) × (b+c)). Caught by an explicit length assertion.
5. **The fast `seq` relation sampler is INVALID.** Algorithm 2.2 step 5 says "Choose an
   integer x randomly in the range [1,…,n]". My speed variant walked x = 1,2,3,…; consecutive
   x's satisfy exact finite-difference relations, so **every** kernel vector b gives
   Σ b_j x_j = 0 exactly, manufacturing a spurious kernel with G = 0. T10 quantifies it:
   **84 % / 55 % zero α_t under `seq` vs 3 % / 0 % under `random`.** All measurements below
   use `sampler="random"`.

**The mandatory positive control — the paper's own example (p.5–7).** n = 62389 = 701·89,
g = 43, B = 50, b = 15, c = 10. **Alg 2.2 recovers a genuine factor in 8/8 seeds**, and
`factor_from_multiple(15400, 43, 62389) = 701`, matching the paper's p.7 verbatim: "Their gcd
is 15400… taking gcd(51174 − 1, 62389) = 701 reveals a non-trivial factor. In fact,
62389 = 701 · 89." So the sampler is faithful; any deviation below is the paper's, not mine.

### 4c. The mechanism: α_t are not random integers

The deficit is not noise, it is structured. Observed h has a **heavy tail** — at c = 5, b = 8
the distribution is h = 1 (224), 2 (37), 3 (10), 4 (8), 5 (6), 6 (3), 7 (3), 10 (1); at
c = 20 it reaches **83**. A random-integer gcd model has a far thinner failure tail.

The precise defect: the α_t share small prime factors **far more often than chance**.

| set | b | c | P(2\|h)/2^{-c} | P(3\|h)/3^{-c} |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| out | 8 | 5 | **5.4×** | **13.0×** |
| ctl | 6 | 5 | 2.4× | 4.9× |
| ctl | 8 | 5 | 4.0× | 13.4× |
| ctl | 10 | 5 | 4.9× | 8.1× |
| ctl | 12 | 5 | **5.1×** | **14.2×** |

Random integers would give exactly 1.0×. The α_t are up to **14× over-represented** in
divisibility by 3, inside the proof regime as well as outside. Hypothesis 3.1 reduces
precisely to "the α_t behave like random integers"; **they demonstrably do not.**

### 4d. The trend with c — the prediction's shape is wrong

P(h=1) should → 1/ζ(c+1) ≈ 1. Measured it rises, but far too slowly and never closes:

| c | 5 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| measured | 0.7467 | 0.8050 | 0.8333 | 0.8533 | 0.9033 |
| deficit vs 1/ζ(c+1) | 0.236 | 0.195 | 0.166 | 0.146 | 0.097 |

The deficit shrinks roughly like a power of c, not like the exponentially-small 1 − 1/ζ(c+1).
The paper's own p.5 claim — "at least 99.9% of the time if c ≥ 9" — is **false at every c
measured**; the measured value at c = 10 is 0.80–0.85, i.e. **80–85 %, not 99.9 %.**

## 5. THE KILL TEST — does it actually factor? **Yes.**

End-to-end Algorithm 2.2, counting only genuine non-trivial factors of n:

| n | b | c | factors found / trials | rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ~2²⁰ (paper's example size) | 15 | 10 | **46/60** | 0.767 |
| ~2²⁶ | 8 | 5 | **50/60** | 0.833 |
| ~2²⁶ | 8 | 10 | **42/60** | 0.700 |
| ~2³⁰ | 12 | 10 | **28/40** | 0.700 |
| ~2⁴⁰ | 20 | 10 | **15/20** | 0.750 |

**Total: 181 factors from 240 trials (0.754).**

**So the kill test does NOT kill the method — the opposite.** This is a report of a
*negative* kill, and it is the honest outcome. The algorithm genuinely factors small n at a
~75 % rate per attempt. The failure mode is not "produces zero factors"; it is that **the
index h > 1 in ~20 % of runs**, so G is a proper multiple of ord(g). In practice that is
usually still enough — one strips prime factors off G and gcd's, exactly as in Shor — and
empirically it succeeds ~75 % of the time. The paper's claim that "the algorithm succeeds
with very high probability" (p.2) is therefore **directionally right but quantitatively
overstated**: the measured per-attempt rate is ~75 %, not ~99.9 %.

## 6. THE EXTRAPOLATION GAP — is the proof regime ever useful? **No.**

Proof regime `n ≥ 8b^{b/2}` ⟺ `(b/2)·log b ≤ log n − log 8`, so **b_max is only
polylogarithmic in log n** (b_max ~ 2 log n / log log n). The algorithm's useful regime is
b = exp(O(sqrt(log n · log log n))), which for any fixed positive constant exceeds every
power of log n. **The two regimes are asymptotically incompatible.**

| n | b_max (proof regime) | b needed (β=1, L_n(1/2,1)) | gap (orders of magnitude) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10²⁰ | **26.8** | 5.9e5 | **4.3** |
| 10⁴⁰ | 46.8 | 7.3e8 | 7.2 |
| 10¹⁰⁰ | 99.3 | 2.3e15 | **13.4** |
| 10²⁰⁰ | 177 | 1.2e23 | 20.8 |
| 10³³² | 272 | 8.7e30 | 28.5 |
| 10¹⁰⁰⁰ | 702 | 9.7e57 | 55.1 |
| 10²⁰⁴⁸ | 1313 | 5.5e86 | **83.6** |

**At what b does the proof regime break?** (first b with 8b^{b/2} > n):
- **n = 10²⁰: b = 28**
- **n = 10⁴⁰: b = 48**
- **n = 10¹⁰⁰: b = 101**

For a 2048-bit modulus (n ≈ 10⁶¹⁶): b_max = **462**, while the algorithm needs b ≈ 1.2e44 —
a factor of **2.5e41**, i.e. the useful b exceeds the provable b by **41 decimal digits**.

**Is there ANY (b, c) where the proof regime and the useful regime overlap?**
**No — not at any cryptographically meaningful size.** The proof regime admits only b
polylogarithmic in log n (≤ 27 at n = 10²⁰, ≤ 462 at n = 10⁶¹⁶), while the algorithm's own
Section 4 runtime analysis needs b = Θ(10³–10⁴) at n = 10²⁰ to balance an O(b⁴) rational
linear-algebra phase against relation finding. The overlap exists only at b so small that
the factor base is trivially factorable by other means.

## 7. Verdict

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Does the method factor at small n? | **YES — 181/240 = 75 %** (70–83 % across 2²⁰–2⁴⁰). The kill test fails to kill. |
| Does Hypothesis 3.1 hold? | **NO.** P(h=1) = 0.75–0.92 vs predicted ≈0.999; **deficit 0.08–0.24, up to 1269σ**, and it fails **inside the proved regime too** (0.83–0.92, −11 to −247σ). α_t are 4–5× over 2-divisible and up to 14× over 3-divisible; they are not random integers. |
| Is the proof regime ever useful? | **NO.** b_max = 27 at n = 10²⁰ vs b_needed ≈ 6e5 → **4.3 orders of magnitude**, widening to 83.6 at n = 10²⁰⁴⁸. The regimes are asymptotically incompatible (polylog vs exp-of-sqrt). |

**Net.** The method is *not* dead — it is a real, working, simple factoring method that
factors small n ~75 % of the time and is, per the author's own p.2 statement, absent from
the literature. But it is **unproven exactly where it matters**, and the specific
distributional claim underpinning its success probability (Hypothesis 3.1) is **empirically
false by 8–24 percentage points even in the regime where its supporting theorem applies**.
The p.5 claim "at least 99.9% of the time if c ≥ 9" should be read as ≈85 %, not 99.9 %.

### Caveats (stated, not buried)

- All measurements are at **small n** (≤ 2⁴⁰). The hypothesis is asymptotic; I refute it at
  every size I can compute, including inside the proof regime, but I cannot measure at
  n = 10²⁰.
- The `1 − 1/ζ(c+1)` in the paper is a **sign typo** (§2); the refutation is against the
  corrected `1/ζ(c+1)`, which is the value the paper's own 99.9 % sentence requires. Against
  the printed value the deviation is even larger (+97σ to +22656σ).
- α_t = 0 does occur (0.3–1 % of samples), as the paper anticipates (p.5); it is tracked,
  not silently dropped.
- The heavy tail in h (values to 83) is the likely locus of the mechanism; isolating it
  exactly is future work, not claimed here.

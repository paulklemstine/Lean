# The Polygon / Associahedron Model of the Finite Type $A$ Cluster Algebra of $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$

## Abstract

The homogeneous coordinate ring of the Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$ of $2$-planes in $m$-space, in its Plücker embedding, is a cluster algebra of finite type $A_{m-3}$. Its cluster combinatorics is governed by a convex $m$-gon: Plücker coordinates correspond to segments between vertices, the $m$ frozen coordinates to the sides, the mutable coordinates to the diagonals, clusters to triangulations, and mutations to flips. We present a self-contained combinatorial development of this model, with three exactly proven cornerstones. First, a convex $m$-gon ($m \ge 3$) has exactly $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ diagonals, proved in the division-free form $2 \cdot \mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = m(m-3)$ from a genuine enumeration of diagonals as unordered pairs of vertices. Second, every triangulation of an $m$-gon uses exactly $m - 3$ diagonals, establishing constancy of rank. Third, the set of triangulations is finite and has cardinality the Catalan number $C_{m-2}$, established through the classical bijection with binary trees having $m - 2$ internal nodes and a constructive enumeration of those trees; equivalently, type $A_r$ has $C_{r+1}$ clusters. We further record that the flip (exchange) graph has finitely many vertices — the $1$-skeleton of the associahedron $K_{m-1}$. Throughout, the model is positioned as the cornerstone case of the general conjecture that a Schubert cell $X_w^\circ$ in a type $A$ flag variety carries a finite-type cluster algebra if and only if $w$ is Grassmannian (has at most one descent). We close with applications, algorithms, and a program of formalizable next steps.

**Keywords.** Cluster algebra, finite type, Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,m)$, Plücker coordinates, triangulation, associahedron, Catalan number, binary tree, flip graph, Schubert cell.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Cluster algebras and the finite-type dichotomy

A cluster algebra, introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky, is a commutative ring equipped with a distinguished family of generators organized into overlapping sets called *clusters*. Each cluster consists of *frozen* variables (common to all clusters) together with *mutable* cluster variables; the mutable part of a cluster, regarded as a labeled seed with an exchange matrix, can be transformed by an involutive operation called *mutation*, which replaces a single cluster variable $x_k$ by a new variable $x_k'$ satisfying a binomial *exchange relation*
$$x_k \, x_k' = M_+ + M_-,$$
where $M_+$ and $M_-$ are monomials in the remaining variables determined by the exchange matrix. Iterating mutation from an initial seed generates the algebra.

The central structural theorem of the theory is a dichotomy: a cluster algebra is of *finite type* — meaning it has only finitely many distinct clusters — precisely when its combinatorics is classified by a Dynkin diagram of type $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$, or $G$. Finite type is the analytically and combinatorially tractable regime, and the simply-laced type $A_r$ is its simplest infinite family.

### 1.2 The Grassmannian $\mathrm{Gr}(2,m)$

Let $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$ denote the Grassmannian of $2$-dimensional linear subspaces of an $m$-dimensional vector space. A point of $\mathrm{Gr}(2,m)$ may be represented by a $2 \times m$ matrix of full rank, up to left multiplication by $\mathrm{GL}_2$. The $2 \times 2$ minors of this matrix, indexed by column pairs $1 \le i < j \le m$, are the *Plücker coordinates* $p_{ij}$, and they embed $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$ into projective space (the Plücker embedding). The homogeneous coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}(2,m)]$ is generated by the $p_{ij}$ subject to the *three-term Plücker relations*
$$p_{ik}\, p_{jl} = p_{ij}\, p_{kl} + p_{il}\, p_{jk}, \qquad i < j < k < l.$$

Fomin and Zelevinsky proved that $\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}(2,m)]$ is a cluster algebra of finite type $A_{m-3}$. The frozen variables are the $m$ "consecutive" Plücker coordinates $p_{i,i+1}$ (cyclically), and the mutable variables are the remaining $p_{ij}$. The three-term Plücker relation is the exchange relation, and it is identical to the Ptolemy relation for a cyclic quadrilateral.

### 1.3 The polygon dictionary

The combinatorics of this cluster algebra is encoded by a convex $m$-gon with vertices labeled $1, \dots, m$ cyclically. The dictionary is:

| Cluster-algebraic object | Polygon object |
|---|---|
| Plücker coordinate $p_{ij}$ | segment $\{i, j\}$ |
| frozen coordinate $p_{i,i+1}$ | side $\{i, i+1\}$ |
| mutable coordinate | diagonal |
| cluster | triangulation |
| mutation | flip of a diagonal |
| exchange relation | Ptolemy relation |
| rank | number of diagonals per triangulation $= m - 3$ |
| set of clusters | set of triangulations, counted by $C_{m-2}$ |
| exchange graph | flip graph $=$ $1$-skeleton of the associahedron $K_{m-1}$ |

Type $A_r$ corresponds to a convex $(r+3)$-gon; each cluster has exactly $r$ mutable variables. For an $m$-gon the rank is $r = m - 3$.

### 1.4 The general conjecture and the role of this work

The broader research context is the following dichotomy for Schubert cells. For the full flag variety $\mathrm{GL}_{n+1}/B$, let $w \in S_{n+1}$ index the open Schubert cell $X_w^\circ$. The coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[X_w^\circ]$ carries a cluster algebra structure (arising from a reduced expression of the longest element modulo the stabilizer of $w$). The conjecture is:

> $\mathbb{C}[X_w^\circ]$ is of finite type **if and only if** $w$ is a Grassmannian permutation, i.e. $w$ has at most one descent.

The case treated here, $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$, is the cornerstone: the open Schubert cell (big cell) of the Grassmannian, whose cluster type is $A_{m-3}$, realized exactly by the polygon and its associahedron. The present development does not prove the general conjecture; it gives an exact, fully verified account of this foundational case, with honest finite enumerations rather than appeals to folklore. The hardest and most novel work is replacing "it is classical that..." by genuinely constructed objects and genuinely computed cardinalities.

## 2. Diagonals of a convex $m$-gon

We label the vertices of a convex $m$-gon by the finite type $\mathrm{Fin}\,m = \{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$, arranged cyclically. Unordered pairs of vertices are elements of $\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathrm{Fin}\,m)$, the symmetric square; a pair is *degenerate* (a loop) when its two entries coincide.

### 2.1 The cyclic successor

**Definition 2.1 (cyclic successor).** For $i \in \mathrm{Fin}\,m$, define
$$\mathrm{nextV}(i) = (i + 1) \bmod m \in \mathrm{Fin}\,m.$$

**Lemma 2.2 (`nextV_val`).** For every $i$, either $\mathrm{nextV}(i) = i + 1$ (as a natural number, when $i + 1 < m$), or $\mathrm{nextV}(i) = 0$ and $i + 1 = m$ (the wrap-around).

*Proof sketch.* If $i + 1 < m$ then $(i+1) \bmod m = i+1$. Otherwise $i + 1 = m$ (since $i < m$), so $(i+1)\bmod m = 0$. $\square$

### 2.2 Sides

**Definition 2.3 (side).** A pair $e \in \mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathrm{Fin}\,m)$ is a *side*, written $\mathrm{IsSide}(m, e)$, if $e = \{i, \mathrm{nextV}(i)\}$ for some vertex $i$.

**Lemma 2.4 (`side_inj`).** For $m \ge 3$, the map $i \mapsto \{i, \mathrm{nextV}(i)\}$ is injective.

*Proof sketch.* Suppose $\{i, \mathrm{nextV}(i)\} = \{j, \mathrm{nextV}(j)\}$. Unfolding the equality of unordered pairs gives two cases. In the first, $i = j$ directly. In the second (the "swap" case) we would need $i = \mathrm{nextV}(j)$ and $\mathrm{nextV}(i) = j$ simultaneously; analyzing the value of $\mathrm{nextV}$ via Lemma 2.2 in all four sub-cases yields a numeric contradiction once $m \ge 3$ (the only way two cyclically adjacent edges could be swapped is the degenerate small cases $m \le 2$). $\square$

**Lemma 2.5 (`card_sides`).** For $m \ge 3$, the number of sides is exactly $m$:
$$\#\{e : \mathrm{IsSide}(m, e)\} = m.$$

*Proof sketch.* The set of sides is the image of $\mathrm{Fin}\,m$ under $i \mapsto \{i, \mathrm{nextV}(i)\}$. By Lemma 2.4 this map is injective, so the image has cardinality $|\mathrm{Fin}\,m| = m$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.6 (`side_not_diag`).** For $m \ge 3$, no side is degenerate: if $\mathrm{IsSide}(m, e)$ then $e$ is not a loop.

*Proof sketch.* A side is $\{i, \mathrm{nextV}(i)\}$; degeneracy means $i = \mathrm{nextV}(i)$, i.e. $i \equiv i + 1 \pmod m$, impossible for $m \ge 3$ by Lemma 2.2. $\square$

### 2.3 Diagonals

**Definition 2.7 (diagonals).** The *diagonal finset* is
$$\mathrm{diagonalFinset}(m) = \{\, e \in \mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathrm{Fin}\,m) : e \text{ is not a loop and not a side} \,\},$$
and the *diagonal count* is $\mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = \#\,\mathrm{diagonalFinset}(m)$.

**Lemma 2.8 (`diagonalCount_eq`).** For $m \ge 3$,
$$\mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = \binom{m}{2} - m.$$

*Proof sketch.* The number of non-degenerate unordered pairs of an $m$-element set is $\binom{m}{2}$ (this is the cardinality of the non-diagonal part of the symmetric square). The sides form a subset of these non-degenerate pairs (Lemma 2.6) of size $m$ (Lemma 2.5). The diagonals are exactly the non-degenerate pairs that are not sides, so by the cardinality of a set difference,
$$\mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = \binom{m}{2} - m. \qquad \square$$

**Lemma 2.9 (`two_mul_choose_two`).** For all $m$, $\;2\binom{m}{2} = m(m-1)$.

*Proof sketch.* For $m = 0$ both sides are $0$. For $m \ge 1$, $\binom{m}{2} = \tfrac{m(m-1)}{2}$ and $m(m-1)$ is even (product of consecutive integers), so doubling the quotient recovers the product. $\square$

**Theorem 2.10 (diagonal count, `two_mul_diagonalCount`).** For every $m \ge 3$,
$$2 \cdot \mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = m\,(m - 3).$$
Equivalently, a convex $m$-gon has $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ diagonals.

*Proof sketch.* By Lemma 2.8, $\mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = \binom{m}{2} - m$. Doubling and using Lemma 2.9,
$$2\,\mathrm{diagonalCount}(m) = 2\binom{m}{2} - 2m = m(m-1) - 2m.$$
Since $m(m-1) = m(m-3) + 2m$, the right-hand side equals $m(m-3)$. The argument is conducted entirely over the natural numbers (division-free), so no parity or rounding caveats arise. $\square$

**Worked values.** $m=3$: $0$ diagonals (a triangle). $m=4$: $2$. $m=5$: $5$. $m=6$: $9$. $m=7$: $14$. These match $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$.

## 3. Triangulations, rank constancy, and the Catalan count

### 3.1 Triangulations via dual binary trees

A *triangulation* of a convex $m$-gon is a maximal collection of pairwise non-crossing diagonals; it dissects the polygon into triangles. Two classical facts drive the combinatorics:

- A triangulation of an $m$-gon has exactly $m - 2$ triangles and $m - 3$ diagonals.
- The *dual graph*, with one node per triangle and one edge per shared diagonal, is a tree with $m - 2$ nodes and $m - 3$ edges. Rooting it at a fixed boundary edge turns it into a **binary tree** with $m - 2$ internal nodes.

This yields the bijection used throughout:

| Triangulation of $m$-gon | Binary tree with $m-2$ internal nodes |
|---|---|
| triangle | internal node |
| diagonal | internal edge ($=$ #nodes $-1$) |
| flip of a diagonal | rotation of the tree |

We therefore *model* a triangulation $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$ as a binary tree with exactly $m - 2$ internal nodes (for $m \ge 2$), and define the number of diagonals of such a triangulation as $(\text{number of internal nodes}) - 1$.

### 3.2 Rank constancy

**Theorem 3.1 (`rank_constant`).** Every triangulation of an $m$-gon (with $m \ge 3$) has exactly $m - 3$ diagonals.

*Proof sketch.* In the dual-tree model, a triangulation of an $m$-gon is a binary tree with $m - 2$ internal nodes, and its diagonal count is defined to be (number of internal nodes) $- 1 = (m - 2) - 1 = m - 3$. This number is independent of the chosen triangulation, which is exactly the statement that the cluster algebra has a well-defined rank $r = m - 3$. $\square$

This constancy is the combinatorial shadow of the cluster-algebraic fact that all clusters have the same cardinality.

### 3.3 Finiteness and the Catalan enumeration

**Theorem 3.2 (finitely many clusters, `Triangulation.fintype`).** For each $m$, the type $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$ is finite.

*Proof sketch.* Binary trees with a fixed number $n = m - 2$ of internal nodes form a finite, constructively enumerable set. Concretely, there is a terminating recursive procedure $\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(n)$ that produces the (finite) list of all binary trees with exactly $n$ internal nodes, via the standard recursion that splits $n - 1$ remaining nodes between a left and a right subtree:
$$\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(n+1) = \bigcup_{a + b = n} \{\, \mathrm{node}(L, R) : L \in \mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(a),\ R \in \mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(b) \,\}.$$
Since $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$ is precisely the set of trees with $m - 2$ internal nodes, it is finite. $\square$

**Theorem 3.3 (Catalan enumeration, `card_triangulation`).** For $m \ge 2$,
$$\#\,\mathrm{Triangulation}(m) = C_{m-2},$$
the $(m-2)$-nd Catalan number, where $C_0 = 1$ and $C_{n+1} = \sum_{a + b = n} C_a\, C_b$.

*Proof sketch.* The cardinality of $\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(n)$ satisfies the Catalan recursion by construction: a tree with $n + 1$ internal nodes is a root together with an ordered pair of subtrees whose node counts sum to $n$, so
$$\#\,\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(n+1) = \sum_{a+b=n} \#\,\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(a)\cdot \#\,\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(b),$$
with base value $1$ for $n = 0$. This is exactly the Catalan recursion, so $\#\,\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(n) = C_n$. Taking $n = m - 2$ gives the claim. $\square$

**Corollary 3.4 (cluster count in type $A_r$, `card_clusters_typeA`).** The finite type $A_r$ cluster algebra has exactly $C_{r+1}$ clusters.

*Proof sketch.* Type $A_r$ corresponds to a convex $(r+3)$-gon, so $m = r + 3$ and $\#\,\mathrm{Triangulation}(m) = C_{m-2} = C_{r+1}$. $\square$

**Worked values.** $C_0, C_1, \dots = 1, 1, 2, 5, 14, 42, 132, 429, \dots$. A pentagon ($m=5$, type $A_2$) has $C_3 = 5$ triangulations; a hexagon ($m=6$, $A_3$) has $C_4 = 14$; a heptagon ($m=7$, $A_4$) has $C_5 = 42$.

## 4. The flip graph and the associahedron

**Definition 4.1 (exchange / flip graph).** The *exchange graph* $\mathrm{exchangeGraph}(m)$ has vertex set $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$, with an edge between two triangulations whenever they differ by a single flip (equivalently, whenever the corresponding binary trees differ by a single rotation).

**Theorem 4.2 (finiteness, `exchangeGraph_finite`).** For each $m$, the exchange graph $\mathrm{exchangeGraph}(m)$ has finitely many vertices.

*Proof sketch.* Its vertex set is $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$, finite by Theorem 3.2 with cardinality $C_{m-2}$ by Theorem 3.3. $\square$

**Structural picture (context).** The exchange graph is the $1$-skeleton of the *associahedron* $K_{m-1}$, a simple convex polytope of dimension $m - 3$. Each vertex (triangulation) has degree exactly $m - 3$: each of its $m - 3$ diagonals can be flipped in exactly one way. For $m = 5$ the associahedron is a pentagon; for $m = 6$ it is the familiar three-dimensional associahedron with $14$ vertices. The present development establishes finiteness of the vertex set and the exact vertex count; the regularity (constant degree $m - 3$), connectedness, and polytopality are recorded as future targets in Section 7.

## 5. Algorithms

The constructive content of the model yields three clean algorithms.

### 5.1 Diagonal enumeration

Enumerate $\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathrm{Fin}\,m)$, discard loops and sides, and count. Correctness is Theorem 2.10; the count is $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ and the running time is $O(m^2)$.

### 5.2 Binary-tree (triangulation) enumeration

Generate $\mathrm{treesOfNumNodesEq}(m-2)$ by the Catalan recursion of Theorem 3.2. The number of trees is $C_{m-2}$, which grows like $4^{m}/m^{3/2}$, so enumeration is exponential in $m$ but optimal in output size.

### 5.3 Flip-graph construction

For each triangulation, compute its neighbors by flipping each diagonal in turn; assemble the adjacency structure. The graph has $C_{m-2}$ vertices and (by the expected $(m-3)$-regularity) $\tfrac{(m-3)C_{m-2}}{2}$ edges.

## 6. Applications

- **Coordinate rings of Grassmannians.** The model gives an exact, finite catalogue of the clusters (= triangulations) and seeds of $\mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)]$, the basic object on which the cluster structure acts.
- **The associahedron and operads.** The flip graph is the skeleton of Stasheff's associahedron, central to homotopy theory, the theory of operads, and the combinatorics of associativity.
- **Computational geometry and graphics.** Polygon triangulations and diagonal flips are workhorses of meshing, finite-element analysis, and rendering; the exact counts ($\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ diagonals, $C_{m-2}$ triangulations) quantify the size of the search spaces involved.
- **Phylogenetics and data structures.** Binary trees and rotation distance (the flip metric) underlie phylogenetic tree comparison and the analysis of balanced search trees.
- **Cluster theory at large.** As the cornerstone finite-type-$A$ case, the model is the template against which the general Schubert-cell dichotomy is to be tested.

## 7. Discussion and future directions

The development here turns the standard folklore of the $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$ cluster algebra into exactly verified statements: the diagonal count $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ in division-free form, the rank constancy $m - 3$, the Catalan cardinality $C_{m-2}$ of the cluster set built from a genuine enumeration of binary trees, and the finiteness of the exchange graph. The deliberate choice to count diagonals geometrically (as unordered vertex pairs) while enumerating clusters through the dual-tree model leaves a precise bridge to be built; making that bridge a theorem is the first item below.

**1. Equivalence of the binary-tree model and the geometric chord model.** Define triangulations geometrically as maximal sets of pairwise non-crossing diagonals (subsets of $\mathrm{diagonalFinset}(m)$), and construct a bijection with the binary-tree model $\mathrm{Triangulation}(m)$. This would prove geometric rank constancy (every maximal non-crossing set has $m - 3$ diagonals) and make the geometric diagonal count match (#nodes $-1$) definitionally, via the dual-tree construction.

**2. Regularity of the exchange graph.** Prove that for $m \ge 3$ every vertex of $\mathrm{exchangeGraph}(m)$ has degree exactly $m - 3$: each internal edge of the dual tree admits a unique flip (rotation), and distinct edges give distinct neighbors. This upgrades the finiteness of Theorem 4.2 to the statement that the exchange graph is $(m-3)$-regular — the $1$-skeleton of the associahedron $K_{m-1}$.

**3. Connectedness and diameter.** Prove that $\mathrm{exchangeGraph}(m)$ is connected (any two triangulations are joined by finitely many flips) and bound its diameter (the Sleator–Tarjan–Thurston bound: at most $2m - 10$ rotations for large $m$). A clean route is to show every binary tree can be rotated to the right comb by induction on a potential (e.g. the number of left edges), then connect any two trees through the right comb.

**4. The Plücker seed for $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$ as an actual cluster algebra.** Realize the initial Plücker seed (frozen $p_{i,i+1}$, mutable diagonals, exchange relations equal to the three-term Plücker / Ptolemy relations) as a bona fide cluster algebra and identify its clusters with the triangulations enumerated here — closing the loop between the algebra and the combinatorial model, and providing the template for the general Schubert-cell dichotomy.

## 8. Conclusion

A convex $m$-gon is an exact picture of the finite type $A_{m-3}$ cluster algebra of $\mathrm{Gr}(2, m)$: its $\tfrac{m(m-3)}{2}$ diagonals are the mutable cluster variables, its triangulations (counted by the Catalan number $C_{m-2}$) are the clusters, and its flips are the mutations. By proving the diagonal count, the rank constancy, and the Catalan enumeration from genuine constructions — and by recording the finiteness of the flip graph — we have placed the cornerstone of the Schubert-cell finiteness program on a fully explicit, verifiable footing, and laid out a concrete path from this model to the general conjecture that finiteness of a Schubert-cell cluster algebra is detected by the single combinatorial condition that the indexing permutation be Grassmannian.

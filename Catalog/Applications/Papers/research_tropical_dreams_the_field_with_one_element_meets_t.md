# Tropical Dreams, Reforged: A Formal Bridge Between 𝔽₁-Combinatorics and Tropical Convexity

## Abstract

We establish a rigorous, machine-verified correspondence between tropical convexity and 𝔽₁-combinatorics in the setting of finite distributive lattices. We prove five theorems: (1) tropically indecomposable elements under the join operation are exactly the sup-irreducible elements; (2) every element of a finite distributive lattice is generated by the sup-irreducibles below it; (3) in Boolean lattices, the sup-irreducibles are exactly the singletons; (4) the 𝔽₁-cardinality of a Boolean lattice equals the ground set size; (5) sup-preserving maps are uniquely determined by their values on sup-irreducible elements (the base change theorem). All proofs are formalized in Lean 4 with Mathlib, verified by the kernel, and depend only on the standard axioms (propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound). We provide verified algorithms for extracting 𝔽₁-points, computational demonstrations on Boolean lattices, divisor lattices, and chain lattices, and discuss extensions toward toric geometry and Birkhoff representation.

**Keywords:** tropical geometry, field with one element, 𝔽₁-geometry, finite distributive lattices, sup-irreducible elements, Birkhoff representation, base change, formal verification

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

The idea that tropical geometry and 𝔽₁-geometry should be "the same thing" has been a guiding metaphor in arithmetic geometry since the work of Connes–Consani [CC10], Deitmar [Dei05], and others. The tropical semiring (ℝ ∪ {∞}, min, +) exhibits the same idempotent character as the hypothetical "field with one element," and tropical varieties share structural features with schemes over 𝔽₁ as envisioned by Tits, Soulé, and Borger.

However, making this identification precise has remained elusive. The categorical frameworks for 𝔽₁-geometry (monoid schemes, blueprints, Λ-rings) and for tropical geometry (tropical varieties, valuated matroids, tropical linear spaces) do not directly correspond at the level of objects and morphisms.

### 1.2 Our Contribution

We identify a concrete, formalizable setting where the tropical/𝔽₁ correspondence becomes a theorem: **finite distributive lattices** equipped with the join operation as the tropical combination. In this setting:

- **Tropical extreme points** (elements that cannot be decomposed as a nontrivial join) coincide with **sup-irreducible elements** (the standard order-theoretic notion).
- These elements serve as **𝔽₁-points**: the combinatorial generators from which the entire lattice is reconstructed.
- The **base change theorem** shows that structure-preserving maps are determined by their values on 𝔽₁-points, formalizing the philosophical principle that "𝔽₁-data determines ℤ-data."

### 1.3 Relationship to Prior Work

Our work connects to several threads:

- **Birkhoff's representation theorem** [Bir37]: every finite distributive lattice is isomorphic to the lattice of lower sets of its poset of join-irreducibles. Our Theorem 2 (generation by 𝔽₁-points) is a key component of this classical result, and our base change theorem (Theorem 5) can be seen as a functorial refinement.
- **Connes–Consani 𝔽₁-geometry** [CC10]: our 𝔽₁-cardinality and base change theorem provide a concrete finite model of their "base change from 𝔽₁ to ℤ."
- **Tropical convexity** [DS04]: our notion of tropical indecomposability parallels the notion of extreme point of a tropical polytope.
- **Formal concept analysis** [GW99]: our sup-irreducible elements correspond to attribute concepts, providing a bridge between 𝔽₁-geometry and knowledge representation.

## 2. Definitions and Notation

### 2.1 Tropical Indecomposability

**Definition 1** (Tropically indecomposable element). Let $(L, \vee)$ be a join-semilattice. An element $v \in L$ is *tropically indecomposable* if for all $a, b \in L$:
$$a \vee b = v \implies a = v \text{ or } b = v.$$

In Lean 4:
```lean
def IsIndecomposable {α : Type*} (op : α → α → α) (v : α) : Prop :=
  ∀ a b, op a b = v → a = v ∨ b = v
```

### 2.2 Sup-Irreducibility

**Definition 2** (Sup-irreducible). In a join-semilattice, $v$ is *sup-irreducible* (or join-irreducible) if $v$ is not minimal and for all $a, b$: $a \vee b = v \implies a = v \text{ or } b = v$.

This is the standard Mathlib definition `SupIrred`:
```
SupIrred v ↔ ¬IsMin v ∧ ∀ b c, b ⊔ c = v → b = v ∨ c = v
```

### 2.3 𝔽₁-Cardinality

**Definition 3**. The *𝔽₁-cardinality* of a finite join-semilattice $L$ is:
$$\text{F1Card}(L) = |\{v \in L : \text{SupIrred}(v)\}|$$

### 2.4 Sup-Irreducible Extraction Algorithm

**Algorithm 1** (𝔽₁-point extraction).

```
Input: Finite lattice L with elements, join operation ⊔, bottom ⊥
Output: Set of sup-irreducible elements

function ExtractF1Points(L):
    result ← ∅
    for each v ∈ L \ {⊥}:
        is_irred ← true
        for each (a, b) ∈ L × L:
            if a ⊔ b = v and a ≠ v and b ≠ v:
                is_irred ← false
                break
        if is_irred:
            result ← result ∪ {v}
    return result
```

**Complexity:** O(n³) time, O(n) space, where n = |L|.

**Correctness:** Verified formally in Lean via `mem_supIrredFinset_iff`.

## 3. Main Results

### 3.1 Theorem 1: Tropical Indecomposability = Sup-Irreducibility

**Theorem 1.** Let $L$ be a join-semilattice with bottom element $\bot$. For any $v \in L$:
$$\text{IsIndecomposable}(\vee, v) \iff \text{SupIrred}(v) \lor v = \bot$$

**Corollary.** For $v \neq \bot$: $\text{IsIndecomposable}(\vee, v) \iff \text{SupIrred}(v)$.

**Proof sketch.** The forward direction: if $v$ is indecomposable and $v \neq \bot$, then $v$ is not minimal (since $\bot \leq v$ and $\bot \neq v$ would give a strictly smaller element), and the join-irreducibility condition holds by definition. The backward direction: SupIrred gives the second condition directly, and $v = \bot$ is indecomposable because $a \vee b = \bot$ implies $a \leq \bot$ and $b \leq \bot$, so $a = b = \bot = v$. □

**Significance.** This theorem establishes that the philosophical notion of "𝔽₁-point" (tropically indecomposable element) has a precise, standard characterization in lattice theory. The identification is not approximate — it is exact.

### 3.2 Theorem 2: Generation by 𝔽₁-Points

**Theorem 2.** Let $L$ be a finite distributive lattice with bottom. For every $x \in L$:
$$x = \bigvee\{e \in L : \text{SupIrred}(e) \text{ and } e \leq x\}$$

**Proof sketch.** By well-founded induction on $x$.

*Base case:* If $x = \bot$, the set of sup-irreducibles below $x$ is empty (since sup-irreducibles are not minimal), so $\bigvee \emptyset = \bot = x$.

*Inductive case:* If $x$ is itself sup-irreducible, then $x$ appears in the set, and since all elements of the set are $\leq x$, the join equals $x$.

If $x$ is not sup-irreducible and $x \neq \bot$, then there exist $a, b < x$ with $a \vee b = x$. By induction, $a = \bigvee\{e : \text{SupIrred}(e), e \leq a\}$ and $b = \bigvee\{e : \text{SupIrred}(e), e \leq b\}$. Every sup-irreducible $\leq a$ or $\leq b$ is also $\leq x$, so $x = a \vee b \leq \bigvee\{e : \text{SupIrred}(e), e \leq x\} \leq x$. □

**Significance.** This is the "generation theorem": every element of a finite distributive lattice is built from its 𝔽₁-skeleton. The tropical object is fully controlled by its irreducible generators.

### 3.3 Theorem 3: Boolean Lattice — Singletons are 𝔽₁-Points

**Theorem 3.** In the Boolean lattice $\mathcal{P}(\alpha) = (\text{Finset}\ \alpha, \cup)$, for any $s \in \text{Finset}\ \alpha$:
$$\text{SupIrred}(s) \iff \exists a \in \alpha,\ s = \{a\}$$

**Proof sketch.** Forward: if $|s| \geq 2$, pick $a \in s$ and write $s = \{a\} \cup (s \setminus \{a\})$, contradicting sup-irreducibility. If $s = \emptyset$, then $s$ is minimal, contradicting $\neg\text{IsMin}$. So $|s| = 1$.

Backward: if $s = \{a\}$ and $b \cup c = \{a\}$, then $a \in b$ or $a \in c$. If $a \in b$, then $\{a\} \subseteq b \subseteq b \cup c = \{a\}$, so $b = \{a\} = s$. □

### 3.4 Theorem 4: 𝔽₁-Cardinality of Boolean Lattice

**Theorem 4.** $\text{F1Card}(\text{Finset}\ \alpha) = |\alpha|$ for any finite type $\alpha$.

**Proof sketch.** By Theorem 3, the sup-irreducible elements are exactly the singletons $\{a\}$ for $a \in \alpha$. The map $a \mapsto \{a\}$ is a bijection between $\alpha$ and the set of sup-irreducibles. □

### 3.5 Theorem 5: Base Change — Determination by 𝔽₁-Points

**Theorem 5.** Let $L$ be a finite distributive lattice with bottom, and $M$ a join-semilattice with bottom. If $f, g : L \to M$ are sup-and-bot-preserving maps (SupBotHoms) satisfying $f(e) = g(e)$ for all sup-irreducible $e \in L$, then $f = g$.

**Proof sketch.** For any $x \in L$, by Theorem 2, $x = \bigvee S$ where $S = \{e : \text{SupIrred}(e), e \leq x\}$. Since $f$ and $g$ preserve finite joins and bot:
$$f(x) = f(\bigvee S) = \bigvee_{e \in S} f(e) = \bigvee_{e \in S} g(e) = g(\bigvee S) = g(x). \quad \square$$

**Significance.** This is the finite affine "base change" theorem. It says that the 𝔽₁-skeleton (the sup-irreducible elements with their ordering) carries all the information needed to reconstruct any structure-preserving map. Passing from the 𝔽₁-skeleton to the full lattice (or to any lattice that receives a sup-preserving map) is a "free extension" — exactly the base change from 𝔽₁ to ℤ in the arithmetic geometry metaphor.

### 4.3 Möbius Function Comparison

For Boolean lattices B_n, $\mu(\bot, \top) = (-1)^n$, while F1Card = n. These are distinct invariants: the Möbius function captures the topology of the order complex (which is contractible for Boolean lattices), while F1Card captures the combinatorial generation structure.

### 4.4 Base Change Verification

For B_3, we verified that two sup-preserving maps agreeing on the three singletons agree on all 8 elements. Conversely, maps differing on even one singleton differ on composite elements.

## 5. Algorithms

### 5.1 𝔽₁-Point Extraction

**Algorithm 1** has time complexity O(n³) and space complexity O(n). For specific lattice families, faster algorithms exist:
- **Boolean lattices:** O(n) time — singletons are the 𝔽₁-points by Theorem 3.
- **Divisor lattices:** O(n log n) time — prime power detection via factorization.
- **Chain lattices:** O(n) time — all non-bottom elements are sup-irreducible.

### 5.2 Birkhoff Map Computation

Given a finite distributive lattice L with sup-irreducibles J = {j₁, ..., jₖ}, the Birkhoff map φ: L → P(J) defined by φ(x) = {j ∈ J : j ≤ x} is computable in O(n·k) time and yields an order isomorphism onto the lattice of lower sets of J.

### 5.3 Sup-Preserving Map Reconstruction

Given values f(j₁), ..., f(jₖ) on the sup-irreducibles, the unique sup-preserving extension to all of L is computed by:
$$f(x) = \bigvee\{f(j) : j \in J, j \leq x\}$$
This takes O(n·k) join operations.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Why Finite Distributive Lattices?

The choice of finite distributive lattices as the 𝔽₁-model is not arbitrary. Several features make them the natural first case:

1. **Idempotency:** The join operation satisfies x ∨ x = x, matching the tropical semiring's idempotent addition.
2. **Distributivity:** The lattice identity x ∧ (y ∨ z) = (x ∧ y) ∨ (x ∧ z) ensures that the theory of irreducibles is well-behaved.
3. **Birkhoff's theorem:** The representation as lower sets provides a canonical "coordinate system" using 𝔽₁-points.
4. **Ubiquity:** Finite distributive lattices arise naturally in combinatorics (Young's lattice, partition lattices), algebraic geometry (face lattices of polytopes, orbit lattices of toric varieties), and computer science (concept lattices, domain theory).

### 6.2 What "Base Change" Means

In classical algebraic geometry, base change from a field k to an extension K transforms k-varieties into K-varieties by tensoring: X_K = X ×_Spec k Spec K. In our finite setting, "base change from 𝔽₁ to ℤ" is the passage from the poset of sup-irreducibles (the 𝔽₁-data) to the full lattice (the ℤ-data) via the free completion (taking lower sets). Theorem 5 shows this passage is faithful: no information is lost or ambiguously recovered.

### 6.3 Limitations

1. **Finite setting only:** We work with finite lattices. Extending to infinite lattices (e.g., algebraic lattices, continuous lattices) requires additional completeness assumptions.
2. **Distributivity required:** Non-distributive lattices (e.g., the lattice of subgroups of a non-abelian group) do not admit a clean Birkhoff representation, and the generation theorem may fail.
3. **No geometric content:** We do not construct tropical varieties or toric varieties; our results live purely in the combinatorial/order-theoretic realm.

### 6.4 Conjectural Extensions

**Conjecture 1** (Tropical 𝔽₁-Euler principle). For a finite simple polytope P with face lattice L(P), F1Card(L(P)) equals the number of vertices f₀(P).

This is verified for simplices (where L(P) is Boolean) and is expected to hold for all simple polytopes since the vertices of a simple polytope are precisely the atoms of its face lattice, and atoms are sup-irreducible.

**Conjecture 2** (Toric fan reconstruction). For a complete smooth toric variety X with fan Σ, the 𝔽₁-skeleton of the orbit lattice of X is isomorphic to the poset of rays of Σ.

## 7. Future Work

1. **Birkhoff representation theorem:** Formalize the full order isomorphism between a finite distributive lattice and the lattice of lower sets of its sup-irreducible poset.

2. **Non-distributive extensions:** Investigate which results extend to modular or semimodular lattices, where partial Birkhoff-type representations exist.

3. **Tropical variety connections:** Connect the lattice-theoretic 𝔽₁-skeleton to the type decomposition of tropical linear spaces and the Bergman fan of a matroid.

4. **Computational complexity:** Study the complexity of 𝔽₁-point extraction for implicit lattices (where elements are not listed but accessed via an oracle).

5. **𝔽₁-zeta functions:** Define and study zeta functions associated to the 𝔽₁-skeleton, connecting to the motivic zeta functions of Connes–Consani.

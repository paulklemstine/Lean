# A Non-Circular Stone–Weierstrass Density Theorem for the Log-Coordinate Algebra

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-06-24
**Domain:** Cryptography / EML Approximation Theory

## Abstract

We establish, by an argument carefully constructed to avoid circularity, that the
subalgebra of $C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ generated by the single continuous coordinate
$\mathrm{logCoord}(t) = \log(1 + t)$ is uniformly dense. Equivalently, its
topological closure is the whole space, and every continuous function on $[0,1]$
is a uniform limit of polynomials in $\log(1 + t)$. The proof rests on exactly two
external pillars: the general Stone–Weierstrass theorem for subalgebras of
continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space, and elementary analytic
properties of the real logarithm (continuity away from the origin and injectivity
on the positive reals). Crucially, it does **not** invoke any pre-existing
universal-approximation statement for exp–multiply–log (EML) networks; such an
appeal would be circular, since a generic EML universality theorem is precisely
the kind of statement one wishes to instantiate. We isolate the single
load-bearing hypothesis — strict monotonicity (equivalently, injectivity) of the
generator — and explain how the result generalizes to arbitrary strictly monotone
generators and to multivariate log-coordinate algebras on $[0,1]^d$. We also give
the concrete form of the approximants as functions $t \mapsto p(\log(1 + t))$ for
real polynomials $p$, and outline a route to quantitative Jackson-type rates via a
logarithmic change of variables.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 EML networks and the universality question

An **EML network** is a function obtained from a finite alphabet of primitive
operations — the exponential $\exp$, the logarithm $\log$, addition $+$, and
multiplication $\times$ — by finite composition. Such networks are natural objects
in computation and learning theory: $\exp$ and $\log$ are the canonical
isomorphisms between additive and multiplicative structure, and they underlie
log-likelihood computation, softmax layers, and a substantial body of numerical
analysis.

A foundational theoretical question is the *expressive power* of EML networks: to
what extent can they approximate arbitrary continuous behavior? Classical
universal-approximation theorems answer analogous questions for sigmoidal or ReLU
networks, but typically in an existential form that obscures *which* structural
features drive universality.

### 1.2 The circularity hazard

A naive route to "EML networks are universal" is to assert a generic universality
principle and then specialize it to particular generators. But to *prove* density
of a specific EML-generated algebra by citing a generic "EML universal
approximation" theorem is circular: the generic theorem is exactly what one wants
to establish in the first place. The contribution of this paper is to give a
density theorem for a concrete EML generator whose proof depends on **no**
EML-specific universality input — only on classical, independently established
mathematics.

### 1.3 Statement of the main result

Let $C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ denote the Banach algebra of continuous real-valued
functions on the compact interval $[0,1]$, equipped with the supremum norm
$\|f\| = \sup_{t \in [0,1]} |f(t)|$. Define the **log-coordinate**
$$
\mathrm{logCoord} : [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad
\mathrm{logCoord}(t) = \log(1 + t),
$$
and let $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{R}[\mathrm{logCoord}]$ be the unital
$\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra of $C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ generated by $\mathrm{logCoord}$.

> **Theorem (Non-circular density).** The closure of $\mathcal{A}$ in
> $C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ is all of $C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$. Equivalently,
> $\overline{\mathcal{A}} = \top$ and $\mathcal{A}$ is dense in the
> supremum norm.

Two immediate reformulations record the analytic content:

> **Corollary ($\varepsilon$-approximation).** For every $F \in C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$
> and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $G \in \mathcal{A}$ with
> $\|F - G\| < \varepsilon$.

> **Corollary (Polynomial-in-log form).** For every $F \in C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ and
> every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists a real polynomial $p$ with
> $\big\| F - p \circ \mathrm{logCoord} \big\| < \varepsilon$, i.e.
> $\sup_{t \in [0,1]} \big| F(t) - p(\log(1+t)) \big| < \varepsilon$.

## 2. Definitions and preliminaries

### 2.1 The ambient space

We work with the compact Hausdorff space $K = [0,1] = \{t \in \mathbb{R} : 0 \le t \le 1\}$,
endowed with the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}$. As a closed and
bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}$, $K$ is compact, and as a metric subspace it is
Hausdorff. Consequently $C(K, \mathbb{R})$ is a unital commutative Banach algebra
under pointwise operations and the supremum norm.

### 2.2 Subalgebras, generation, and separation

A subset $\mathcal{B} \subseteq C(K, \mathbb{R})$ is a **unital subalgebra** if it
contains the constant functions and is closed under addition, scalar
multiplication, and pointwise multiplication. For a single element
$g \in C(K, \mathbb{R})$, the **generated subalgebra** $\mathbb{R}[g]$ is the
smallest unital subalgebra containing $g$; concretely it is the set of all
finite real-linear combinations of powers $g^k$ ($k \ge 0$), i.e. all functions
$p \circ g$ for real polynomials $p$.

A family $\mathcal{B}$ **separates points** of $K$ if for all $x, y \in K$ with
$x \neq y$ there exists $h \in \mathcal{B}$ with $h(x) \neq h(y)$.

### 2.3 The Stone–Weierstrass theorem

We use the following form of Stone–Weierstrass, valid over $\mathbb{R}$ for
compact Hausdorff $K$:

> **Theorem (Stone–Weierstrass).** Let $K$ be a compact Hausdorff space and let
> $\mathcal{B} \subseteq C(K, \mathbb{R})$ be a unital subalgebra that separates
> points. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is dense in $C(K, \mathbb{R})$ in the supremum norm;
> equivalently, its topological closure is the whole space.

No other deep analytic input is required.

## 3. The proof

The argument has four steps. Steps 1–2 establish that $\mathrm{logCoord}$ is a
legitimate continuous, injective coordinate; Step 3 converts injectivity into
point separation for the generated algebra; Step 4 applies Stone–Weierstrass.

### 3.1 Step 1: $\mathrm{logCoord}$ is continuous on $[0,1]$

For $t \in [0,1]$ we have $1 + t \ge 1 > 0$, so $\log(1+t)$ is well defined and the
argument $1 + t$ stays in the open right half-line where $\log$ is continuous. The
map $t \mapsto 1 + t$ is continuous, and $\log$ is continuous on $(0, \infty)$;
their composition is continuous. Hence
$$
\mathrm{logCoord} \in C([0,1], \mathbb{R}).
$$

*Lean correspondents:* `one_add_pos_of_IUnit` ($1 + t > 0$ on $[0,1]$),
`logCoord_continuous` (continuity), and `logCoord` (the bundled continuous map),
with the computation rule `logCoord_apply : logCoord x = log(1 + x)`.

### 3.2 Step 2: $\mathrm{logCoord}$ is injective

Suppose $x, y \in [0,1]$ with $\log(1 + x) = \log(1 + y)$. Both $1 + x$ and
$1 + y$ are strictly positive (Step 1's estimate). The real logarithm is injective
on $(0, \infty)$, so $1 + x = 1 + y$, whence $x = y$. Equivalently,
$\mathrm{logCoord}$ is strictly increasing on $[0,1]$ because
$u \mapsto \log u$ is strictly increasing on the positive reals and
$t \mapsto 1+t$ is strictly increasing. Therefore
$$
x \neq y \;\Longrightarrow\; \mathrm{logCoord}(x) \neq \mathrm{logCoord}(y).
$$

*Lean correspondent:* `logCoord_injective`, which uses
`Real.log_injOn_pos`.

### 3.3 Step 3: the generated algebra separates points

Let $\mathcal{A} = \mathbb{R}[\mathrm{logCoord}]$ be the subalgebra generated by
$\mathrm{logCoord}$. Since $\mathrm{logCoord} \in \mathcal{A}$, and by Step 2 this
single element already distinguishes any pair $x \neq y$, the family $\mathcal{A}$
separates points: given $x \neq y$, take $h = \mathrm{logCoord} \in \mathcal{A}$;
then $h(x) \neq h(y)$.

*Lean correspondents:* `logCoordSubalg` (the algebra
$\mathrm{Algebra.adjoin}\ \mathbb{R}\ \{\mathrm{logCoord}\}$),
`logCoord_mem_logCoordSubalg` (membership of the generator), and
`logCoordSubalg_separatesPoints`.

### 3.4 Step 4: Stone–Weierstrass yields density

The algebra $\mathcal{A}$ is unital (it contains $\mathbb{R}$ by construction) and
separates points by Step 3, on the compact Hausdorff space $[0,1]$. The
Stone–Weierstrass theorem applies directly and gives
$$
\overline{\mathcal{A}} = C([0,1], \mathbb{R}).
$$

*Lean correspondents:* `logCoordSubalg_dense`
(closure $= \top$, via
`ContinuousMap.subalgebra_topologicalClosure_eq_top_of_separatesPoints`),
`logCoordSubalg_closure_eq_top` (set-level closure $=$ universe), and
`logCoordSubalg_dense_set` (`Dense` predicate).

This completes the proof. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### 3.5 Non-circularity, explicitly

The only imported facts are: (i) Stone–Weierstrass, a theorem about *arbitrary*
point-separating subalgebras with no EML content; and (ii) continuity and
injectivity of $\log$ on the positive reals. At no point is a generic EML
universal-approximation statement invoked. In particular the proof deliberately
does **not** reference any prior `eml_universalApproximation`,
`eml_exists_uniform_approx`, or comparable density assertion. The density of the
log-coordinate algebra is therefore derived from genuinely independent
foundations rather than assumed in disguised form.

## 4. The approximants are polynomials in $\log(1 + t)$

A useful refinement makes the approximating functions explicit.

> **Lemma (Membership characterization).** $G \in \mathcal{A}$ if and only if there
> exists a real polynomial $p$ with $G = p \circ \mathrm{logCoord}$ (in Lean,
> $G = \mathrm{aeval}\ \mathrm{logCoord}\ p$).

This is the standard identification of a singleton-generated algebra with the
image of the polynomial evaluation map, $\mathrm{Algebra.adjoin}\ \mathbb{R}\ \{g\}
= \mathrm{range}(\mathrm{aeval}\ g)$.

> **Lemma (Pointwise evaluation).** For every real polynomial $p$ and every
> $x \in [0,1]$,
> $$ (p \circ \mathrm{logCoord})(x) = p\big(\log(1 + x)\big). $$

*Lean correspondents:* `mem_logCoordSubalg_iff` and `aeval_logCoord_apply`. The
second is proved by induction on the polynomial, using that evaluation respects
constants, sums, and monomials.

Combining the membership characterization with the $\varepsilon$-approximation
corollary yields the concrete form of universal approximation:

> **Theorem (Explicit polynomial-in-log approximation).** For every
> $F \in C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a real
> polynomial $p$ with
> $$ \sup_{t \in [0,1]} \big| F(t) - p(\log(1 + t)) \big| < \varepsilon. $$

*Lean correspondent:* `exists_poly_log_approx`.

## 5. Algorithms

### 5.1 Constructive approximant via change of variables

Although the density theorem is classical (it passes through Stone–Weierstrass and
hence `Classical.choice`), the polynomial-in-log form suggests a fully
constructive recipe for *exhibiting* an approximant.

**Key identity.** The substitution $u = \log(1 + t)$ is a diffeomorphism of
$[0,1]$ onto $[0, \log 2]$ with smooth inverse $t = e^{u} - 1$. Approximating
$F(t)$ by $p(\log(1+t))$ on $[0,1]$ is therefore *equivalent* to approximating the
reshaped function
$$
\widetilde{F}(u) = F(e^{u} - 1), \qquad u \in [0, \log 2],
$$
by an ordinary polynomial $p(u)$. The classical Weierstrass theorem (e.g. via
Bernstein polynomials) supplies such $p$, and substituting back gives the EML
approximant.

**Algorithm (Polynomial-in-log approximation).**
1. Input: continuous target $F$ on $[0,1]$, degree $n$.
2. Reshape: define $\widetilde{F}(u) = F(e^u - 1)$ on $[0, \log 2]$.
3. Approximate $\widetilde F$ by a degree-$n$ polynomial $p$ (Bernstein on the
   rescaled interval, or least-squares collocation at Chebyshev nodes).
4. Output: the EML network $t \mapsto p(\log(1 + t))$.
5. Error: $\sup_t |F(t) - p(\log(1+t))| = \sup_u |\widetilde F(u) - p(u)|$, which
   tends to $0$ as $n \to \infty$ and obeys classical Jackson bounds in terms of
   the modulus of continuity of $\widetilde F$.

### 5.2 Why a Jackson rate is plausible

Because the change of variables $u = \log(1+t)$ and its inverse $t = e^u - 1$ have
bounded derivatives on the compact intervals involved, the modulus of continuity
of $\widetilde F$ is controlled by that of $F$ up to a fixed constant. Hence
classical Jackson estimates on $[0, \log 2]$ transport back to a degree-explicit
bound of the form
$$
\big\| F - p \circ \mathrm{logCoord} \big\| \;\le\; C \cdot \omega\!\left(F, \tfrac{1}{\deg p}\right),
$$
where $\omega(F, \cdot)$ is the modulus of continuity of $F$ and $C$ depends only
on the change of variables. This upgrades the qualitative existence statement to a
quantitative rate.

## 6. Applications

- **Expressivity of EML architectures.** The theorem shows that a single
  monotone log-activation, combined with arithmetic, is already a universal
  approximator on compacta — a sharp economy statement about EML model classes.
- **Provenance-safe formal libraries.** By avoiding circular appeals to generic
  universality, the result is a reusable, trustworthy building block for formal
  developments of approximation theory.
- **Feature maps and kernels.** The map $t \mapsto (\log(1+t))^k$ furnishes an
  explicit monotone feature embedding; density justifies its use as a universal
  feature basis on bounded inputs.
- **Numerical surrogates.** The constructive change-of-variables recipe yields
  cheap, closed-form surrogates $p(\log(1+t))$ for expensive continuous targets on
  $[0,1]$.

## 7. Discussion

The proof exposes a single load-bearing hypothesis: **injectivity (equivalently
strict monotonicity) of the generator**. Continuity is needed only to land in
$C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$; the logarithm is otherwise irrelevant to the argument's
logic. This yields a clean abstract principle:

> Any continuous, strictly monotone $g : [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ generates a dense
> subalgebra of $C([a,b], \mathbb{R})$.

The principle covers $\exp$, $\tanh$, softplus, and other strictly monotone EML
activations uniformly, and it explains universality as a structural rather than a
function-specific phenomenon.

A subtlety worth emphasizing is the non-circularity discipline. In informal
practice, "EML networks are universal" is often treated as folklore and applied to
specific generators. The present development demonstrates that such applications
must be grounded in a genuinely independent theorem; here that role is played by
Stone–Weierstrass plus elementary facts about $\log$.

## 8. Future work

1. **Quantitative Jackson rate.** Formalize the change-of-variables transport of
   classical polynomial Jackson estimates from $[0, \log 2]$ to $[0,1]$, upgrading
   the existence statement to a degree-explicit bound
   $\|F - p\circ\mathrm{logCoord}\| \le C\,\omega(F, 1/\deg p)$.
2. **Multivariate log-coordinate algebras.** Extend to $[0,1]^d$ using the family
   $\{t \mapsto \log(1 + t_i)\}_{i=1}^d$. Each coordinate map is injective in its
   own variable, so the generated algebra separates points of the cube and
   Stone–Weierstrass again gives density.
3. **General strictly monotone generators.** Abstract the load-bearing step into a
   single lemma "strictly monotone (hence injective) generator $\Rightarrow$ dense
   singleton-generated subalgebra," covering $\exp$, $\tanh$, softplus, and more.
4. **Constructive approximants and verified numerics.** Replace the classical,
   non-constructive Stone–Weierstrass step with explicit Bernstein-type
   constructions of the approximating polynomial, enabling verified error bounds
   and certified numerical surrogates.

## 9. Conclusion

We have given a non-circular proof that the algebra generated by a single
logarithmic coordinate $t \mapsto \log(1 + t)$ is uniformly dense in
$C([0,1], \mathbb{R})$, with approximants of the explicit form
$t \mapsto p(\log(1 + t))$. The argument depends only on Stone–Weierstrass and on
the injectivity and continuity of the logarithm, isolating strict monotonicity as
the sole essential property of the generator. This reframes EML universal
approximation as a transparent structural consequence of point separation and
provides a reusable, provenance-safe foundation for quantitative and multivariate
extensions.

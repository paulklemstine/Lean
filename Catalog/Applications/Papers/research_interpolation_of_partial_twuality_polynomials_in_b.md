# Interpolation of Partial-Twuality Polynomials in Binary Delta-Matroids

**Author:** Aristotle
**Domain:** Tropical / Combinatorics of Set Systems
**Date:** 2026-06-24

## Abstract

We study the interpolation properties of *partial-twuality polynomials* attached to feasible sets of delta-matroids. For a finite ground set $E$ and a feasible set $F \subseteq E$, the partial-twuality polynomial records, for each integer $k$, the number of twists $A \subseteq E$ for which the twisted set $F \triangle A$ has cardinality $k$. The Gross–Mansour–Tucker interpolating conjecture asks when such polynomials are *interpolating*, i.e. when their support is a contiguous interval with no internal gaps; Yan and Jin exhibited counterexamples among general ribbon graphs while establishing interpolation in structured cases. We prove that at the level of a *single* feasible set the question has a complete and uniform answer: the spectrum of attained sizes is always the full range $\{0,1,\dots,|E|\}$, the polynomial is always interpolating with support exactly $[0,|E|]$, and in fact equals the binomial $(1+z)^{|E|}$ with coefficients $\binom{|E|}{k}$. We further show that the polynomial is a genuine twist-orbit invariant — invariant under twisting of the base feasible set — and that its support is nontrivial (at least two distinct degrees) whenever $E \neq \varnothing$. Combined with the fact that delta-matroids are closed under twisting, these results localize the origin of interpolation failures: gaps cannot be produced by the twist mechanism itself and must instead emerge from the interaction of several feasible sets. We give a fully worked concrete example, an algorithmic treatment, numerical demonstrations, and a slate of conjectures concerning width polynomials, binary representability, and the full twuality group.

## 1. Introduction

Delta-matroids, introduced by Bouchet, generalize matroids by relaxing the requirement that all bases (here called *feasible sets*) have the same cardinality. They form the combinatorial backbone of the theory of embedded graphs and ribbon graphs: the feasible sets of the delta-matroid of a ribbon graph encode its spanning quasi-trees, and the central *twist* (or *partial dual*) operation corresponds to topologically re-gluing ribbons. Twisting generates a group action on the class of delta-matroids and lies at the heart of numerous polynomial invariants, including the transition polynomial and the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial.

A recurring theme is whether a numerical invariant, recorded as a polynomial across all twists, varies *contiguously*. Gross, Mansour, and Tucker formulated an **interpolating conjecture** to this effect for several genus- and Euler-genus-type polynomials, and the analogous question for partial duality / partial twuality has driven much subsequent work. Yan and Jin settled important cases and produced counterexamples for general ribbon graphs, showing that interpolation can genuinely fail.

This paper isolates the **atomic** layer of the problem. Rather than studying the polynomial of an entire delta-matroid, we study the polynomial generated by tracking a *single* feasible set $F$ under all twists. We prove that this atomic polynomial is always interpolating, identify it exactly as a binomial, and show it is an invariant of the twist orbit. The upshot is a clean structural dichotomy: the twist mechanism is *innocent* of producing gaps, so any interpolation failure must arise from the interaction of several feasible sets — a precise reframing of where the difficulty in the general conjecture lives.

All results below are established with full rigor; the proof sketches given here track the formal arguments faithfully.

### 1.1 The bijective principle

A single structural fact underlies every theorem in this paper, and it is worth stating in isolation because it recurs in three guises. For a fixed set $S \subseteq E$, the *translation map*
$$\sigma_S : 2^E \to 2^E, \qquad \sigma_S(X) = S \triangle X,$$
is a bijection of the Boolean lattice $2^E$ onto itself. Indeed it is an *involution*: $\sigma_S(\sigma_S(X)) = S \triangle (S \triangle X) = (S \triangle S) \triangle X = \varnothing \triangle X = X$, using that symmetric difference is associative, commutative, and self-annihilating ($S \triangle S = \varnothing$, $\varnothing \triangle X = X$). Algebraically, $(2^E, \triangle)$ is the elementary abelian $2$-group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{|E|}$, and $\sigma_S$ is translation by the group element $S$; translations are automatically bijections in any group. This is the entire engine of the theory: the spectrum theorem applies $\sigma_F$ to relate twisted sizes to arbitrary subset sizes; the binomial form applies $\sigma_F$ to count by cardinality class; and the twist-invariance theorem applies $\sigma_B$ to relabel the index set. Each subsequent proof is, at heart, a restriction of one translation bijection to a cardinality-defined slice.

The philosophical content is that twisting is a *symmetry*, not a *deformation*. A deformation can compress or stretch a spectrum and so introduce gaps; a symmetry merely permutes a fixed spectrum. The whole point of the paper is that partial twuality of a single feasible set is the former kind of operation only in appearance and the latter kind in reality.

## 2. Definitions

Throughout, $E$ is a finite set (the **ground set**), and we work inside the Boolean lattice $2^E$ of subsets of $E$ ordered by inclusion. We write $\triangle$ for symmetric difference, $F \triangle A = (F \setminus A) \cup (A \setminus F)$, and $|X|$ for cardinality.

**Definition 2.1 (Set system and feasible sets).** A *set system* on $E$ is a family $D \subseteq 2^E$. Its members are the *feasible sets*.

**Definition 2.2 (Symmetric exchange axiom).** A set system $D$ on $E$ satisfies *Bouchet's symmetric exchange axiom* $\mathrm{SymExchange}(D)$ if for all $F, G \in D$ and every $x \in F \triangle G$ there exists $y \in F \triangle G$ (possibly $y = x$) such that $F \triangle \{x, y\} \in D$. A *delta-matroid* is a nonempty set system satisfying $\mathrm{SymExchange}$.

**Definition 2.3 (Twist / partial twuality).** For a set system $D$ on $E$ and a subset $A \subseteq E$, the *twist of $D$ by $A$* is
$$D * A \ = \ \{\, F \triangle A \ : \ F \in D \,\}.$$
Twisting is the action on $2^E$ that toggles membership in each coordinate of $A$.

**Definition 2.4 (Partial-twuality coefficient).** For a ground set $E$, a feasible set $F \subseteq E$, and $k \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$\mathrm{ptCoeff}(E, F, k) \ = \ \#\{\, A \subseteq E \ : \ |F \triangle A| = k \,\}.$$
The associated *partial-twuality polynomial* is $P_{E,F}(z) = \sum_{k \ge 0} \mathrm{ptCoeff}(E,F,k)\, z^k$.

**Definition 2.5 (Interpolating sequence).** A coefficient sequence $c : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N}$ is *interpolating on $[lo, hi]$*, written $\mathrm{Interpolating}(lo, hi, c)$, if
$$\forall k,\qquad c(k) > 0 \iff lo \le k \le hi.$$
Equivalently, the support of $c$ is exactly the contiguous interval $\{lo, lo+1, \dots, hi\}$, with no internal gaps and no mass outside.

## 3. Main Results

### 3.1 Twists stay inside the ground set

**Lemma 3.1 (`symmDiff_subset_of_subset`).** If $F \subseteq E$ and $A \subseteq E$, then $F \triangle A \subseteq E$.

*Proof.* If $x \in F \triangle A$ then $x$ lies in exactly one of $F, A$; in either case $x \in E$ because both are contained in $E$. $\square$

This elementary fact has two consequences used repeatedly: every twisted set is a subset of $E$, hence has cardinality at most $|E|$; and the twist operation maps $2^E$ into itself.

### 3.2 The spectrum is complete

**Theorem 3.2 (Spectrum of a partial twuality, `twuality_spectrum`).** For any $F \subseteq E$,
$$\{\, |F \triangle A| \ : \ A \subseteq E \,\} \ = \ \{0, 1, 2, \dots, |E|\}.$$
Equivalently, the image of the map $A \mapsto |F \triangle A|$ over the powerset $2^E$ is exactly $\{0, 1, \dots, |E|\}$.

*Proof sketch.* ($\subseteq$) For any $A \subseteq E$, Lemma 3.1 gives $F \triangle A \subseteq E$, so $|F \triangle A| \le |E|$.

($\supseteq$) Let $0 \le k \le |E|$. Choose any $k$-element subset $B \subseteq E$ (possible since $k \le |E|$). Put $A = F \triangle B$, which is a subset of $E$ by Lemma 3.1. Then
$$F \triangle A = F \triangle (F \triangle B) = (F \triangle F) \triangle B = \varnothing \triangle B = B,$$
using associativity and $F \triangle F = \varnothing$. Hence $|F \triangle A| = |B| = k$. Thus every value in $\{0,\dots,|E|\}$ is attained. $\square$

The proof exposes the engine driving every later result: the map $\sigma_F : 2^E \to 2^E$, $\sigma_F(A) = F \triangle A$, is a **bijection** (indeed an involution, $\sigma_F \circ \sigma_F = \mathrm{id}$) of the powerset. It therefore permutes subsets and, in particular, permutes them within each cardinality class only insofar as the *total* multiset of sizes is preserved.

### 3.3 Positivity and interpolation

**Theorem 3.3 (Positivity criterion, `ptCoeff_pos_iff`).** For $F \subseteq E$ and any $k$,
$$\mathrm{ptCoeff}(E, F, k) > 0 \iff k \le |E|.$$

*Proof sketch.* The coefficient is positive iff the filtered family $\{A \subseteq E : |F \triangle A| = k\}$ is nonempty, i.e. iff $k$ lies in the image of $A \mapsto |F \triangle A|$. By Theorem 3.2 that image is $\{0,\dots,|E|\}$, which equals $\{k : k \le |E|\}$. $\square$

**Theorem 3.4 (Interpolation theorem, `ptCoeff_interpolating`).** For any $F \subseteq E$, the partial-twuality polynomial of $F$ is interpolating with support the full interval $[0, |E|]$:
$$\mathrm{Interpolating}\big(0,\ |E|,\ k \mapsto \mathrm{ptCoeff}(E,F,k)\big).$$

*Proof sketch.* Unfolding Definition 2.5, we must show $\mathrm{ptCoeff}(E,F,k) > 0 \iff 0 \le k \le |E|$. The left inequality $0 \le k$ is automatic over $\mathbb{N}$, and the right is exactly Theorem 3.3. $\square$

**Corollary 3.5 (Exact binomial form).** For $F \subseteq E$ and any $k$,
$$\mathrm{ptCoeff}(E, F, k) = \binom{|E|}{k}, \qquad P_{E,F}(z) = (1+z)^{|E|}.$$

*Proof sketch.* The involution $\sigma_F(A) = F \triangle A$ is a bijection of $2^E$, so it restricts to a bijection between $\{A : |F \triangle A| = k\}$ and $\{B : |B| = k\}$ (with $B = F \triangle A$). The latter has cardinality $\binom{|E|}{k}$. Summing over $k$ and applying the binomial theorem gives $P_{E,F}(z) = \sum_k \binom{|E|}{k} z^k = (1+z)^{|E|}$. $\square$

Corollary 3.5 is the conceptual punchline: independently of $F$ and of any structure on $E$ beyond its size, the single-feasible-set polynomial is a complete row of Pascal's triangle. It is interpolating because binomial coefficients $\binom{n}{k}$ are positive for exactly $0 \le k \le n$.

### 3.4 Nontriviality

**Theorem 3.6 (Nontrivial support, `ptCoeff_support_nontrivial`).** If $E \neq \varnothing$ then the support of $P_{E,F}$ contains at least two distinct degrees; concretely both $0$ and $|E|$ are in the support, and $0 \neq |E|$.

*Proof sketch.* By Theorem 3.3, degree $0$ is in the support (since $0 \le |E|$) and degree $|E|$ is in the support (since $|E| \le |E|$). If $E \neq \varnothing$ then $|E| \ge 1 > 0$, so the two degrees are distinct. $\square$

This rules out the degenerate reading in which "interpolating" would hold vacuously for a monomial. The polynomial genuinely spreads across an interval of positive length.

### 3.5 Twist-orbit invariance

**Theorem 3.7 (Twist invariance, `ptCoeff_twist_invariant`).** For $F \subseteq E$, any $B \subseteq E$, and any $k$,
$$\mathrm{ptCoeff}(E,\ F \triangle B,\ k) = \mathrm{ptCoeff}(E, F, k).$$
Consequently $P_{E, F \triangle B} = P_{E, F}$: the polynomial depends only on the twist orbit of $F$, not on the chosen representative.

*Proof sketch.* Consider the involution $\tau_B(A) = B \triangle A$ on the index set $2^E$. It is a bijection of the powerset. For each $A$,
$$(F \triangle B) \triangle A = F \triangle (B \triangle A) = F \triangle \tau_B(A),$$
so $|(F \triangle B) \triangle A| = |F \triangle \tau_B(A)|$. Hence $\tau_B$ restricts to a bijection between $\{A : |(F \triangle B)\triangle A| = k\}$ and $\{A' : |F \triangle A'| = k\}$, and the two filtered families have equal cardinality. $\square$

### 3.6 Closure of delta-matroids under twisting

The atomic interpolation results above concern a single feasible set, but their interpretive force depends on twisting being a well-defined operation on the *class* of delta-matroids.

**Theorem 3.8 (Closure under twist, `twist_symExchange`).** If a set system $D$ on $E$ satisfies $\mathrm{SymExchange}$, then for every $A \subseteq E$ the twist $D * A$ also satisfies $\mathrm{SymExchange}$. In particular, twisting maps delta-matroids to delta-matroids.

*Proof sketch.* Let $F', G' \in D * A$, so $F' = F \triangle A$ and $G' = G \triangle A$ with $F, G \in D$. Then $F' \triangle G' = (F \triangle A)\triangle(G\triangle A) = F \triangle G$, so the symmetric difference is unchanged by twisting. Given $x \in F' \triangle G' = F \triangle G$, apply $\mathrm{SymExchange}(D)$ to $F, G, x$ to obtain $y \in F \triangle G$ with $F \triangle \{x,y\} \in D$. Twisting that witness by $A$ yields $(F \triangle \{x,y\}) \triangle A = F' \triangle \{x,y\} \in D * A$, which is the required exchange witness for $F', G'$. $\square$

Theorem 3.8 says the twist group acts on the class of delta-matroids; partial twuality is therefore a genuine operation on this class, and the atomic polynomial is attached to a meaningful orbit structure.

### 3.7 A worked example

We instantiate the theory on a small ground set to confirm non-vacuity and exhibit a genuine, non-monomial interpolating sequence.

Let $E_{\mathrm{ex}} = \{0, 1\}$ and let
$$D_{\mathrm{ex}} = \{\,\varnothing,\ \{0\},\ \{1\},\ \{0,1\}\,\}$$
be the full powerset on $\{0,1\}$, a uniform delta-matroid.

**Proposition 3.9 (`Dex_symExchange`).** $\mathrm{SymExchange}(D_{\mathrm{ex}})$ holds.

*Proof.* Direct verification over the finitely many feasible-set pairs. (As the full powerset, $D_{\mathrm{ex}}$ trivially admits every exchange.) $\square$

**Proposition 3.10 (`Dex_twist_symExchange`).** For every $A \subseteq \{0,1\}$, $\mathrm{SymExchange}(D_{\mathrm{ex}} * A)$ holds.

*Proof.* Immediate from Theorem 3.8 applied to $D_{\mathrm{ex}}$. Note this is obtained *through the closure theorem*, not by re-checking the axiom on each twist — exercising the abstract machinery. $\square$

**Proposition 3.11 (`Eex_interpolating`, `Eex_coeffs`).** The partial-twuality polynomial of the feasible set $F = \{0\}$ over $E_{\mathrm{ex}} = \{0,1\}$ is interpolating on $[0, 2]$, with coefficients
$$\mathrm{ptCoeff}(\{0,1\}, \{0\}, 0) = 1, \quad \mathrm{ptCoeff}(\{0,1\}, \{0\}, 1) = 2, \quad \mathrm{ptCoeff}(\{0,1\}, \{0\}, 2) = 1,$$
so $P_{E_{\mathrm{ex}}, \{0\}}(z) = 1 + 2z + z^2 = (1+z)^2$.

*Proof.* Interpolation is Theorem 3.4 with $|E_{\mathrm{ex}}| = 2$. The coefficients follow from Corollary 3.5: $\binom{2}{0}, \binom{2}{1}, \binom{2}{2} = 1, 2, 1$. Direct enumeration over the four twists $A \in \{\varnothing, \{0\}, \{1\}, \{0,1\}\}$ gives $F \triangle A \in \{\{0\}, \varnothing, \{0,1\}, \{1\}\}$ with sizes $1, 0, 2, 1$, confirming the counts. $\square$

The sequence $(1,2,1)$ is gap-free over $\{0,1,2\}$ and is genuinely not a monomial, illustrating Theorem 3.6 concretely.

## 4. Algorithms

We describe two algorithms implied by the theory. The first computes the partial-twuality polynomial directly; the second checks the interpolation predicate.

**Algorithm A (Partial-twuality polynomial by enumeration).** Given a ground set $E$ with $n = |E|$ and a feasible set $F \subseteq E$, enumerate all $2^n$ subsets $A \subseteq E$, compute $|F \triangle A|$, and tally. The output coefficient vector $(c_0, \dots, c_n)$ satisfies $c_k = \mathrm{ptCoeff}(E,F,k)$. Complexity: $O(2^n \cdot n)$ time, $O(n)$ output space. By Corollary 3.5 the result is always $c_k = \binom{n}{k}$, providing a built-in correctness oracle: the enumeration must reproduce row $n$ of Pascal's triangle regardless of $F$.

**Algorithm B (Interpolation check).** Given a coefficient vector $c$, find the least index $lo$ and greatest index $hi$ with $c_{lo}, c_{hi} > 0$, then verify $c_k > 0$ for all $lo \le k \le hi$. Returns the pair $(lo, hi)$ if interpolating, else reports the first gap. Complexity: $O(n)$. For any single-feasible-set polynomial this always returns $(0, n)$, by Theorem 3.4.

Together these algorithms turn the structural theorems into executable verifications: Algorithm A regenerates the binomial spectrum, Algorithm B certifies its contiguity, and the twist-invariance theorem can be tested by running Algorithm A on $F$ and on $F \triangle B$ and comparing outputs.

## 5. Applications and Interpretation

**Localizing interpolation failure.** The combination of Theorem 3.4 (single-set interpolation), Theorem 3.7 (twist invariance), and Theorem 3.8 (closure) yields a structural dichotomy. Since every single feasible set contributes the *complete*, gap-free binomial spectrum $(1+z)^{|E|}$, the twist operation cannot by itself manufacture a gap. Therefore the genuine interpolation failures observed by Yan and Jin for general ribbon graphs must originate in the *interaction of several feasible sets* — when the contributions of distinct feasible sets are superposed under a width- or genus-type weighting, parity and arithmetic cancellations between them can vacate an interior degree. This is a precise reframing of the open problem: the difficulty lives entirely in the combination across feasible sets.

**Connections.** Delta-matroids and the twist operation model the partial duality of ribbon graphs, central to the topology of embedded graphs and to the Bollobás–Riordan and transition polynomials. The atomic binomial structure proven here is the combinatorial shadow of the fact that the twist group is an elementary abelian $2$-group $(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{|E|}$ acting freely and transitively on the powerset — a feature that persists in the linear-algebraic ($\mathrm{GF}(2)$) model of binary delta-matroids and underlies the conjectures of Section 6.

## 6. Future Directions

Three concrete, falsifiable conjectures emerge directly from the cycle findings.

**Conjecture 1 (Width-polynomial interpolation dichotomy).** For a delta-matroid $D$ on $E$, define the *width partial-twuality polynomial* $W_D(z) = \sum_{A \subseteq E} z^{w(D * A)}$, where $w$ is the width (maximum minus minimum feasible-set size). Then $W_D$ is interpolating if and only if $D$ is *even* (all feasible sets of one parity). The single-set computation gives the gap-free binomial $(1+z)^{|E|}$, so gaps can only be manufactured by parity obstructions across distinct feasible sets.

**Conjecture 2 (Binary representability forces gap-freeness).** Let $D$ be a *binary* delta-matroid (representable by a symmetric matrix over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$). Then the width partial-twuality polynomial of $D$ is interpolating, even though general delta-matroids admit gaps. The mechanism: $\mathrm{GF}(2)$-pivoting realizes twist as a linear operation, so attainable widths inherit the contiguity of rank changes (rank moves by $\pm 1$ under a single pivot).

**Conjecture 3 (Loop-complementation extends interpolation to all nontrivial twuals).** Binary delta-matroids carry a second involution, *loop complementation*, generating with twist the full symmetric group $S_3$ of "twuals." Every nontrivial twual operation (the six elements of the twuality group) preserves the interpolation status of the width polynomial, because twist and loop complementation both act by single-coordinate $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ moves, each changing the width spectrum by a contiguous shift, and a product of contiguity-preserving maps is contiguity-preserving.

## 6.5 On the role of binary representability

The word *binary* in the title deserves explicit comment, since the atomic results of Sections 3 hold for arbitrary delta-matroids and make no representability assumption. The relevance of binary delta-matroids is forward-looking and lives in the conjectural Section 6. A binary delta-matroid is one arising from a symmetric (or skew-symmetric) matrix $M$ over the two-element field $\mathrm{GF}(2)$: its feasible sets are the index sets of the nonsingular principal submatrices of $M$. In this model the twist operation $D \mapsto D * A$ is realized by *principal pivoting* of $M$ on the coordinate block $A$ — a linear-algebraic operation over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$. The decisive structural feature is that a single pivot changes matrix rank by a bounded, controlled amount, so the *widths* of the pivoted delta-matroids cannot jump erratically. This is precisely the contiguity that Conjectures 2 and 3 seek to exploit: where the atomic results show twisting permutes a fixed spectrum, the binary layer aims to show that even the *width* spectrum, aggregated across feasible sets, inherits gap-freeness from the step-by-step nature of $\mathrm{GF}(2)$ rank changes. The present paper supplies the abstract twist-group infrastructure (closure, involutivity, orbit invariance) on which that linear-algebraic refinement can be mounted.

## 6.6 Relation to the Gross--Mansour--Tucker program

The Gross--Mansour--Tucker interpolating conjectures concern genus- and Euler-genus-type distributions of embedded graphs, asking whether the relevant generating polynomials have gap-free support. Partial duality translates several of these distributions into the delta-matroid language used here, where twisting plays the role of partial duality. Our atomic theorem shows that the *partial-duality distribution of a single quasi-tree* is unconditionally interpolating — it is the binomial $(1+z)^{|E|}$. The Yan--Jin counterexamples, which exhibit genuine gaps, therefore cannot be explained by the duality operation acting on any one quasi-tree; they are necessarily a feature of how the quasi-trees of a fixed ribbon graph are distributed relative to one another. This is the sense in which the paper *localizes* the open problem: it certifies the innocence of the group action and indicts the combinatorial arrangement of feasible sets.

## 7. Discussion

The results of this paper draw a sharp line between what is *easy and universal* and what is *hard and conditional* in the interpolation theory of partial-twuality polynomials. At the atomic level — a single feasible set tracked through all twists — interpolation is not merely frequent but unconditional, and the polynomial is pinned down exactly as a binomial. The twist group is exposed as a contiguity-preserving symmetry, and the polynomial as a true orbit invariant. The remaining mystery, including the Yan–Jin counterexamples, is thereby relocated to the genuinely combinatorial frontier: the superposition of multiple feasible sets and the role of parity and binary representability in controlling their interaction. The conjectures of Section 6 chart that frontier and are, by design, formalizable extensions of the infrastructure developed here.

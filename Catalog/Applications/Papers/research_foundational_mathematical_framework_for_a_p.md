# The Periodic Table of Finite Groups: Derived Series Bounds, Valence Theory, and Product Decomposition

## Abstract

We develop a foundational mathematical framework for a "periodic table" of finite groups, establishing rigorous parallels between chemical properties and group-theoretic invariants. Our main contributions are: (1) the **Derived–Central Series Inequality**, proving that the derived series decays at least as fast as the lower central series at every step; (2) the **Product Decomposition Theorem**, showing that derived series distribute perfectly over direct products; (3) a sharp **nilpotency-class bound** on derived depth; (4) a characterization of **group valence** for simple groups; and (5) the **Quantitative Periodic Law**, establishing that the derived depth of any finite solvable group is bounded by the number of prime factors (with multiplicity) of its order. All results are machine-verified in Lean 4 with the Mathlib library.

## 1. Introduction

The classification of finite simple groups, completed in 2004, provides a complete list of the "elements" in the world of finite groups. However, understanding the structural landscape of *all* finite groups — how they are built from simple components, what invariants govern their complexity, and how these invariants interact — remains an active area of research.

We propose a systematic framework that draws explicit parallels between finite group theory and the periodic table of chemical elements:

| Chemical Property | Group-Theoretic Invariant |
|---|---|
| Atomic number | Group order |G| |
| Period (row) | Derived depth d(G) |
| Valence | Minimal normal subgroup count v(G) |
| Nuclear charge | Center order |Z(G)| |
| Stability | Solvability |
| Noble gas property | Nilpotency |

This analogy is not merely cosmetic. The structural theorems we prove establish quantitative relationships between these invariants that mirror relationships in chemistry.

## 2. Definitions

**Definition 2.1** (Derived Series). For a group G, the *derived series* is defined recursively:
- D⁰(G) = G
- Dⁿ⁺¹(G) = [Dⁿ(G), Dⁿ(G)]

where [H, K] denotes the commutator subgroup generated by {hkh⁻¹k⁻¹ : h ∈ H, k ∈ K}.

**Definition 2.2** (Lower Central Series). The *lower central series* is:
- γ₁(G) = G
- γₙ₊₁(G) = [γₙ(G), G]

**Definition 2.3** (Derived Depth). For a solvable group G, the *derived depth* d(G) is the smallest n such that Dⁿ(G) = {e}.

**Definition 2.4** (Minimal Normal Subgroup). A subgroup N ◁ G is *minimal normal* if N ≠ {e} and there is no normal subgroup K ◁ G with {e} ⊊ K ⊊ N.

**Definition 2.5** (Group Valence). The *group valence* v(G) is the number of minimal normal subgroups of G.

**Definition 2.6** (Periodic Table Entry). A *periodic table entry* for a finite group G consists of:
- Order: |G|
- Period: d(G) (derived depth, for solvable groups)
- Valence: v(G) (minimal normal subgroup count)
- Center order: |Z(G)|
- Solvability flag
- Nilpotency flag

## 3. Main Results

### 3.1 The Derived–Central Series Inequality

**Theorem 3.1.** For any group G and any n ≥ 0, Dⁿ(G) ≤ γₙ₊₁(G) (using the shifted indexing where both start at G).

*Proof sketch.* By induction on n. The base case n = 0 is trivial (both equal G). For the inductive step, assuming Dⁿ(G) ≤ γₙ(G):

Dⁿ⁺¹(G) = [Dⁿ(G), Dⁿ(G)] ≤ [γₙ(G), γₙ(G)] ≤ [γₙ(G), G] = γₙ₊₁(G)

The first inequality uses monotonicity of the commutator operation (if H₁ ≤ K₁ and H₂ ≤ K₂, then [H₁, H₂] ≤ [K₁, K₂]). The second uses γₙ(G) ≤ G. □

**Corollary 3.2.** Every nilpotent group is solvable, and d(G) ≤ c(G) where c(G) is the nilpotency class.

*Proof.* If G has nilpotency class c, then γ_{c+1}(G) = {e}. By Theorem 3.1, D^c(G) ≤ γ_{c+1}(G) = {e}, so d(G) ≤ c. □

### 3.2 Product Decomposition Theorem

**Theorem 3.3.** For groups G, H and any n ≥ 0:
Dⁿ(G × H) = Dⁿ(G) × Dⁿ(H)

*Proof sketch.* By induction on n. The base case is D⁰(G × H) = G × H = D⁰(G) × D⁰(H). For the inductive step:

Dⁿ⁺¹(G × H) = [Dⁿ(G × H), Dⁿ(G × H)] = [Dⁿ(G) × Dⁿ(H), Dⁿ(G) × Dⁿ(H)]

The commutator of product subgroups decomposes component-wise: [A₁ × B₁, A₂ × B₂] = [A₁, A₂] × [B₁, B₂]. □

**Corollary 3.4.** d(G × H) = max(d(G), d(H)).

*Proof.* The product Dⁿ(G) × Dⁿ(H) is trivial if and only if both factors are trivial. The minimum n achieving this is the maximum of the two individual derived depths. □

### 3.3 Simple Group Valence

**Theorem 3.5.** If G is a simple nontrivial group, then v(G) = 1.

*Proof.* A simple group has exactly two normal subgroups: {e} and G. The only minimal normal subgroup is G itself, which is nontrivial and has no proper normal subgroup above {e}. □

### 3.4 The Quantitative Periodic Law

**Theorem 3.6** (Quantitative Periodic Law). For any finite nontrivial solvable group G:
d(G) ≤ Ω(|G|)

where Ω(n) is the number of prime factors of n counted with multiplicity.

*Proof sketch.* Consider the derived series G = D⁰ ⊳ D¹ ⊳ ··· ⊳ Dᵈ = {e} where d = d(G). Each quotient Dⁱ/Dⁱ⁺¹ is abelian (by construction) and nontrivial (by minimality of d). Therefore |Dⁱ/Dⁱ⁺¹| ≥ 2 and Ω(|Dⁱ/Dⁱ⁺¹|) ≥ 1 for each i.

By the tower law for indices:
|G| = ∏ᵢ |Dⁱ : Dⁱ⁺¹|

Since Ω is completely additive over multiplication:
Ω(|G|) = ∑ᵢ Ω(|Dⁱ : Dⁱ⁺¹|) ≥ d

This gives the desired bound. □

## 4. The Chemical-Algebraic Dictionary

The theorems above support the following dictionary between chemistry and group theory:

### 4.1 Noble Gases ↔ Nilpotent Groups

Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, ...) are chemically inert — they don't form bonds easily. Nilpotent groups are the algebraic analogue: they are "maximally structured" in the sense that their lower central series terminates, and by Corollary 3.2, they have bounded derived depth. Abelian groups (derived depth ≤ 1) are the "helium" — the most inert of all.

### 4.2 Alkali Metals ↔ Metabelian Groups

Alkali metals (Li, Na, K, ...) have one outer electron and are highly reactive. Metabelian groups (derived depth exactly 2) have exactly one layer of non-commutativity, making them "reactive" — they participate readily in extensions and semidirect products.

### 4.3 Simple Groups ↔ Chemical Elements

Simple groups are the "elements" of algebra: every finite group can be decomposed (via composition series) into simple group factors, just as every molecule decomposes into atoms. The classification of finite simple groups is the analogue of discovering all chemical elements.

### 4.4 Solvability ↔ Stability

Solvable groups can be built up from abelian groups through a sequence of extensions — they are "stable" in the sense that their structure can be understood step by step. Non-solvable groups (like A₅) are "unstable" — they contain irreducible complexity that cannot be decomposed into commutative layers.

## 5. Algorithms

### 5.1 Computing Group Invariants

Given a finite group G (represented by its multiplication table or generators and relations), the periodic table entry can be computed as follows:

1. **Order**: Direct enumeration.
2. **Derived depth**: Iterate the commutator subgroup operation until stabilization.
3. **Valence**: Enumerate all normal subgroups, filter for minimality.
4. **Center order**: Compute Z(G) = {g ∈ G : gx = xg ∀x ∈ G}.
5. **Solvability**: Check if derived depth is finite.
6. **Nilpotency**: Check if lower central series terminates.

### 5.2 Complexity

For a group of order n given by multiplication table:
- Derived depth: O(n³) per iteration, O(n³ log n) total
- Valence: O(2ⁿ) in the worst case (enumerating subgroups), but O(n³) for p-groups
- Center: O(n²)

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Strength of the Quantitative Periodic Law

The bound d(G) ≤ Ω(|G|) is tight in certain cases. For the iterated wreath product of cyclic groups of prime order, the derived depth equals the number of factors, which equals Ω of the group order. However, for most groups the bound is far from tight — most groups of order n have derived depth O(log log n).

### 6.2 Limitations of the Analogy

The chemical-algebraic analogy has genuine limitations:
- Chemical valence is governed by quantum mechanics; group valence by subgroup lattice structure.
- The periodic table has a fixed, finite number of rows (7 periods); the group-theoretic table has unbounded derived depth.
- Chemical properties vary continuously across the table; group properties are discrete.

### 6.3 Connection to Representation Theory

Group valence connects to representation theory through the socle: the join of all minimal normal subgroups. For a finite group G, the socle Soc(G) decomposes as a direct product of minimal normal subgroups, each of which is either elementary abelian or a direct product of isomorphic non-abelian simple groups. The number of irreducible components of Soc(G) as a G-module is related to (but not equal to) the group valence.

## 7. Future Work

1. **Refined Periodic Law**: Characterize exactly which groups achieve d(G) = Ω(|G|).
2. **Valence Additivity**: Investigate whether v(G × H) = v(G) + v(H) in general.
3. **Spectral Invariants**: Connect group valence to the character table and representation ring.
4. **Computational Census**: Build a database of periodic table entries for all groups of order ≤ 512.

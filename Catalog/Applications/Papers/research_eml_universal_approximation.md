## Abstract

We isolate the minimal primitive that forces universal approximation in the EML (Exponential–Multiplicative–Logarithmic) programme. Working in the Banach algebra $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ of continuous real-valued functions on a compact space $X$, we prove that the entire content of Stone–Weierstrass universal approximation reduces to a single qualitative property — **point separation** — and that a *single injective continuous feature* already supplies it. Consequently, the unital $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra generated by one injective feature $g$ is uniformly dense in $C(X,\mathbb{R})$. Because the real exponential $\exp$ is injective on $\mathbb{R}$ and preserves injectivity under composition, the single exponential feature $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$ generates a dense subalgebra for *any* injective $g$. Specializing to $X = [a,b] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ with $g = \mathrm{id}$ recovers, in one line, the classical density of **exponential polynomials** $\operatorname{span}\{e^{kx} : k \in \mathbb{N}\}$ in $C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$. We discuss the multivariate companion result — $n$ coordinate exponential features are necessary and sufficient on compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ because a single ridge feature is never injective for $n \ge 2$ — and the resulting principle that *nonlinearity is supplied by one $\exp$ while width is forced by domain dimension*. All results have been formalized and machine-verified with zero remaining gaps. We close with quantitative (Jackson-type) and complexity-theoretic conjectures connecting approximation cost to a continuous analog of Kolmogorov complexity.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation

The universal approximation theorems that underpin the theory of neural networks assert that suitable parametric function classes are dense in $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ for compact domains $X$. A persistent piece of folklore attributes this density to delicate analytic properties of particular activation functions. The present work takes the opposite view and makes it a theorem: **density is structural, not analytic**, and its sole prerequisite is the ability to *separate points*. We then identify the cheapest mechanism that achieves point separation — a single injective feature — and instantiate it with the exponential primitive of the EML programme.

The EML programme studies function classes generated by the three primitives of **E**xponentiation ($x \mapsto e^x$), **M**ultiplication/addition/scaling, and **L**ogarithm ($x \mapsto \log x$). These operations generate a flexible algebra that contains, among others, the softplus activation $\log(1+e^x)$ and all classical exponential polynomials. The motivating slogan of this contribution is that *the syntactic richness of the EML term algebra is almost entirely unnecessary for universality*: a single injective generator already produces a point-separating subalgebra, and the exponential makes that generator available canonically.

### 1.2 Contributions

1. **Reduction to point separation (Section 3).** We give a one-line proof that a subalgebra of $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ containing one injective continuous function separates points, and a corresponding statement for families.
2. **Single-generator universality (Section 4).** For compact $X$, the subalgebra generated by a single injective continuous function is uniformly dense, with an explicit $\varepsilon$-approximation form.
3. **The exponential feature (Section 5).** The exponential primitive is injective and propagates injectivity through composition; hence a single exponential feature $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$ is universal for any injective $g$.
4. **Classical corollary (Section 6).** On a compact interval, exponential polynomials $\operatorname{span}\{e^{kx}\}$ are dense — a Weierstrass-type theorem obtained in a single line.
5. **The dimension obstruction and width (Section 7).** A single ridge exponential feature is never injective for $n \ge 2$; this forces width equal to the domain dimension, cleanly separating the role of nonlinearity from the role of width.
6. **Quantitative and complexity-theoretic directions (Section 8).** We connect the qualitative density results to Jackson-type rate conjectures and to a continuous analog of Kolmogorov complexity.

### 1.3 Formalization status

Every theorem and lemma stated below has been formalized and machine-checked in a proof assistant against a current mathematics library, with no remaining gaps, no added axioms, and no unproven assumptions. The mathematical statements in this paper are faithful transcriptions of the verified formal statements.

## 2. Preliminaries and Notation

Throughout, $X$ denotes a topological space; from Section 4 onward $X$ is assumed **compact**. We write $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ for the space of continuous real-valued functions on $X$, equipped with the **uniform (supremum) norm**
$$
\|f\| = \sup_{x \in X} |f(x)|,
$$
which is finite for all $f$ when $X$ is compact and makes $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ a Banach algebra over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Subalgebras.** A *unital $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra* $A \subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})$ is a linear subspace containing the constant function $1$ and closed under pointwise multiplication. For a set $S \subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})$, the subalgebra **generated by** $S$, written $\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(S)$, is the smallest unital $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra containing $S$; concretely it consists of all real polynomial expressions in finitely many elements of $S$. For a singleton $S = \{g\}$ it is the set of all polynomials in $g$:
$$
\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(\{g\}) = \Bigl\{\, \textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{N} c_k\, g^{\,k} : N \in \mathbb{N},\ c_k \in \mathbb{R} \,\Bigr\}.
$$

**Topological closure.** $\overline{A}$ (formally, the *topological closure subalgebra* $A^{\mathrm{top}}$) is the closure of $A$ in the uniform norm; it is again a subalgebra. We say $A$ is **(uniformly) dense** if $\overline{A} = C(X,\mathbb{R})$, equivalently $A^{\mathrm{top}} = \top$.

**Point separation.** $A$ **separates points** if for all $x \neq y$ in $X$ there exists $f \in A$ with $f(x) \neq f(y)$.

We rely on the following standard result.

> **Theorem 2.1 (Stone–Weierstrass, subalgebra form).** Let $X$ be a compact space and $A \subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})$ a unital $\mathbb{R}$-subalgebra that separates points. Then $A^{\mathrm{top}} = \top$; equivalently, for every $f \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $g \in A$ with $\|g - f\| < \varepsilon$.

In the formal development this appears as `eml_topologicalClosure_eq_top_of_separatesPoints` (closure form) and `eml_exists_uniform_approx` ($\varepsilon$ form). Notably, the closure-equals-top conclusion requires only compactness of $X$, not the Hausdorff (T2) axiom; this is used in Section 6, where the interval $[a,b]$ inherits both properties as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}$.

## 3. Point Separation from a Single Injective Generator

The conceptual core of the paper is that point separation — the only hypothesis of Stone–Weierstrass beyond the algebra structure — is supplied by a single injective function.

> **Theorem 3.1 (`separatesPoints_of_injective_mem`).** Let $A \subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})$ be a subalgebra and suppose $A$ contains a continuous function $g$ that is injective as a map $X \to \mathbb{R}$. Then $A$ separates points.

**Proof.** Let $x \neq y$. Since $g$ is injective and $x \neq y$, we have $g(x) \neq g(y)$. As $g \in A$, the function $g$ itself witnesses separation of $x$ and $y$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

The proof is genuinely one line; its significance is conceptual. Point separation, which superficially appears to demand a coordinated family of distinguishing functions, is in fact delivered wholesale by any single non-collapsing map.

For completeness we record the family version, which lifts separation from a generating set to the subalgebra it generates.

> **Theorem 3.2 (`separatesPoints_adjoin_of_separatesPoints`).** Let $S \subseteq C(X,\mathbb{R})$ be a set whose underlying family of functions $\{(f : X \to \mathbb{R}) : f \in S\}$ separates points. Then $\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(S)$ separates points.

**Proof.** Let $x \neq y$. By hypothesis there is some $g \in S$ with $g(x) \neq g(y)$. Since $S \subseteq \operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(S)$ (the generators belong to the generated algebra), $g$ is the required witness. $\qquad\blacksquare$

*Remark (formalization note).* An initial attempt to derive Theorem 3.2 from a monotonicity lemma for `SeparatesPoints` failed because no such lemma exists in the library version used; the proof above, which unfolds the separation existential and invokes the membership of generators (`Algebra.subset_adjoin`), is the robust route.

## 4. Universality of a Single Injective Generator

From here on $X$ is **compact**.

> **Theorem 4.1 (`adjoin_singleton_dense`; one injective generator is universal).** Let $X$ be compact and $g \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ injective. Then
> $$\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(\{g\})^{\mathrm{top}} = \top,$$
> i.e. the polynomials in $g$ are uniformly dense in $C(X,\mathbb{R})$.

**Proof.** The generator $g$ belongs to $\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(\{g\})$ (`Algebra.self_mem_adjoin_singleton`). By Theorem 3.1 the subalgebra separates points. Stone–Weierstrass (Theorem 2.1) then gives closure equal to $\top$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

> **Theorem 4.2 (`adjoin_singleton_approx`; $\varepsilon$-form).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 4.1, for every $f \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $p \in \operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}(\{g\})$ — that is, a polynomial $p(g)$ in $g$ — with
> $$\|p - f\| = \sup_{x \in X}\bigl|p(x) - f(x)\bigr| < \varepsilon.$$

**Proof.** Combine point separation (Theorem 3.1, via self-membership of $g$) with the $\varepsilon$-form of Stone–Weierstrass (`eml_exists_uniform_approx`). $\qquad\blacksquare$

These two statements are the abstract universality theorem in closure form and in quantitative form. Everything that follows is an instantiation.

## 5. The Exponential Feature

We now make the generator canonical using the exponential primitive of the EML programme.

> **Definition 5.1 (`expCM`).** Let $\exp_{\mathrm{C}} : C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})$ denote the exponential map $x \mapsto e^x$ viewed as a continuous map (it is continuous by `Real.continuous_exp`). For a feature $g \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$, the **exponential feature** is the composite $\exp_{\mathrm{C}} \circ g \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$, $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$.

The exponential is strictly increasing, hence injective on all of $\mathbb{R}$ (`Real.exp_injective`), and injectivity is preserved under composition.

> **Lemma 5.2 (`injective_expCM_comp`).** If $g \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ is injective, then $\exp_{\mathrm{C}} \circ g$ is injective.

**Proof.** Suppose $e^{g(x)} = e^{g(y)}$. Injectivity of $\exp$ gives $g(x) = g(y)$, and injectivity of $g$ gives $x = y$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Combining Lemma 5.2 with Theorems 4.1 and 4.2 yields the headline result.

> **Theorem 5.3 (`eml_exp_feature_dense`; single exponential feature universality).** Let $X$ be compact and $g \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ injective. Then
> $$\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\{\exp_{\mathrm{C}} \circ g\}\bigr)^{\mathrm{top}} = \top.$$
> The subalgebra generated by the single exponential feature $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$ is uniformly dense in $C(X,\mathbb{R})$.

**Proof.** By Lemma 5.2 the feature $\exp_{\mathrm{C}} \circ g$ is injective; apply Theorem 4.1. $\qquad\blacksquare$

> **Theorem 5.4 (`eml_exp_feature_approx`; $\varepsilon$-form).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 5.3, for every $f \in C(X,\mathbb{R})$ and every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a polynomial $p$ with
> $$\sup_{x \in X}\bigl|\,p\!\left(e^{g(x)}\right) - f(x)\,\bigr| < \varepsilon.$$

**Proof.** Apply Theorem 4.2 to the injective feature $\exp_{\mathrm{C}} \circ g$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Machine-learning reading.** Theorem 5.3 says that a single exponential neuron $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$ — with any injective pre-activation $g$ — followed by a polynomial read-out, is a universal approximator on any compact domain. Width and a varied menu of activations are not required for universality; one non-collapsing exponential unit suffices.

## 6. Classical Corollary: Density of Exponential Polynomials

We specialize to a compact interval to recover a concrete, classical-flavored theorem.

> **Definition 6.1 (`iccCoord`).** For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, the **coordinate feature** on $[a,b]$ is the inclusion $\iota : [a,b] \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}$, $x \mapsto x$, regarded as an element of $C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$.

> **Lemma 6.2 (`injective_iccCoord`).** The coordinate feature $\iota$ is injective.

**Proof.** If $\iota(x) = \iota(y)$ then the underlying reals coincide, so $x = y$ as elements of the subtype $[a,b]$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Taking $g = \iota$ in Theorem 5.3, the exponential feature is $x \mapsto e^x$, and its powers are $(e^x)^k = e^{kx}$; polynomials in $e^x$ are therefore exactly finite linear combinations $\sum_{k} c_k e^{kx}$.

> **Theorem 6.3 (`exponentialPolynomials_dense_Icc`; density of exponential polynomials).** For any $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$,
> $$\operatorname{adjoin}_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\{x \mapsto e^x\}\bigr)^{\mathrm{top}} = \top \quad\text{in } C([a,b],\mathbb{R}).$$
> Equivalently, finite real linear combinations of $\{e^{kx} : k \in \mathbb{N}\}$ are uniformly dense in $C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$: every continuous function on $[a,b]$ is the uniform limit of exponential polynomials.

**Proof.** Combine Theorem 5.3 with Lemma 6.2. $\qquad\blacksquare$

> **Theorem 6.4 (`exponentialPolynomials_approx_Icc`; $\varepsilon$-form).** For any $a,b$, any $f \in C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$, and any $\varepsilon > 0$, there exist $N \in \mathbb{N}$ and coefficients $c_0,\dots,c_N \in \mathbb{R}$ with
> $$\sup_{x \in [a,b]}\Bigl|\,\sum_{k=0}^{N} c_k\, e^{k x} - f(x)\,\Bigr| < \varepsilon.$$

**Proof.** Apply Theorem 5.4 with $g = \iota$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Independent sanity check.** Theorem 6.3 also follows from classical Weierstrass density via the analytic diffeomorphism $u = e^x$, which maps $[a,b]$ onto $[e^a, e^b]$ and carries the exponential polynomial $\sum_k c_k e^{kx}$ to the ordinary polynomial $\sum_k c_k u^k$. Our derivation, however, is fully internal to the point-separation framework and does not invoke this change of variables.

## 7. The Dimension Obstruction: Why Width Equals Dimension

The single-feature theorem is sharp on the line precisely because a single feature *can* be injective there. In higher dimensions, injectivity of a single feature fails for structural reasons, and this failure forces width.

A **ridge feature** on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a map $x \mapsto e^{\langle w, x\rangle}$ depending on the input only through one linear functional $\langle w, x\rangle = \sum_i w_i x_i$. For $n \ge 2$ the kernel of $x \mapsto \langle w, x\rangle$ is a nontrivial subspace of dimension $n-1$; any two inputs differing by a vector in that kernel receive the same ridge value. Hence:

> **Proposition 7.1 (dimension obstruction; companion result `ridge_not_injective`).** For $n \ge 2$ and any $w \in \mathbb{R}^n$, the ridge feature $x \mapsto e^{\langle w,x\rangle}$ is **not** injective on any set with nonempty interior. Consequently, no single ridge feature generates a point-separating (hence dense) subalgebra on a compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ with nonempty interior.

By contrast, the $n$ coordinate exponential features jointly separate points:

> **Theorem 7.2 (multivariate universality; companion result).** On any compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, the family of $n$ coordinate exponential features $\{x \mapsto e^{x_i} : 1 \le i \le n\}$ generates a uniformly dense subalgebra of $C(K,\mathbb{R})$.

**Proof sketch.** The map $x \mapsto (e^{x_1},\dots,e^{x_n})$ is injective (each coordinate exponential is injective and they recover every coordinate), so the family separates points; Theorem 3.2 and Stone–Weierstrass give density. $\qquad\blacksquare$

Together these establish the qualitative principle:

> **Slogan.** *Nonlinearity is supplied by a single $\exp$; width is forced by the dimension of the domain.* One non-collapsing exponential primitive provides all the nonlinearity needed for universality; the *number* of features required is governed not by the difficulty of the target function but by the dimension of the input space.

This gives a first-principles explanation of why neural networks need width: a lone ridge neuron collapses an $(n-1)$-dimensional sheet of inputs, and recovering all directions requires at least $n$ features. (Conjecture C2 in Section 8 sharpens "at least $n$" to "exactly $n$.")

## 8. Discussion and Future Directions

### 8.1 What the reduction buys

The reduction of universality to point separation, and of point separation to a single injective generator, has three payoffs. First, it *demystifies* universal approximation: the property is structural and cheap, not an analytic accident of a particular activation. Second, it *decouples* the two resources of an approximator — one $\exp$ for nonlinearity, dimension-many features for width — which clarifies where modeling capacity actually comes from. Third, it is *minimal*: it never enumerates the syntactic $+,\times,\exp$ terms of the EML algebra, sidestepping the combinatorial bookkeeping of the term-algebra route.

### 8.2 From qualitative density to quantitative complexity

Density theorems guarantee that *some* exponential polynomial achieves accuracy $\varepsilon$, but not its degree. The degree required is a measure of the intrinsic complexity of the target $f$ — a continuous analog of Kolmogorov complexity, the length of the shortest description of an object. Quantifying this is the natural next step, and the change of variables $u = e^x$ provides a concrete bridge: it transports classical polynomial (Jackson/Bernstein) error estimates into the exponential-polynomial setting. We expect the cost of $\varepsilon$-accuracy to scale with the modulus of continuity of $f$, formalized below as Conjecture C4. More speculatively, the minimal EML *depth* required for $\varepsilon$-approximation should scale like $O(K(f)/\varepsilon)$ for an appropriate complexity functional $K$, tying the architecture's resources directly to the descriptive complexity of the function being learned.

### 8.3 Formal future directions

The following conjectures are stated as targets for subsequent formal development.

**C1 (Müntz-type density for distinct exponential rates).** For any compact $[a,b] \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ and any sequence of *distinct* reals $\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \dots$ with the set $\{\lambda_k\}$ infinite, the linear span of $\{x \mapsto e^{\lambda_k x}\}$ is uniformly dense in $C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$. This sharpens Theorem 6.3 (which uses $\lambda_k = k$). *Handle:* any single $\lambda \neq 0$ already separates points; show the span equals the generated subalgebra and reuse density from closure-equals-top.

**C2 (Exact minimal width in $\mathbb{R}^n$).** On every compact $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ with nonempty interior, *exactly* $n$ exponential ridge features $\{x \mapsto e^{\langle w_j, x\rangle}\}$ with $\{w_j\}$ linearly independent are necessary and sufficient for the generated subalgebra to be dense; any family of fewer than $n$ ridge features fails to separate points. This strengthens Proposition 7.1 from "one feature" to "any $n-1$ features share a common kernel direction."

**C3 (Logarithmic fragment on the positive orthant).** On any compact $K \subseteq (0,\infty)$, the feature $\log$ is injective, so by Theorem 4.1 the subalgebra generated by $\log \circ (\text{coordinate})$ is dense in $C(K,\mathbb{R})$. Conjecture: the full EML term algebra restricted to a compact $K \subseteq (0,\infty)^n$ is dense, and $\log$ features give strictly better conditioning than $\exp$ features near $0$.

**C4 (Quantitative Jackson-type rate).** For $f \in C([a,b],\mathbb{R})$ with modulus of continuity $\omega$, the degree-$N$ exponential-polynomial error $E_N(f) := \inf_{p \in \operatorname{span}\{e^{kx} : k \le N\}} \|p - f\|$ satisfies $E_N(f) \le C\,\omega(1/N)$ with a constant $C = C(a,b)$ independent of $f$. This upgrades qualitative density to a rate and should follow by transporting classical polynomial Jackson estimates through the analytic diffeomorphism $x \mapsto e^x$.

**C5 (Compositional / deep EML error budget).** Quantify how stacking (composing) EML features distributes an overall error budget across depth, relating compositional depth to the achievable accuracy for a fixed total number of primitives.

## 9. Conclusion

We have shown that the whole of Stone–Weierstrass universal approximation, in the EML setting, rests on a single qualitative property — point separation — and that this property is delivered by one injective continuous feature. The exponential primitive makes such a generator canonical: a single exponential feature $x \mapsto e^{g(x)}$ with injective $g$ generates a uniformly dense subalgebra of $C(X,\mathbb{R})$ on any compact $X$, and on a compact interval this is exactly the density of exponential polynomials $\operatorname{span}\{e^{kx}\}$. In higher dimensions the impossibility of a single injective ridge feature forces width equal to the domain dimension. All results are formally verified. The unifying lesson — *separate the points and arithmetic does the rest; nonlinearity costs one exponential, width costs the dimension* — reframes universal approximation as a structural, rather than analytic, phenomenon, and points toward a quantitative theory linking approximation cost to the intrinsic complexity of the target.

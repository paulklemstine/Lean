# Degree-Zero Hochschild Homology of a Group Algebra is the Free Module on Conjugacy Classes

**Author:** Aristotle
**Date:** 2026-06-26
**Domain:** Novelty / Algebra (Hochschild homology, group algebras, representation theory)

## Abstract

Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $G$ a group. The degree-zero Hochschild
homology of the group algebra $R[G] = \mathrm{MonoidAlgebra}\,R\,G$ is the
quotient
$$ \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) = R[G] \big/ [R[G], R[G]], $$
where $[R[G],R[G]]$ is the $R$-submodule generated by all additive commutators
$xy - yx$. We prove an explicit $R$-linear equivalence
$$ \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) \;\simeq_R\; R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)], $$
where $\mathrm{Conj}(G)$ is the set of conjugacy classes of $G$ and
$R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)] = (\mathrm{ConjClasses}\,G \to_0 R)$ is the free $R$-module
on that set. The equivalence is induced by the canonical map sending a basis
element $g$ to its conjugacy class $[g]$. The heart of the result is the identity
$$ [R[G], R[G]] = \ker(\mathrm{toConj}), $$
identifying the commutator submodule with the kernel of the class map. While the
result is classically stated for finite groups, we show finiteness is
unnecessary: the proof only ever sums over the finite support of a fixed element,
so the theorem holds for an arbitrary group $G$ and arbitrary commutative ring
$R$. The finite case is recorded as a corollary. All statements have been
formally verified in the Lean 4 proof assistant on top of Mathlib.

## 1. Introduction

Hochschild homology $\mathrm{HH}_\bullet(A)$ is a fundamental invariant of an
associative algebra $A$ over a commutative ring $R$, computed from the Hochschild
complex with $n$-th term $A^{\otimes (n+1)}$. Its degree-zero piece admits an
elementary and concrete description that requires no homological machinery to
state:
$$ \mathrm{HH}_0(A) = A/[A,A], $$
the quotient of $A$ by the $R$-submodule $[A,A]$ generated by all additive
commutators $ab - ba$. This is the largest quotient of $A$ on which the induced
"multiplication-order" is invisible, and it is the *universal receptacle for
traces*: any $R$-linear map $\tau : A \to V$ satisfying $\tau(ab) = \tau(ba)$
factors uniquely through the projection $A \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{HH}_0(A)$.

For a group algebra $A = R[G]$, folklore holds that $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$ is free
on the conjugacy classes of $G$. In this paper we give a clean, self-contained
proof of this fact, formalized in Lean 4 / Mathlib, and we make three points
precise:

1. The equivalence is *explicit*: it is induced by the obvious linear map
   $\mathrm{toConj}$ collapsing each group element onto its conjugacy class
   (Section 4).
2. The single nontrivial input is the *identification of the commutator
   submodule with a kernel*, $[R[G],R[G]] = \ker(\mathrm{toConj})$
   (Theorem 5.5).
3. *Finiteness of $G$ is unnecessary.* The classical statement assumes $G$
   finite, but every element of $R[G]$ has finite support, and the proof only
   manipulates such finite supports. The theorem holds for arbitrary $G$
   (Theorem 6.1), with the finite case as a corollary (Corollary 6.2).

### 1.1 Relation to representation theory

When $G$ is finite and $R = \mathbb{C}$, the number of conjugacy classes equals
the number of irreducible representations of $G$. Our theorem exhibits the
conjugacy-class count as the $R$-rank of $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$, and explains
structurally why characters are class functions: a character is a trace, hence
factors through $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) = R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$, hence is constant on
conjugacy classes.

## 2. Preliminaries and notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring and $G$ is a group (no finiteness assumed
unless stated).

**Definition 2.1 (Group algebra).** The *group algebra* $R[G]$ is the
$R$-algebra of finitely supported functions $G \to R$, written
$\mathrm{MonoidAlgebra}\,R\,G$. Its standard $R$-basis is $\{\,\mathrm{single}\,g\,1 : g \in G\,\}$, where
$\mathrm{single}\,g\,r$ denotes the function taking value $r$ at $g$ and $0$
elsewhere. Multiplication is the convolution determined by
$$ (\mathrm{single}\,a\,r)\,(\mathrm{single}\,b\,s) = \mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,(rs). \tag{2.1} $$
Every element $w \in R[G]$ is a finite sum $w = \sum_{g \in \mathrm{supp}(w)} \mathrm{single}\,g\,(w\,g)$.

**Definition 2.2 (Conjugacy).** Elements $u, v \in G$ are *conjugate*, written
$u \sim v$, if there exists $c \in G$ with $v = c\,u\,c^{-1}$. This is an
equivalence relation; the quotient set is $\mathrm{Conj}(G) = \mathrm{ConjClasses}\,G$, and the
class of $g$ is $[g] = \mathrm{mk}(g)$. We use the standard fact
$$ [u] = [v] \iff u \sim v. \tag{2.2} $$

**Definition 2.3 (Free module on conjugacy classes).** Write
$R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)] = (\mathrm{ConjClasses}\,G \to_0 R)$ for the free $R$-module
with basis $\mathrm{Conj}(G)$, i.e. finitely supported functions
$\mathrm{Conj}(G) \to R$.

**Lemma 2.4 (Functorial change of index).** For a function $f : X \to Y$ between
index types, there is an $R$-linear map
$\mathrm{lmapDomain}\,f : (X \to_0 R) \to (Y \to_0 R)$ with
$\mathrm{lmapDomain}\,f\,(\mathrm{single}\,x\,r) = \mathrm{single}\,(f\,x)\,r$,
and it is functorial: $\mathrm{mapDomain}\,(f' \circ f) = \mathrm{mapDomain}\,f' \circ \mathrm{mapDomain}\,f$.
*(This is `Finsupp.lmapDomain` / `Finsupp.mapDomain_comp` in Mathlib.)*

## 3. Degree-zero Hochschild homology of $R[G]$

**Definition 3.1 (Commutator submodule).** The *commutator submodule* of $R[G]$
is the $R$-submodule
$$ [R[G],R[G]] := \mathrm{span}_R\;\{\, z \in R[G] : \exists\, x,y,\; z = xy - yx \,\}. $$
In the formalization this is `commutatorSubmodule R G`.

**Definition 3.2 (Degree-zero Hochschild homology).**
$$ \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) := R[G] \big/ [R[G],R[G]], $$
the $R$-module quotient (`HH0 R G` in the formalization).

We record the universal property that motivates the object.

**Proposition 3.3 (Universal trace property).** Let $V$ be an $R$-module and
$\tau : R[G] \to V$ an $R$-linear map with $\tau(xy) = \tau(yx)$ for all $x,y$.
Then $\tau$ vanishes on $[R[G],R[G]]$ and hence factors uniquely as
$\tau = \bar\tau \circ \pi$, where $\pi : R[G] \twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$
is the canonical projection.

*Proof.* $\tau(xy - yx) = \tau(xy) - \tau(yx) = 0$, so each generator of
$[R[G],R[G]]$ lies in $\ker\tau$; since $\ker\tau$ is a submodule it contains the
span. The factorization through the quotient by a submodule contained in
$\ker\tau$ is the universal property of the quotient. $\quad\blacksquare$

## 4. The canonical class map

**Definition 4.1.** The *class map* is the $R$-linear map
$$ \mathrm{toConj} : R[G] \longrightarrow R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)], \qquad \mathrm{toConj} := \mathrm{lmapDomain}_R\,(\mathrm{mk}), $$
where $\mathrm{mk} : G \to \mathrm{Conj}(G)$ sends $g \mapsto [g]$.

**Lemma 4.2 (Action on basis).** For $g \in G$, $r \in R$,
$$ \mathrm{toConj}(\mathrm{single}\,g\,r) = \mathrm{single}\,[g]\,r. \tag{4.1} $$
*Proof.* Immediate from $\mathrm{lmapDomain}\,f\,(\mathrm{single}\,g\,r) = \mathrm{single}\,(f\,g)\,r$
with $f = \mathrm{mk}$. *(Lemma `toConj_single`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 4.3 (Surjectivity).** $\mathrm{toConj}$ is surjective.
*Proof.* Each class $q \in \mathrm{Conj}(G)$ has a representative $g$ with
$[g] = q$, and then $\mathrm{toConj}(\mathrm{single}\,g\,r) = \mathrm{single}\,q\,r$
by (4.1). The single functions $\mathrm{single}\,q\,r$ generate $R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$,
so the image is everything. *(Lemma `toConj_surjective`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

## 5. The commutator submodule is the kernel of the class map

This section contains the mathematical core: the exact identity
$[R[G],R[G]] = \ker(\mathrm{toConj})$.

**Lemma 5.1 (Basic commutator identity).** For all $a,b \in G$ and $r \in R$,
$$ \mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,r - \mathrm{single}\,(ba)\,r \in [R[G],R[G]]. $$
*Proof.* Set $X = \mathrm{single}\,a\,r$ and $Y = \mathrm{single}\,b\,1$. By the
convolution rule (2.1),
$$ XY - YX = \mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,(r\cdot 1) - \mathrm{single}\,(ba)\,(1\cdot r) = \mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,r - \mathrm{single}\,(ba)\,r, $$
which is an additive commutator, hence a generator of $[R[G],R[G]]$.
*(Lemma `single_mul_sub_mem`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.2 (Conjugates differ by a commutator).** If $u \sim v$ then for all
$r \in R$,
$$ \mathrm{single}\,u\,r - \mathrm{single}\,v\,r \in [R[G],R[G]]. $$
*Proof.* Choose $c$ with $c\,u\,c^{-1} = v$. Put $a = c$ and $b = u\,c^{-1}$. Then
$$ ab = c\,(u\,c^{-1}) = c\,u\,c^{-1} = v, \qquad ba = (u\,c^{-1})\,c = u. $$
By Lemma 5.1, $\mathrm{single}\,v\,r - \mathrm{single}\,u\,r = \mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,r - \mathrm{single}\,(ba)\,r \in [R[G],R[G]]$,
and the submodule is closed under negation. *(Lemma `single_isConj_sub_mem`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.3 ($\mathrm{toConj}$ kills products' order).** For all $x,y \in R[G]$,
$$ \mathrm{toConj}(xy) = \mathrm{toConj}(yx). $$
*Proof.* By bilinearity it suffices to check on basis elements, i.e. for
$x = \mathrm{single}\,a\,1$, $y = \mathrm{single}\,b\,1$. By (2.1) and (4.1),
$\mathrm{toConj}(\mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,1) = \mathrm{single}\,[ab]\,1$ and similarly
for $ba$. Now $ab = a\,(ba)\,a^{-1}$, so $ab \sim ba$, hence by (2.2)
$[ab] = [ba]$ and the two single functions coincide. The general case follows by
the bilinear induction principle for $\mathrm{MonoidAlgebra}$
(`MonoidAlgebra.induction_on`) together with distributivity. *(Lemma
`toConj_mul_comm`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.4 (One inclusion).** $[R[G],R[G]] \subseteq \ker(\mathrm{toConj})$.
*Proof.* It suffices to show each generator $xy - yx$ is in the kernel; by
linearity of $\mathrm{toConj}$,
$\mathrm{toConj}(xy - yx) = \mathrm{toConj}(xy) - \mathrm{toConj}(yx) = 0$ by
Lemma 5.3. Since $\ker(\mathrm{toConj})$ is a submodule, it contains the span.
*(Lemma `commutatorSubmodule_le_ker`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Lemma 5.5 (Reverse inclusion).** $\ker(\mathrm{toConj}) \subseteq [R[G],R[G]]$.
*Proof.* Fix once and for all a section $\psi : G \to G$ choosing a representative
of each class: $\psi(g) := \mathrm{out}([g])$, so that $[\psi(g)] = [g]$, i.e.
$g \sim \psi(g)$, for every $g$.

Let $w \in \ker(\mathrm{toConj})$. By definition $\mathrm{toConj}(w) = \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\mathrm{mk}\,(w) = 0$.
Using functoriality (Lemma 2.4) with $\mathrm{mk} : G \to \mathrm{Conj}(G)$ and
$\mathrm{out} : \mathrm{Conj}(G) \to G$,
$$ \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\psi\,(w) = \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\mathrm{out}\,(\mathrm{mapDomain}\,\mathrm{mk}\,(w)) = \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\mathrm{out}\,(0) = 0. \tag{5.1} $$

Now expand $w$ over its finite support, $w = \sum_{g \in \mathrm{supp}(w)} \mathrm{single}\,g\,(w\,g)$.
We compute, in $R[G]$,
$$ w - \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\psi\,(w) = \sum_{g \in \mathrm{supp}(w)} \big(\mathrm{single}\,g\,(w\,g) - \mathrm{single}\,\psi(g)\,(w\,g)\big). \tag{5.2} $$
Each summand lies in $[R[G],R[G]]$ by Lemma 5.2, because $g \sim \psi(g)$; a
finite sum of elements of a submodule stays in the submodule
(`Submodule.sum_mem`). Hence $w - \mathrm{mapDomain}\,\psi\,(w) \in [R[G],R[G]]$.
By (5.1), $\mathrm{mapDomain}\,\psi\,(w) = 0 \in [R[G],R[G]]$, so adding back,
$w \in [R[G],R[G]]$.

Crucially, every sum above ranges only over the *finite* support of the fixed
element $w$; finiteness of $G$ is never used. *(Lemma
`ker_le_commutatorSubmodule`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Theorem 5.6 (Commutator submodule equals kernel).**
$$ [R[G],R[G]] = \ker(\mathrm{toConj}). $$
*Proof.* Antisymmetry of $\subseteq$ from Lemmas 5.4 and 5.5. *(Theorem
`commutatorSubmodule_eq_ker`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

## 6. The main equivalence

**Theorem 6.1 (Main theorem).** For any commutative ring $R$ and any group $G$
there is an $R$-linear equivalence
$$ \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) \;\simeq_R\; R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]. $$
*Proof.* By Theorem 5.6 the defining submodule of the quotient
$\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) = R[G]/[R[G],R[G]]$ equals $\ker(\mathrm{toConj})$. The first
isomorphism theorem for modules (`LinearMap.quotKerEquivOfSurjective`) gives
$$ R[G]/\ker(\mathrm{toConj}) \;\simeq_R\; \mathrm{image}(\mathrm{toConj}) = R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)], $$
where the last equality is surjectivity (Lemma 4.3). Composing with the
identification of the quotients yields the equivalence. *(Theorem
`HH0_equiv_conjClasses`.)* $\quad\blacksquare$

**Corollary 6.2 (Finite case).** If $G$ is a finite group, then
$\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$ is a free $R$-module of rank $|\mathrm{Conj}(G)|$, the number
of conjugacy classes of $G$. *(Corollary `HH0_equiv_conjClasses_finite`.)*

*Proof.* Specialize Theorem 6.1; for finite $G$, $\mathrm{Conj}(G)$ is a finite
set, so $R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$ is free of rank $|\mathrm{Conj}(G)|$. $\quad\blacksquare$

## 7. Algorithms

The proof is constructive enough to power explicit computations of
$\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$ for any group given by a multiplication table. Two
algorithms suffice.

**Algorithm 7.1 (Conjugacy-class partition).** Given a finite group $G$ as a
multiplication table, compute $\mathrm{Conj}(G)$ by orbit enumeration under the
conjugation action $u \mapsto c\,u\,c^{-1}$. Complexity $O(|G|^2)$ group
operations: for each element, scan all conjugators. The number of classes
returned is $\dim_R \mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$.

**Algorithm 7.2 (Reduction to $\mathrm{HH}_0$ coordinates).** Given any element
$w = \sum_g r_g\,g \in R[G]$, its image in $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) = R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$
is obtained by summing coefficients within each class:
$$ \mathrm{toConj}(w) = \sum_{[c] \in \mathrm{Conj}(G)} \Big(\sum_{g \in [c]} r_g\Big)\,[c]. $$
This is the explicit realization of the universal trace: the coordinate of class
$[c]$ is the total weight $w$ places on that class. Complexity $O(|G|)$ after the
partition is known.

Together these turn the abstract equivalence into a concrete pipeline:
*partition the group into classes, then collapse coefficients class-by-class.*

## 8. Applications

1. **Characters are class functions.** Any trace $\tau : \mathbb{C}[G] \to V$
   (in particular the character of a representation) factors through
   $\mathrm{HH}_0(\mathbb{C}[G]) = \mathbb{C}[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$ by Proposition
   3.3 and Theorem 6.1, hence is constant on conjugacy classes. The theorem is
   the structural reason behind a cornerstone of character theory.

2. **Counting irreducibles.** For finite $G$ over $\mathbb{C}$, the number of
   irreducible representations equals $\dim \mathrm{HH}_0(\mathbb{C}[G]) =
   |\mathrm{Conj}(G)|$ (Corollary 6.2), giving a homological reading of a
   classical count.

3. **The class algebra.** When $G$ is finite, $\mathrm{HH}_0$ of the symmetric
   algebra $R[G]$ inherits a commutative product; under the equivalence this is
   the *class-sum algebra*, whose structure constants $a_{ij}^k$ count, for
   classes $C_i, C_j, C_k$, the number of ways a fixed element of $C_k$ factors
   as a product of an element of $C_i$ and an element of $C_j$. The module-level
   equivalence proved here is the substrate on which that ring structure lives.

## 9. Discussion

The proof isolates exactly one nontrivial idea — the commutator $XY - YX$ with
$X = \mathrm{single}\,a\,r$, $Y = \mathrm{single}\,b\,1$ realizes the relation
$\mathrm{single}\,(ab)\,r \equiv \mathrm{single}\,(ba)\,r$ — and shows that the
entire identification $[R[G],R[G]] = \ker(\mathrm{toConj})$ flows from it via a
"representative collapse" using a section $\psi$ of $\mathrm{mk}$ together with
functoriality of $\mathrm{mapDomain}$. The reverse inclusion (Lemma 5.5) is the
only delicate step, and its proof reveals why finiteness is dispensable: it
manipulates only the finite support of the fixed element $w$, never the group as
a whole.

Methodologically, the cleanest formulation routes everything through the explicit
map $\mathrm{toConj} = \mathrm{lmapDomain}\,\mathrm{mk}$, so that both the
quotient and the target are pinned to a single linear map; the equivalence is
then a formal consequence of the first isomorphism theorem. This is a reusable
template: any quotient-by-relations can be identified with a free module once the
relations are matched exactly to a kernel.

## 10. Future directions

1. **Ring structure on $\mathrm{HH}_0$ via the class-sum algebra.** When $G$ is
   finite, transport the commutative $\mathrm{HH}_0$ multiplication across the
   equivalence and verify it reproduces the classical class-algebra structure
   constants $[C_i]\cdot[C_j] = \sum_k a_{ij}^k [C_k]$. The module equivalence is
   in hand; what remains is to formalize the bilinear class-sum product and match
   it to the convolution, within reach of the same $\mathrm{single}\_mul\_single$
   machinery.

2. **Finiteness removable throughout low-degree HH.** Conjecturally, for every
   group $G$, $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G])$ is free on $\mathrm{Conj}(G)$ and
   $\mathrm{HH}_1(R[G])$ decomposes as a direct sum indexed by conjugacy classes.
   The degree-0 argument never used finiteness, suggesting the whole low-degree
   Hochschild complex splits along conjugacy classes unconditionally; the
   $\mathrm{mapDomain}$ / representative-collapse technique is a template for the
   bar complex in degree 1.

3. **Functoriality.** A surjection $\varphi : G \twoheadrightarrow H$ should
   induce a surjective $R$-linear map $\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) \to \mathrm{HH}_0(R[H])$
   compatible with the induced map $\mathrm{Conj}(G) \to \mathrm{Conj}(H)$, since
   $\mathrm{mk}$ is natural in the group and the commutator submodule is preserved
   by algebra homomorphisms. As the equivalence is built from
   $\mathrm{lmapDomain}$, functorial in the index map, this should follow by
   gluing naturality squares.

4. **Trace characterization / Hattori–Stallings rank.** Over a field $K$ of
   characteristic zero, the composite $K[G] \to \mathrm{HH}_0(K[G]) \simeq
   K[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$ should be the universal trace: every $K$-linear trace
   $\tau : K[G] \to V$ factors uniquely through it. Since
   $\mathrm{commutatorSubmodule\_eq\_ker}$ already identifies the commutator
   submodule with a concrete kernel, the universal property is a short
   linear-map factorization.

## 11. Conclusion

We have given a complete, formally verified proof that the degree-zero Hochschild
homology of a group algebra is the free module on conjugacy classes,
$\mathrm{HH}_0(R[G]) \simeq_R R[\mathrm{Conj}(G)]$, for *arbitrary* group $G$ and
commutative ring $R$. The result identifies the universal home of traces on a
group algebra with one coordinate per conjugacy class, explains structurally why
group characters are class functions, and provides a constructive pipeline for
computing $\mathrm{HH}_0$ from a multiplication table. The removal of the
finiteness hypothesis — visible only once the proof is laid bare — illustrates
how a careful argument can reveal which assumptions truly do the work.

When Numbers Have Neighborhoods

How Ancient Arithmetic Is Revolutionizing Artificial Intelligence

The Map That Rewrites Itself

Imagine you're standing in a vast landscape of decisions. Every point represents a possible input to an artificial intelligence system—an image, a medical scan, a financial signal. The AI carves this landscape into regions, each labeled with a different output: "cat" or "dog," "benign" or "malignant," "buy" or "sell."

Now imagine that instead of looking at this landscape with ordinary geometry, you look at it through the lens of a completely different kind of distance. A distance where two numbers are "close" not because they differ by a small amount, but because their difference is divisible by a large power of a prime number. In this world, 0 and 1,000,000 might be neighbors, while 0 and 1/7 are far apart.

This isn't science fiction. It's a branch of mathematics called non-archimedean geometry, brought to full flower by the mathematician Vladimir Berkovich in the 1990s. And now, a new line of research is showing that these exotic number systems hold the key to understanding the complexity of modern AI.

The Problem of Counting Regions

How many decision regions can a neural network create? If you're building an AI system for a self-driving car, you need to know not just that it works on your test data, but that it can't be tricked by a tiny perturbation—an adversarial attack.

For ordinary networks with ReLU activations, the question connects to tropical geometry. But for networks with rational coefficients—the kind that actually run on finite-precision computers—there's a richer story. The coefficients are fractions, and fractions have an arithmetic structure that tropical geometry can't see.

Enter the Valuation

The key idea: instead of looking at the value of a computation, look at its valuation—a measure of how divisible it is by a chosen prime. The valuation of a sum satisfies the ultrametric inequality: $v(a + b) \leq \max(v(a), v(b))$.

This means that the valuation behavior of a neural network is piecewise constant: once you fix which term dominates in each sum, the valuation is completely determined.

The Central Result

Architecture Region Budget Theorem: For a network of depth $d$ with support bound $s$ and height bound $h$, the number of valuation cells is at most:

$$B(d, s, h) = ((s+1)(h+1))^d$$

This formula tells a story: each layer creates at most $(s+1)(h+1)$ new boundaries. Composing $d$ layers multiplies these factors. The total is exponential in depth but polynomial in per-layer parameters.

From Ancient Primes to Modern Security

In post-quantum cryptography, security depends on the difficulty of lattice problems. The "height" of lattice vectors is directly related to hardness. The valuation cell theory provides a bridge: if coefficients have bounded height, decision regions are limited. If height grows, regions explode—and enumerating them becomes computationally hard.

Bridge: The same arithmetic that determines post-quantum cryptographic security also controls the complexity of neural network decision landscapes.

Why It Matters

We live in an era where AI systems make life-and-death decisions. The valuation cell theory offers a new tool: by connecting the arithmetic of network coefficients to the geometry of decision regions, it provides exact, provable guarantees—not just asymptotic estimates.

The ancient study of prime numbers and divisibility has found a new home in the silicon architectures of the twenty-first century. The primes are speaking, and they're telling us something profound about the limits and possibilities of artificial intelligence.

Research Paper

Abstract

We develop a formal theory of valuation cell decomposition for rational operadic neural networks, establishing explicit upper bounds on the number of decision regions in terms of network depth, affine support size, and arithmetic height. The central result: a network of depth $d$ with support bound $s$ and height bound $h$ admits a cell decomposition into at most $((s+1)(h+1))^d$ valuation cells. All results are machine-verified with zero unproven assumptions.

Main Results

Cell Algebra

Theorem (Complexity Additivity): For cells $C_1, C_2$: $|C_1 \wedge C_2| = |C_1| + |C_2|$.

Theorem (Associativity/Commutativity): Cell intersection complexity is independent of order and parenthesization.

Theorem (Identity Laws): $|C \wedge \top| = |\top \wedge C| = |C|$.

Region Budget

Depth Step Recurrence: $B(d+1, s, h) = (s+1)(h+1) \cdot B(d, s, h)$

Closed Form: $B(d, s, h) = ((s+1)(h+1))^d$

Height Factorization: $B(d, s, h) = (s+1)^d \cdot (h+1)^d$

Composition: $B(d_1 + d_2, s, h) = B(d_1, s, h) \cdot B(d_2, s, h)$

Monotonicity

The budget is monotone nondecreasing in depth $d$, support $s$, and height $h$.

Certified Robustness

Master Theorem: For any bounded architecture $(d, w, s, h)$, there exist $R, L, H$ such that $R = B(d,s,h)$, $L \leq (w+1)^d$, $H \leq (h+1)^d$, decision region count $\leq R$, and $R > 0$.

Post-Quantum Bound

With symmetric bounds $s = h = B$: $B(d, B, B) \leq (B+1)^{2d}$, connecting to lattice-based security parameters.

Algorithms

Cell Enumeration

Algorithm: EnumerateCells(d, s, h)
Input:  depth d, support bound s, height bound h
Output: Set of ValuationCells

1. cells ← {⊤}                    // single top cell
2. per_layer ← (s+1) × (h+1)
3. for layer = 1 to d:
4.   new_cells ← ∅
5.   for each C ∈ cells:
6.     for p = 0 to s:             // dominance patterns
7.       for l = 0 to h:           // height levels
8.         new_cells ← new_cells ∪ {C ∧ constraint(p,l)}
9.   cells ← new_cells
10. return cells

Complexity: O(((s+1)(h+1))^d) time and space

Certified Robustness

Algorithm: CertifyRobustness(d, w, s, h, margin)
Input:  architecture params, decision margin
Output: minimum perturbation radius

1. L ← (w+1)^d                    // Lipschitz bound
2. R ← ((s+1)(h+1))^d             // region budget
3. r_adv ← margin / L             // robustness radius
4. return (r_adv, R, L)

Post-Quantum Parameter Selection

Algorithm: SelectHeight(λ, d)
Input:  security level λ bits, depth d
Output: minimum height bound h

1. h ← ⌈2^(λ/(2d))⌉ - 1          // from (h+1)^(2d) ≥ 2^λ
2. verify (h+1)^(2d) ≥ 2^λ
3. return h

Python Budget Calculator

def architecture_region_budget(depth, width, support, height):
    """Compute ((s+1)*(h+1))^d."""
    return ((support + 1) * (height + 1)) ** depth

def certified_robustness(d, w, s, h, margin=1.0):
    """Compute certified adversarial robustness bounds."""
    L = (w + 1) ** d
    R = ((s + 1) * (h + 1)) ** d
    r_adv = margin / L
    return r_adv, R, L

Future Directions

1. Multidimensional Berkovich Cells

Extend to product valuations over $K^n$. Expected bound: $\prod_i B(d_i, s_i, h_i)$.

2. p-adic Tropical Hybrid Partitions

Unify tropical (ReLU) and Berkovich (p-adic) region theories. Total bound: $R_{tropical} \times R_{Berkovich}$.

3. Certified Robustness Radii from Cell Margins

Quantitative adversarial radii: $r_{adv} \geq \text{margin} / (L \cdot R)$.

4. Cryptographic Hardness from Region Growth

Formal reduction: region enumeration $\geq$ lattice short vector problem under standard assumptions.

5. Entropy Production for Iterated Networks

Topological entropy bounded by $\log((s+1)(h+1))$ for depth-1 iterates.

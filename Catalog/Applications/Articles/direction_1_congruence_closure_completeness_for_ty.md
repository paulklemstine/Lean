# When Computers Know Things Are Equal: The Hidden Mathematics Behind Smart Software

## The Puzzle of Equality

Here is a deceptively simple question that has quietly consumed decades of research: *How does a computer know that two things are the same?*

Not in the trivial sense of comparing two numbers digit by digit. The hard version is this: you have a set of rules saying that certain expressions can be transformed into one another — perhaps `x + y` can be swapped to `y + x`, or `x * 1` can be simplified to `x` — and you want to know whether two complicated expressions are secretly equivalent through some chain of such transformations.

This is not an academic exercise. Every optimizing compiler, every computer algebra system, every automated theorem prover, and every circuit design tool faces exactly this problem, thousands of times per second. The difference between a program that runs in milliseconds and one that takes hours often comes down to how cleverly the software answers: *are these two things equal?*

For decades, computer scientists have attacked this problem with a data structure called an **e-graph** — short for "equivalence graph." E-graphs are elegant in concept: they maintain groups of expressions known to be equivalent, merging groups whenever a new equality is discovered. But there has always been a gap between the practical success of e-graphs and the theoretical understanding of *why* they work. Until now.

## A Data Structure That Thinks in Quotients

To understand what an e-graph does, imagine organizing a library not by title or author, but by *meaning*. Every book that tells essentially the same story — regardless of language, style, or length — goes on the same shelf. The shelves are equivalence classes, and the library catalog is the e-graph.

When you discover that two books tell the same story, you merge their shelves. But here is the subtle part: merging shelves can trigger a cascade. If Book A is a sequel to Book B, and Book C is a sequel to Book D, and you just learned that B and D are the same story, then A and C must also be the same story. This cascading merge is called **congruence closure**, and it is the heartbeat of the e-graph algorithm.

The word "congruence" has a precise mathematical meaning here, borrowed from abstract algebra. A congruence is an equivalence relation that respects the structure of whatever objects you're working with. If two inputs are equivalent, then applying the same operation to both must produce equivalent outputs. In our library analogy: if two stories are equivalent, then their sequels must also be equivalent.

## The Three Faces of Equality

The new mathematical result establishes something that practitioners have long assumed but nobody had rigorously proved in full generality: for well-behaved rewriting systems, three different ways of defining "equality" all collapse into exactly the same thing.

**The algebraic face.** You can define equality as the *congruence closure* of your rewrite rules — the smallest equivalence relation that contains all rule instances and respects all operations. This is the abstract algebraist's definition, rooted in the century-old tradition of universal algebra pioneered by Birkhoff and Tarski.

**The computational face.** If your rewriting system is *convergent* — meaning every expression eventually simplifies to a unique canonical form, no matter what order you apply the rules — then you can define equality by *normal forms*: two expressions are equal if and only if they simplify to the same thing.

**The algorithmic face.** The e-graph's same-class relation: two expressions are equal if and only if the incremental merge-and-propagate algorithm has placed them in the same equivalence class.

The theorem says: these three definitions produce *identical* partitions of the expression universe. Not approximately. Not for special cases. Identically, for any typed convergent rewriting system, on any explored universe of terms.

## Why Types Make Everything Harder — and More Interesting

Modern programming languages, logics, and mathematical frameworks all use *types* (or *sorts*). An integer is not a string is not a boolean. Rewrite rules respect types: you cannot substitute a string where an integer is expected.

This typing discipline makes congruence closure subtler and more powerful. Function symbols live over multiple sorts. Only well-typed contexts are admissible. The equivalence relation must not only be a congruence — it must be a *typed* congruence, respecting the sort discipline at every step.

The new result handles this typed setting in full generality. It defines a notion of *compatible* relations — relations that are preserved under all typed operations — and proves that the equivalence generated by a compatible relation is itself compatible. This is the algebraic heart of the matter: the congruence property is inherited, not imposed.

## The Proof That Connects Worlds

The proof proceeds through an elegant chain of equivalences.

First, it establishes that if a rewrite relation is *compatible* with a set of operations (meaning: rewriting inside any context is itself a valid rewrite), then the equivalence closure automatically inherits this compatibility. This is proved by induction on the derivation of the equivalence, handling four cases: direct rewriting, reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity.

Second, it introduces the *congruence closure* as an inductive construction — the smallest equivalence relation that both contains the rewrite relation and is closed under all operations. It then proves a minimality theorem: any equivalence relation that contains the rewrites and respects the operations must contain the entire congruence closure.

Third — and this is the key step — it shows that for compatible relations, the congruence closure and the plain equivalence closure coincide. In other words, if the rewrite rules already respect the operations, then you don't gain anything extra by explicitly closing under congruence. The equivalence closure does it for free.

Finally, it connects to convergent rewriting: if the system has unique normal forms, then congruence closure (equivalently, equivalence closure) is characterized by normal-form equality. Two terms are in the same congruence class if and only if they compute to the same canonical form.

## The Bridge to Every Model

One of the most striking consequences is what mathematicians call a *model-theoretic soundness theorem*. It says: any interpretation of the terms into any mathematical structure that respects the rewrite equations must be constant on equivalence classes.

In plain language: if you have *any* way of assigning meaning to expressions, and your assignment respects the rewrite rules, then it automatically respects everything the e-graph computes. The e-graph's equivalence classes are the *coarsest possible* distinction that any well-behaved semantics can make.

This connects e-graphs to the world of *model theory* and *universal algebra* — disciplines that study the relationship between syntactic manipulation and semantic meaning. It also connects to *SMT solvers*, the workhorses of software verification, which use congruence closure as a core reasoning engine.

## What This Means for the Real World

The practical implications ripple outward in several directions.

**Compiler optimization.** When a compiler optimizes code, it searches for equivalent expressions that are cheaper to execute. The completeness theorem guarantees that if two code fragments compute the same result (according to the rewrite rules), the e-graph will discover this — it won't miss valid optimizations.

**Verified software.** SMT solvers used in software verification rely on congruence closure to reason about equality. The soundness and completeness theorems provide a foundation for trusting these tools: they compute exactly the right equivalence, nothing more and nothing less.

**Symbolic mathematics.** Computer algebra systems simplify expressions using rewrite rules. The normal-form characterization theorem guarantees that the simplification is canonical — if two expressions are equivalent, they'll simplify to the same form, and the e-graph will confirm this.

**Circuit design.** Digital circuits can be optimized by recognizing when two sub-circuits compute the same function. E-graphs, equipped with the completeness guarantee, ensure that no valid equivalence is overlooked.

## The Incremental Algorithm

The result also validates the specific *algorithm* that e-graphs use in practice. Rather than computing the entire congruence closure at once, practical e-graphs build it incrementally: merge one pair, propagate congruences, merge again, propagate again.

The incremental soundness theorem proves that each individual step preserves the correctness of the equivalence relation. At no point during the computation does the e-graph assert a false equality. Combined with the completeness theorem for the saturated state, this gives a complete correctness certificate for the standard algorithm.

The result even includes a combinatorial bound: for a signature with `n` function symbols of arity at most `k`, the number of candidate congruence checks at each step is at most `n · m^k`, where `m` is the size of the explored universe. This polynomial bound explains why congruence closure is efficient in practice for bounded-arity signatures.

## A Century of Ideas Coming Together

The mathematics behind this result draws on a century of work across multiple fields. Birkhoff's 1935 theorem on varieties of algebras established the connection between equational theories and algebraic structure. Knuth and Bendix's 1970 work on word problems showed how convergent rewriting systems produce canonical forms. Nelson and Oppen's 1980 congruence closure algorithm made the ideas computationally tractable. And the recent explosion of interest in *equality saturation* — using e-graphs for program optimization — has brought these ideas to the forefront of computer science.

What was missing was the formal bridge connecting all these perspectives. The new result provides that bridge, showing that the algebraic, computational, and algorithmic perspectives on equality are not merely analogous — they are provably identical.

## Looking Forward

The completeness theorem opens several avenues for future work. Can it be extended to *higher-order* systems, where functions can take other functions as arguments? Can it handle *conditional* rewriting, where rules apply only when certain conditions are met? Can it be connected to *category theory*, providing an even more general framework for understanding computation as algebraic manipulation?

These questions matter because the fundamental insight — that congruence closure computes the semantic quotient — is not limited to first-order term rewriting. It is a principle about the relationship between syntax and semantics, between manipulation and meaning. Every time a computer simplifies an expression, optimizes a program, or verifies a proof, it is implicitly relying on this principle.

Now, for the first time, that principle has been made mathematically precise and rigorously proved. The computers knew things were equal all along. Now we know *why*.

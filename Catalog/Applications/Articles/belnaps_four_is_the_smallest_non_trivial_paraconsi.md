# The Infinite Family Tree of Right Triangles

## A Single Triangle That Spawned Them All

Take the humble 3-4-5 right triangle—the one every carpenter knows, the one stamped into every speed square at the hardware store. Three, four, five. The sides are whole numbers, the angle is exactly ninety degrees, and the numbers share no common factor. It is the smallest, most elemental Pythagorean triple.

Now imagine placing that triangle at the root of a tree—not a tree of wood and leaves, but a mathematical family tree that branches endlessly downward, three children at every node. Each child is itself a perfect right triangle with whole-number sides that share no common factor. And here is the astonishing part: every such triangle in existence appears exactly once in this tree. Miss none, repeat none, forever.

This is the *Berggren tree*, first described by the Swedish mathematician B. Berggren in 1934. It is an infinite ternary tree whose nodes are the primitive Pythagorean triples—those integer right triangles whose side lengths have no common divisor. The tree is generated by three simple matrix operations (the "left," "middle," and "right" steps) applied to the vector (a, b, c) representing each triangle. Apply any sequence of these three moves starting from (3, 4, 5), and you will land on a unique primitive triple. Apply every possible sequence, and you will visit them all.

The work presented here establishes, with machine-checked mathematical certainty, that these three matrix operations truly preserve every property that makes a Pythagorean triple "primitive"—and that the tree always grows, never doubling back on itself.

## The Ancient Equation

Pythagoras's theorem is perhaps the most famous equation in mathematics: *a² + b² = c²*. A Pythagorean triple is a solution in positive integers. The triple (3, 4, 5) is the most familiar—9 + 16 = 25—but there are infinitely many others: (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17), (7, 24, 25), and so on, stretching without bound.

Some of these are merely scaled copies of others. The triple (6, 8, 10) satisfies the equation (36 + 64 = 100), but it is just (3, 4, 5) multiplied by 2. To avoid redundancy, mathematicians focus on *primitive* triples: those where the two legs share no common divisor. If you know all the primitive triples, you know all triples—just multiply by every positive integer.

But how many primitive triples are there, and how do you find them all? The answer has been known for centuries: there are infinitely many, parameterized by pairs of coprime integers of opposite parity. What Berggren discovered is something more elegant: a recursive structure, a family tree, that organizes all of them into a single branching pattern rooted at the simplest example.

## What Makes a Triple Primitive?

A primitive Pythagorean triple (a, b, c) must satisfy five conditions simultaneously:

1. **The Pythagorean equation**: a² + b² = c².
2. **Positivity**: all three numbers are positive.
3. **Coprimality**: the greatest common divisor of the two legs is 1.
4. **Parity**: one leg is odd and the other is even.
5. **Orientation**: by convention, we fix *a* to be the odd leg.

These constraints are remarkably fragile. The Pythagorean equation is a quadratic form—it lives in the world of algebra and geometry. Coprimality is number-theoretic: it says something about the prime factorization of *a* and *b*. The parity condition sits at the intersection of modular arithmetic and algebra. That all five properties can be preserved simultaneously by a linear operation is, at first encounter, surprising.

## Three Matrices, Three Children

The key to the Berggren tree is three 3×3 matrices with integer entries. Applied to the vector (a, b, c), each matrix produces a new vector (a', b', c') through simple arithmetic:

**Left step**: (a', b', c') = (a − 2b + 2c, 2a − b + 2c, 2a − 2b + 3c).

**Middle step**: (a', b', c') = (a + 2b + 2c, 2a + b + 2c, 2a + 2b + 3c).

**Right step**: (a', b', c') = (−a + 2b + 2c, −2a + b + 2c, −2a + 2b + 3c).

Start from (3, 4, 5). The left step produces (5, 12, 13)—the next simplest Pythagorean triple. The middle step gives (21, 20, 29). The right step yields (15, 8, 17). Each of these is a genuine primitive Pythagorean triple, and each has three children of its own. The tree unfurls: (5, 12, 13) begets (7, 24, 25), (55, 48, 73), and (45, 28, 53); and on it goes, each triple finding its unique place in the infinite genealogy.

The matrices look arbitrary at first, but they are not. They are the unique (up to sign and permutation) unimodular matrices that preserve the quadratic form a² + b² = c² while keeping all entries positive and coprime. They arise naturally from the automorphisms of the Lorentzian form x² + y² − z² = 0 over the integers.

## What Was Verified—and Why It Matters

The verified results form a complete certificate that the Berggren tree is well-defined: every step from a primitive triple produces another primitive triple. Concretely, five families of properties were demonstrated for each of the three matrix operations:

**1. Preservation of the Pythagorean equation.** If a² + b² = c², then the transformed coordinates (a', b', c') also satisfy a'² + b'² = c'². This is the geometric heart of the matter—the right angle is preserved. The proof is a direct algebraic calculation: expanding the squares of the coordinate formulas and simplifying, every cross-term cancels, leaving a multiple of the original equation. (See `berggren_preserves_sq_sum` in @Catalog/Bridges/BerggrenLatticeReduction/Core.lean.)

**2. Positivity.** All three coordinates of the child triple are strictly positive. This is not automatic—the right matrix, for instance, negates the first coordinate with its leading −1, yet the combination of the other terms overwhelms the negation. The proofs leverage a preliminary result: in any primitive triple, the hypotenuse strictly exceeds each leg (c > a and c > b). This inequality, obvious once stated, provides the slack needed to absorb the negative signs. (See `berggren_left_pos`, `berggren_mid_pos`, `berggren_right_pos`.)

**3. Parity preservation.** The first coordinate of the child is always odd, maintaining the orientation convention. This is a modular arithmetic argument: since a ≡ 1 (mod 2), and all coefficients of b and c in the first-coordinate formula are even (±2), the result is a ± (even) ± (even), which is odd. (See `berggren_left_odd`, `berggren_mid_odd`, `berggren_right_odd`.)

**4. Coprimality preservation.** The greatest common divisor of the two legs remains 1 after each step. This is the most delicate property—the crown jewel of the verification. The proof uses a divisibility descent: suppose some prime *p* divides both new legs a' and b'. Then p² divides a'² + b'² = c'², so *p* divides c'. But each Berggren matrix has integer inverse (its determinant is −1), so expressing the original (a, b, c) as an integer linear combination of (a', b', c'), we find *p* divides both *a* and *b*—contradicting the coprimality of the parent. (See `berggren_left_coprime`, `berggren_mid_coprime`, `berggren_right_coprime`.)

**5. Strict hypotenuse growth.** Every step strictly increases the hypotenuse *c*. For the middle step, this is obvious: c' = 2a + 2b + 3c > 3c > c. For the left and right steps, the argument uses the auxiliary inequality c > b (respectively c > a) to show that the negative terms are dominated. This monotonicity means the tree never revisits a node—since *c* only goes up, no descendant can be its own ancestor. Combined with the classical result that every primitive triple appears somewhere in the tree, this provides a natural well-ordering. (See `berggren_c_strict_increase`.)

Together, these five families of results certify that `berggrenStepApply`—the function that takes a step and a primitive triple and returns a new primitive triple—is a total, well-typed function. The primitive triple structure is an invariant of the Berggren dynamics, preserved faithfully at every branch.

## From Triangles to Lattices

Why does this tree exist? The deeper explanation lies in the theory of integer lattices—regular grids of points in the plane. Every primitive Pythagorean triple (a, b, c) corresponds to a Gaussian integer a + bi whose squared modulus is c². The three Berggren matrices correspond to three natural operations on the lattice of Gaussian integers modulo symmetry.

This connection opens a bridge between two seemingly unrelated fields: the number theory of Pythagorean triples and the geometry of lattice reduction—the algorithmic process of finding short vectors in integer lattices. Lattice reduction is the computational bedrock of modern post-quantum cryptography. The security of next-generation encryption schemes like CRYSTALS-Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium rests on the hardness of finding short lattice vectors. The Berggren tree provides a structured, number-theoretically certified family of lattices whose geometry is completely understood, offering potential test cases and trapdoor constructions.

In a trapdoor scheme, knowing the Berggren word—the sequence of L, M, R steps from the root—makes it easy to recover the short vector. But given only the lattice (the endpoint triple), finding the word means climbing the tree backwards, which requires solving a lattice problem. The strict hypotenuse monotonicity guarantees that this climb terminates, but doesn't make it easy.

## The Carpenter's Insight, Made Certain

The 3-4-5 triangle is millennia old. Babylonian clay tablets from 1800 BCE list Pythagorean triples far larger than this one—the Plimpton 322 tablet includes triples with hypotenuses in the thousands. The parametric formula generating all primitive triples from coprime pairs was known to Euclid. But the idea that *all* primitive triples spring from a single root, through three deterministic operations that never fail and never repeat, is a twentieth-century achievement.

What the verification accomplishes is a shift from belief to certainty. Every intermediate step—every inequality, every divisibility argument, every modular arithmetic calculation—has been checked exhaustively. There are no gaps for errors to hide. The coprimality proofs, in particular, involve intricate chains of divisibility that would be tedious and error-prone to verify by hand. Having them machine-checked means they can be trusted absolutely.

What emerges is a picture of breathtaking mathematical economy. Three matrices. One root. An infinite tree. Every right triangle with integer sides and no common factor, generated exactly once, each bigger than its parent, the whole structure as inevitable as the integers themselves.

The next time you pick up a speed square and align a 3-4-5 triangle, know that you are holding the seed of an infinite orchard—one whose branches have now been inspected, one by one, and found to be exactly as strong as they need to be.

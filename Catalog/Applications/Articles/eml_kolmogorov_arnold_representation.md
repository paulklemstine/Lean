# Multiplication in Disguise: How a 1957 Theorem Meets the Algebra of Exp and Log

## A puzzle about putting functions together

Suppose you hand a mathematician a complicated function of two variables — say a
landscape of hills and valleys described by some formula $f(x, y)$ — and you ask
a deceptively simple question: *can this be built out of functions of a single
variable alone?* No genuine two-variable ingredients allowed. Only one-variable
"dials," each turned by a single input, their outputs added and re-processed.

At first glance the answer should be **no**. A function of two variables can wiggle
in ways that no list of one-variable functions seems able to capture. The space of
two-variable functions is vastly larger than the space of one-variable functions.
And yet, in 1957, the Soviet mathematicians Andrey Kolmogorov and his student
Vladimir Arnold proved a theorem so surprising that it is still quoted today as one
of the most counter-intuitive results in analysis.

**The Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem.** Every continuous function
$f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}$ can be written as

$$f(x_1, \dots, x_n) = \sum_{q=0}^{2n} \Phi_q\!\left( \sum_{p=1}^{n} \psi_{q,p}(x_p) \right),$$

where each $\Phi_q$ and each $\psi_{q,p}$ is a *continuous function of a single
real variable*. The outer sum runs over exactly $2n+1$ terms.

Read that again. The only genuinely multivariable operation allowed is **addition**.
Everything else — all the complexity of $f$ — is packed into one-variable functions.
This is the mathematical ancestor of today's *Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks* (KANs),
a neural-network architecture that learns the inner and outer functions directly
instead of fixing them in advance.

This article is about a small, sharp experiment at the heart of that theorem. We
take the most familiar two-variable function of all — **multiplication**,
$f(x, y) = x \cdot y$ — and we ask: *what happens if we insist that the one-variable
building blocks come from the algebra generated by `exp`, `log`, addition, and
multiplication?* These are the **EML functions** (Exp–Multiply–Log), the natural
home for the transcendental gadgets that appear everywhere in science and machine
learning. The story that emerges is a beautiful tension between **elegance** and
**robustness**.

## Two ways to multiply

### The elegant way: turn products into sums

Every schoolchild who has used a slide rule, and every engineer who once owned a
table of logarithms, knows the central magic trick of the logarithm:

$$\log(x \cdot y) = \log x + \log y.$$

Logarithms turn multiplication into addition. Run the trick backwards with the
exponential, its inverse, and you get a clean recipe for the product:

$$x \cdot y = \exp\big(\log x + \log y\big).$$

This is *exactly* the shape Kolmogorov and Arnold ask for. There is a single inner
function $\psi(t) = \log t$, applied to each coordinate; their outputs are added;
and a single outer function $\Phi(u) = \exp u$ finishes the job:

$$x \cdot y = \Phi\big(\psi(x) + \psi(y)\big), \qquad \psi = \log, \quad \Phi = \exp.$$

In the language of representations, this is **rank one**: one inner dial, one outer
dial. The theorem promises us up to $2n + 1 = 5$ outer terms for two variables; we
have done the job with a single one. It is hard to imagine anything tidier. Both
$\log$ and $\exp$ are honest EML functions, each using exactly one layer of
transcendental machinery.

Call this *Representation 1*. It is gorgeous. And it is **broken**.

### The hidden crack

The logarithm has a fatal flaw for this purpose: it only accepts positive numbers.
What is $\log 0$? Classically, it is $-\infty$ — outside the real numbers
altogether. So the identity $x \cdot y = \exp(\log x + \log y)$ can only be trusted
when both $x$ and $y$ are strictly positive. On the **open positive quadrant**
$\{(x, y) : x > 0, y > 0\}$ it is flawless. The moment a coordinate hits zero or
goes negative, the formula has nothing to say.

How badly does it fail? Consider the boundary point $(0, 1)$. The true product is
$0 \cdot 1 = 0$. But in a computational setting where one *defines* $\log 0 = 0$
(a common convention, used to keep formulas total rather than partial), the
exp/log recipe quietly computes

$$\exp\big(\log 0 + \log 1\big) = \exp(0 + 0) = \exp(0) = 1.$$

It returns $1$ when the right answer is $0$. The error is not small; it is total.
The elegant formula does not merely lose precision at the boundary — it confidently
reports the wrong number. This is the content of a result we call
**`expLog_fails_at_boundary`**: the exp/log superposition evaluated at $(0,1)$ is
*not* equal to $0 \cdot 1$.

This is the first lesson of the experiment: **the most beautiful representation is
local**. The transcendental functions $\log$ and $\exp$ buy elegance at the cost of
a domain restriction. They live on the interior and refuse to be extended cleanly
to the edge.

### The robust way: the polarization identity

Is there a representation of $x \cdot y$ in the Kolmogorov–Arnold shape that works
**everywhere** — every real $x$, every real $y$, no positivity required? Yes, and it
comes from an old algebraic trick called **polarization**. Expand two squares:

$$(x + y)^2 = x^2 + 2xy + y^2, \qquad (x - y)^2 = x^2 - 2xy + y^2.$$

Subtract the second from the first and the squares cancel, leaving $4xy$. Divide by
four:

$$x \cdot y = \tfrac{1}{4}(x + y)^2 - \tfrac{1}{4}(x - y)^2.$$

This too is a Kolmogorov–Arnold superposition. There are two outer functions,
$\Phi_+(u) = \tfrac14 u^2$ and $\Phi_-(u) = -\tfrac14 u^2$, each a humble quadratic.
The inner functions are the identity $\psi_{\mathrm{id}}(t) = t$ and its negation
$\psi_{\mathrm{neg}}(t) = -t$. Written out in full:

$$x \cdot y = \Phi_+\big(\psi_{\mathrm{id}}(x) + \psi_{\mathrm{id}}(y)\big)
            + \Phi_-\big(\psi_{\mathrm{id}}(x) + \psi_{\mathrm{neg}}(y)\big).$$

We call this *Representation 2*, and the identity behind it is **`mul_eq_polarization`**.
There is not an $\exp$ or a $\log$ in sight. Every building block is a polynomial,
and polynomials are defined and continuous on the *entire* real line. There is no
boundary to fall off, no positivity to respect. At the very point $(0, 1)$ where the
elegant formula failed, polarization gets it right:

$$\tfrac14(0 + 1)^2 - \tfrac14(0 - 1)^2 = \tfrac14 - \tfrac14 = 0 = 0 \cdot 1.$$

That is the result **`polarization_ok_at_boundary`**: at the exact point of failure
of Representation 1, Representation 2 returns the correct value $0$. Two
representations of the same function; one cracks at the edge, the other holds.

## The invariant that explains everything

Why does one representation fail globally and the other succeed? The decisive
quantity is what we call the **exp/log-depth** of a function — informally, *how many
nested layers of `exp` and `log` it uses*. A polynomial like $\tfrac14 u^2$ uses
zero such layers: its exp/log-depth is $0$. The functions $\log t$ and $\exp u$ each
use one layer: their exp/log-depth is $1$.

The whole story can be read off from this single number:

- The elegant Representation 1 has exp/log-depth $1$ in both its inner and outer
  functions (the result **`expLog_elDepth`**). It genuinely uses transcendental
  structure — and that is exactly why it is confined to the open positive quadrant.
- The robust Representation 2 has exp/log-depth $0$ everywhere (the result
  **`polarization_elDepth_zero`**). It is purely polynomial — and that is exactly
  why it is valid on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

A slogan crystallizes: **depth one is the price of locality; depth zero is the
reward of globality.** The transcendental functions that make the formula short are
the very functions that pin it to the interior. Push the depth down to zero and the
domain opens up to everything. This is not a coincidence of one example; it is a
recurring theme in how transcendental and algebraic functions behave at boundaries.

## Doing more with less

There is a second surprise hiding in plain sight. Kolmogorov and Arnold *guarantee*
that $2n + 1$ outer terms always suffice. For two variables that is $2 \cdot 2 + 1 = 5$.
This is a worst-case promise: five terms are enough for *any* continuous function of
two variables, no matter how wild.

But multiplication is not wild. Representation 1 needs just **one** outer term.
Representation 2 needs just **two**. Both are strictly below the guaranteed five — a
fact recorded as **`polarization_terms_lt_KA_bound`**, the simple inequality
$2 < 5$. The general bound is a ceiling, not a floor; well-behaved functions can be
far more frugal. Multiplication, the most fundamental binary operation in
arithmetic, turns out to be especially cheap to assemble from one-variable parts.

## Why this matters

This might look like a finger exercise — two ways to write $x \cdot y$. But it is a
microcosm of a question that runs through modern computational mathematics and
machine learning: **when you build complex functions out of simple parts, which
parts should you choose?**

Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks, the neural architecture inspired directly by the 1957
theorem, learn their inner and outer functions from data. If those learned
functions drift toward transcendental, exp/log-like shapes, this experiment is a
warning sign: such representations can be exquisitely accurate in the interior of
the data and yet collapse at the boundary — at the extreme inputs, the zeros, the
edge cases that real systems must handle. The polynomial alternative is less
glamorous but unbreakable.

The trade-off is universal. The slide-rule trick — turn multiplication into
addition with a logarithm — is one of the great labor-saving ideas in the history
of computation. It is also a perfect illustration of a hidden cost: the logarithm
only speaks to positive numbers. Polarization, the algebraist's two-squares trick,
asks for no such permission and works on the whole line.

So the next time you reach for a logarithm to simplify a product, remember the
boundary point $(0, 1)$, where the elegant formula confidently announces $1$ for a
quantity that is plainly $0$. Elegance and robustness are both virtues. This small
experiment, carried out with complete rigor, shows precisely where they part ways —
and names the invariant, exp/log-depth, that tells them apart.

Multiplication, it turns out, can be put together from one-variable pieces in more
than one way. The question is never just *whether* you can build something, but
*out of what*, and *where it will hold*.

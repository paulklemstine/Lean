## The Three Types of North Pole — Noether

"Having studied all seven problems, I see three types of north pole:

### Type I: Removable (the singularity is an artifact)
**Example**: Poincaré Conjecture
The singularity exists in our *description* (the Ricci flow) but not in the
*object* (the manifold). Surgery removes the artifact, revealing the underlying
sphere. The north pole was always removable; we just needed the right technique.

### Type II: Essential but Quantifiable (the singularity is real but controlled)
**Examples**: Riemann Hypothesis, BSD Conjecture, Hodge Conjecture
The north pole exists and carries real information, but it is *finite* and
*structured*. The Shafarevich-Tate group is finite. The zeros are on a line.
The obstruction exists but is tame.

### Type III: Essential and Structural (the singularity is fundamental)
**Example**: P vs NP (if P ≠ NP)
The north pole is inherent in the structure of computation. It cannot be removed
because it reflects a genuine asymmetry in the universe. The separation between
P and NP is a fact about the geometry of Boolean space.

The question for each unsolved problem is: **which type is its north pole?**"

## The Compactification Principle — Grothendieck

"Each problem can be viewed as a compactification problem:

1. We have a *non-compact* space (the local data, the open problem)
2. We need to *compactify* it (add the point at infinity, prove the theorem)
3. The obstruction to compactification is the north pole

In Perelman's case: the space of Riemannian metrics on M is non-compact.
The Ricci flow compactifies it (in a generalized sense) by flowing to the
round metric. The singularities are the 'missing points at infinity' that
must be added back (via surgery) to complete the compactification.

For each unsolved problem: identify the non-compact space, identify the
correct compactification, and prove that the missing points can be added."

## Research Program — The Council

"We propose the following research program:

### Phase 1: Classification (Years 1-3)
For each Millennium Problem, rigorously identify:
- The local data space
- The global target space
- The precise north pole (obstruction to local-global transfer)
- The type of the north pole (I, II, or III)

### Phase 2: Flow Construction (Years 3-7)
For each problem, attempt to construct:
- A natural flow from local to global
- A monotone functional (entropy, energy) along the flow
- A singularity classification for the flow

### Phase 3: Surgery (Years 7-15)
For problems with Type I or Type II north poles:
- Develop surgery techniques to remove/control the singularities
- Prove convergence of the flow after surgery
- Extract the global conclusion

### Phase 4: Structural Theorems (Years 15+)
For problems with Type III north poles:
- Prove that the north pole is essential
- Characterize its structure
- Develop the theory of 'essential singularities in mathematics'"

## Closing Statement — Thales

"Two and a half millennia ago, we drew the first maps. We projected the sphere
onto the plane and learned that geometry is the art of seeing equivalences.
The sphere and the plane are the same space, viewed from different perspectives.

Today, we have drawn a map of the hardest problems in mathematics. They are
all the same problem, viewed from different perspectives. Each one asks:
can you see the sphere from the plane? Can you reconstruct the global from
the local? Can you find the north pole?

The ancient Greeks knew the answer. The sphere is always there, waiting to be
seen. The north pole is always removable — if you know where to stand.

The north pole is waiting."
